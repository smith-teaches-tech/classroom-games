    5Now look closer

    The Phrenology File

    Seven exhibits. Read them in order. Each one is real.

    Exhibit A

    The method

    Around 1800, the Viennese doctor Franz Joseph Gall argued that the brain is not one organ but many — one for each mental faculty, 27 in all, from language to cunning to kindness. A stronger faculty meant a bigger organ, and a bigger organ pushed the skull outward into a bump.

    To find each organ, Gall collected skulls and plaster casts of heads from people known for one strong trait, then looked for the bump they shared. His followers measured heads with calipers and recorded every reading.

    Exhibit B

    The community

    In 1820 the brothers George and Andrew Combe founded the Edinburgh Phrenological Society. By 1826 it had 120 members, about a third of them with medical training. From 1824 it published its own journal. In 1823 Andrew Combe defended phrenology in a public debate at Edinburgh's Royal Medical Society — and both sides claimed they had won.

    Exhibit C

    The test

    In the 1820s the French scientist Pierre Flourens removed small parts of animals' brains and watched what changed. Gall had said the cerebellum, at the back of the brain, was the organ of the reproductive drive. When Flourens removed it, the animals staggered and lost control of their movements — but kept their senses and their intelligence.

    Removing small pieces of the outer brain never knocked out one faculty and left the others untouched. Gall replied that Flourens's cuts were too crude and damaged many organs at once.

    Exhibit D

    The awkward cases

    Sometimes a head did not match the person. A cruel man might have a large bump for Benevolence (kindness). Phrenologists had an answer ready: other organs were overpowering his Benevolence. Any case that confirmed the system was welcomed as proof.

    Exhibit E

    Mark Twain's two readings

    Around 1872, in London, the American writer Mark Twain visited the famous phrenologist Lorenzo Fowler under a false name. Fowler found a "cavity" on his skull that, he said, showed the total absence of the sense of humor.

    Three months later Twain went back — this time under his own name, already famous as a humorist. Fowler found the loftiest bump of humor he had ever encountered.

    Exhibit F

    What the skulls were used for

    In 1839 the Philadelphia doctor Samuel George Morton published Crania Americana, with more than seventy drawings of skulls sorted by race. Over the 1830s and 1840s he collected more than 1,300 skulls, many of them from enslaved and Indigenous people. He urged his fellow scientists to use phrenology's methods when studying racial differences, and he claimed the size and shape of the skulls proved that white people were more intelligent than other races.

    Arguments like Morton's were used to defend slavery in the United States.

    Exhibit G

    The modern brain map

    In 1861 the French doctor Paul Broca studied a patient who had lost the ability to speak. After the man died, Broca found damage in one small area at the front of the brain's left side.

    In 1953 surgeons removed tissue from both sides of 27-year-old Henry Molaison's brain, including much of the hippocampus, to stop his severe seizures. The seizures stopped. But from then on he could not form new memories: minutes after someone left the room, he had no memory of them. His intelligence, his personality and his perception were unchanged, and he could still learn new physical skills without remembering that he had practised them. The scientists Brenda Milner and William Scoville studied him alongside other patients who had similar operations.

    Modern brain science keeps Gall's starting idea — different parts of the brain do different jobs — but not his list of faculties, and not the idea that you can read them from bumps on the skull.

    6Find the cut

    Two claims, one idea — where do they come apart?

    Talk with your partner first. Then write. Point to the exhibit every time.

    Open only when your teacher says ▸

    The cut is evidence that could say no. Phrenology and the brain map share an idea, a method and a community of experts. If the hippocampus had nothing to do with memory, Henry Molaison's memory would have survived the surgery. So his case could have proved the claim wrong — and it didn't. Phrenology's evidence never took that risk: every misfit was explained away, and Twain's readings show the result followed what the knower already believed.

    Morton is the same break. Racism is not what made phrenology wrong. Evidence that cannot say no will "confirm" whatever its users already believe — a humour bump for a famous humorist, or a racial ranking for people who wanted one.

    The grid is not perfect. Used as a checklist, phrenology passes: there was evidence, there was a community. The grid only works when you ask the questions hard: not "is there evidence?" but "could this evidence have proved the claim wrong?" Not "is there a community?" but "did it hunt its own mistakes?"

    Back to the KQ. Who found phrenology's break — the phrenologists, or people outside the society?

    7Whole class

    Back to astrology

    Astrology and the Neptune prediction both start from precise positions of the planets. Run the grid hard on both.

    Open only when your teacher says ▸

    Evidence. Astrology has evidence: the planetary positions are precise, and believers have many cases where "it fit me." But horoscopes are worded so that almost any week fits — the evidence can't say no. Same break as phrenology.

    Validated. It has been tested. In blind tests, astrologers matching birth charts to personality profiles did no better than chance. Tested — and failed.

    Community. Trained practitioners and a long tradition, yes. But after the tests failed, nothing changed. A community that doesn't hunt its own mistakes.

    Reach. "Do the planets shape personality?" is within science's reach — that's exactly why it could be tested and fail. String theory is different: it isn't tested-and-failed, it can't be tested at all. Two different kinds of "unscientific." We'll come back to Reach next lesson.

    8Write it

    One paragraph: find the cut

    This is the move you will use in your exhibition and your essay. Here is a model on phrenology and the brain map. Copy the moves, not the content — your paragraph is on homeopathy and vaccines.

    Model paragraph — phrenology and the modern brain map Phrenology and the modern map of the brain start from the same idea: different parts of the brain do different jobs. On method and community the two look alike — both measured, collected cases and had trained experts. The cut comes at evidence. When Henry Molaison's hippocampus was removed in 1953, he could no longer form new memories, yet his intelligence and personality were untouched. If the hippocampus had nothing to do with memory, his memory would have survived — so this was evidence that could have proved the claim wrong, and it didn't. Phrenology's evidence never took that risk. Heads that didn't fit were explained away, and Mark Twain's two readings show the result followed what the knower already believed — which is how the same method could "confirm" a racial ranking as easily as a sense of humour. What separates the two claims is not method or community, but whether the evidence could have said no.
    1. What they share. What makes homeopathy and vaccines look alike? Name the grid questions where both seem to pass.
    2. The cut. Where exactly do they come apart? Name the grid question and give the fact from the cards.
    3. What it shows. What does the cut show about where scientific knowledge gets its authority?

    One rule: don't argue whether either one "is" or "isn't" science. Show what they share and where they come apart.
