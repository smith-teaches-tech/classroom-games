Part 2: Shake the box

You couldn't open the mystery boxes, so you shook them and listened to what bounced around inside. Physicists do the same thing with a proton: they fire tiny electrons at it and watch how they bounce. First fire at a proton that is smooth and soft all the way through. Then fire at the real one.

The simulation's numbers are illustrative, not real data.

Field notes: Part 2
  • Fire at the smooth proton. What happens to the electrons?
  • Now fire at the real proton. What's different?
  • What would have to be inside a proton to make an electron bounce off at a sharp angle?
  • Did anyone see inside the proton? So how could they know what's in there?
In 1968, physicists at Stanford did this with electrons from an accelerator about 3 kilometres long. More electrons bounced off at sharp angles than a smooth proton could explain, as if they had hit tiny, hard points inside. Those points were later identified as quarks. Rutherford found the nucleus of the atom the same way in 1911. Since then, physicists have measured features of quarks, such as their electric charge, without ever pulling one out to look at it.

One way to say itWe can never directly observe a lone quark. But by firing electrons at protons, physicists can know features of what's inside: that quarks are there, and what their charge is. The limit is on seeing, not on knowing.TakeawayFrom the outside, knowers can know the features of something without ever seeing it. (The boxes showed the other half: when two things share every feature, the evidence can't tell you which one is there.)
