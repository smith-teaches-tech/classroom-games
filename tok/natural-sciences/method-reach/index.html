Where the method can't reach

You pulled a quark and could never free one. Here are three more places science reaches a wall. Pick one, find the wall, make a claim about it, then put your claim to the test.

The model paragraph — built on the quark case you already know

How to write it

Knowledge question: How do the methods of the natural sciences set the limits of what science can know?

Scientific knowledge of what is inside a proton is built without anyone ever seeing inside one. Physicists describe the proton as three quarks held together by the strong force, but no knower has ever detected a single quark on its own. Pull two quarks apart and the energy between them turns into a new pair of quarks, so every attempt produces more particles, never a lone one, and a bigger collider would only make more of them. So it is tempting to claim that we can never know what is inside a proton. But in 1968, physicists at Stanford fired electrons at protons and found that some bounced back sharply, as if they had hit tiny, hard points inside. Rutherford discovered the nucleus of the atom the same way in 1911: no one saw it, but the way particles bounced off it showed it was there. Most knowledge in physics is built like this. No one has seen an electron either; physicists see the tracks it leaves. The first claim mixed up seeing and knowing, so it gets smaller: what is out of reach is direct observation of a lone quark, not knowledge of quarks. Indirect evidence can strongly justify a claim, but it is always an inference, so a knower's certainty can only be as strong as the reasoning that links the evidence to the thing.
Claim before the testWe can never know what is inside a proton, because we can never see a quark.
Claim after the testWe can never directly observe a lone quark, but indirect evidence can still justify knowing quarks are there. The limit is on seeing, not on knowing.

Why this works: it doesn't stop at the facts, and it doesn't stop at its first claim.

  • A claim on top (shaded), about how the knowledge is built, not a fact about the proton.
  • Evidence, kept short: why no one can pull a quark free. Three sentences, no physics lecture.
  • The test (pink): it starts from a detail that doesn't fit the first claim (the 1968 experiment, Rutherford's nucleus, the electron's tracks). Without it, the paragraph is support, not analysis.
  • The rewritten claim (blue): smaller and more accurate. It names the cut (seeing vs knowing, direct vs indirect) and says what that means for how much certainty a knower should have.

Your case will have a different wall and a different test. Your job is to find both, then write a paragraph with the same shape.

The edge moved

Until 2015, no one had recorded anything from two black holes colliding. That year the LIGO detectors picked up the ripples in space from two black holes merging. In 2019 the Event Horizon Telescope took the first picture of a black hole's shadow, in the galaxy M87.

New technology moved something. What did it move, and what didn't it move?

What the “never” rests on

The event horizon is a prediction of Einstein's theory of gravity, general relativity. The theory has passed every test near black holes so far, including the two above. But the same theory says that at the very centre, matter is crushed to infinite density. Most physicists take that as a sign the theory stops working there.

Your claim rests on a theory. Which part of that theory has been tested, and which part do physicists themselves doubt?

A knower who goes in

According to general relativity, an astronaut falling into a very large black hole would cross the horizon without feeling anything special at that moment. For a while, they could look around inside. They could never send a message back out.

Is the inside out of reach for every knower, or only out of reach for shared knowledge that others can check? Does that difference matter?

Something does come out

In 1974 Stephen Hawking calculated that black holes should slowly give off radiation and, over enormous times, evaporate. No one has detected this radiation from a real black hole yet. Physicists still argue about whether it carries scrambled information about what fell in. For a black hole the mass of the Sun, evaporating would take about 1067 years, far longer than the universe has existed.

If the information does come out, is the inside “never” knowable, or “not for an impossibly long time”? Does that difference matter for knowledge?

    Case 2

    The Earth's core

    How anyone sees inside the Earth: no one has ever been there. The deepest borehole reaches about 12 km; the core begins about 2,900 km down. So seismologists use earthquakes. Earthquake waves speed up, slow down and bend as they pass through different materials, so the time a wave takes to reach a sensor carries information about everything it passed through.

    What is well supported: a rocky mantle, a liquid outer core, and a solid inner core at the centre.

    What is still argued: the very centre. Seismologists have proposed two interiors, and both have to match the same earthquake records.

    More of the same did settle some things

    Earthquake waves alone found the core. In 1906 Richard Oldham noticed that waves passing deep through the Earth arrived late, and argued for a core. Beno Gutenberg placed its edge about 2,900 km down. One kind of wave, the S-wave, cannot travel through liquid, and S-waves never come out the far side of the core, so the outer core is liquid. In 1936 Inge Lehmann found faint waves arriving where none should be, and argued there was a separate inner core.

    The tool said more detectors never break the tie. Which questions about the core did more earthquake data settle?

    What is still argued

    Interiors A and B stand for a real argument. Two seismologists proposed a separate innermost core in 2002. In 2023 a team in Australia reported new evidence for it, using waves that bounced back and forth through the whole Earth up to five times. Other seismologists are not yet convinced. Part of the problem is placement: waves that cross the very centre come out on the far side of the planet from the quake, most earthquakes happen in a few belts, and most sensors are on land, so few sensors sit in the right places.

    Is this a “never” or a “not yet”? What would it take to settle it: more of the same data, sensors in new places, or a new method?

    A different kind of evidence

    Some evidence reaches the core without earthquake waves. The Earth as a whole is about twice as dense as surface rock, so something very dense must be deep inside. Earth's magnetic field needs a layer of moving liquid metal. Lab machines can squeeze iron to the pressures of the core and measure how it behaves. And in 2018, physicists used neutrinos (tiny particles that pass straight through the planet), caught by the IceCube detector in Antarctica, to measure the Earth's density and mass without a single earthquake wave.

    Is this more of the same data, or a different kind? Can a different kind of evidence break a tie that earthquake waves can't?

    About this tool

    In this tool, the two interiors give exactly the same readings everywhere except the far side, and even there the tool can only flag the problem, not settle it. Someone built it that way, to stand for the real argument.

    Who decided the readings would match: the Earth, or the person who built the tool? What can a simulation like this show you about the real core, and what can't it show you?

      How far the lab has got

      Labs have made some of life's building blocks from simple chemicals: amino acids in 1952, and some of the building blocks of RNA in 2009. They have joined building blocks into short chains. Scientists have also made RNA molecules that can copy parts of other RNA, but they designed and selected those molecules themselves. No experiment has gone all the way from simple chemicals to anything alive.

      What exactly has the lab shown is possible: the whole path, or some steps? Does your claim say which?

      Traces that survived

      The event left no recording, but it left some traces. Tiny crystals called zircons suggest there was liquid water on Earth about 4.4 billion years ago. Layered rocks built by microbes (stromatolites) are about 3.5 billion years old. By comparing the genes of living things, biologists reconstruct the last common ancestor of all life; one 2024 study estimated it lived about 4.2 billion years ago, though others put it later.

      The tool said what happened “is gone.” Is all of it gone? Which questions can these traces answer, and which can't they?

      Not every pathway is equally likely

      The famous 1952 experiment assumed the early air was full of methane and ammonia. Most scientists now think the early air was mostly carbon dioxide and nitrogen, which makes far fewer building blocks, so that version became less likely. But in 2008, a team re-tested old samples from a volcano-style version of the same experiment and found many more building blocks, so eruptions might have made the right conditions nearby.

      If nobody can replay the event, can evidence still make one pathway more or less likely? Is “we can't know which one happened” the same as “we can't rank them”?

      A second example

      As far as we know, life began once, here. Space missions are searching Mars and icy moons such as Europa and Enceladus for signs of life. Finding life that began separately would give scientists a second example to compare.

      What kind of evidence would a second example be? Would it tell us how life began on Earth, or something else?
