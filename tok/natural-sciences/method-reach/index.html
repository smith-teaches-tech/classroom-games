Where the method can't reach

Science can't see everything. Each case gives you a claim about where science hits a wall. Your job: test the claim, one observation at a time, and find out whether the wall is as big as it looks.

Worked example: read this first

Start here: the proton

Knowledge question: How do the methods of the natural sciences set the limits of what science can know?

You've already tried to pull a quark out of a proton, and you couldn't. Here is a claim about knowledge, tested with the proton. Your assessment paragraph will do the same thing with another case.

The claim about knowledge
"Knowers cannot know what they can never observe directly."
Knowers cannot know what they can never observe directly. Quarks seem to prove this. No one has ever observed a single quark on its own: pull two quarks apart and the energy between them turns into new quarks, so every attempt makes more particles, never a lone one. But in 1968, physicists fired electrons at protons, and some bounced off at sharp angles, as if they had hit tiny, hard points inside. Nobody saw a quark. They saw what the quarks did to the electrons, and from effects like these they have since worked out features of quarks, such as their electric charge. So the claim is too big. Knowers can know things they can never see by studying what those things do to things they can see. This changes where the limit of science sits. The reach of science is not set by what we can observe, but by what leaves evidence we can measure: something that left no measurable effects at all would be out of reach. And because no one sees the quark itself, the evidence only justifies the claim if the reasoning from the bounce to the quark holds up, which is why physicists keep testing it.
Claim before the testKnowers cannot know what they can never observe directly.
Claim after the testKnowers can know things they can never see by studying their effects. The reach of science is set by what leaves effects we can measure, not by what we can observe.

Observation 2: What we can measure from outside

Card 2

Everything that falls into a black hole stays inside it, so all of its mass is still in there. Scientists can measure that mass from outside: the more mass inside, the faster nearby stars swing around the black hole. By timing those stars, astronomers worked out that the black hole at the centre of our galaxy holds about 4 million times the mass of the Sun. They can also measure how fast a black hole spins.

According to Einstein's theory, that is all the outside can ever show: how much mass is inside, how fast it spins, and its electric charge. Two black holes made from completely different things, one from a collapsed star and one from a cloud of gas, would look exactly the same from outside if those numbers matched. Physicists sum this up as “black holes have no hair.”

Field notes

  • Two black holes were made from completely different things, but they have the same mass, spin and charge. Could scientists tell them apart? How is this like the mystery boxes?

Observation 3: Who can't know?

Card 3

The nearest black hole we know of is more than 1,500 light-years away. Even travelling at the speed of light, a trip there would take more than 1,500 years.

According to Einstein's theory of gravity, an astronaut falling into a very large black hole would cross the event horizon without feeling anything special at that moment. For a while, she could look around inside. But nothing she sends, not even light, could ever get back out to anyone else.

Field notes
  • Could a person ever travel to a black hole? What stops us now?
  • Suppose an astronaut does reach one and falls in. What could she see that no one on Earth can see?
  • Could any information about what she saw get back out to Earth? Use what happened to your probe to explain.
  • If only the astronaut could ever know what is inside, and no one could check it, does it count as scientific knowledge? Why or why not?
  • Getting there and getting information back out are two different problems. Which one is a “not yet,” and which one is a “never”? Explain.

Your new claim

In your notes, write your first claim and your new claim side by side. What changed? Which observation changed it most? Then check your thinking.

One way to say itFrom the outside, science can measure a few features of what is inside a black hole, like how much mass is in there, but it can never know what the black hole is made of. Nothing from inside can ever get out. Getting to a black hole is a “not yet”; getting information back out is a “never.”TakeawayThe black hole shows both kinds of limit. Like the mystery boxes, we can know features of what is inside, but not which thing it is. And like the origin of life, the thing itself sends us no evidence, so we can only study what is around it.

Case 2

The Earth's core

The claim you are testing
"From the surface, we can never know what is at the centre of the Earth."

Before you start: do you agree? In your notes, write yes, no or partly, and one reason. Then click your answer.

How scientists see inside the Earth: nobody has ever been there. The deepest hole ever drilled is about 12 km deep; the core starts about 2,900 km down. So scientists use earthquakes. Earthquake waves travel right through the planet, and they speed up, slow down and bend depending on what they pass through. So the time a wave takes to reach a sensor tells you something about what's inside.

What scientists still argue about: the very centre. There are two ideas:

Interior AThe solid inner core has no separate centre.
Interior BThe solid inner core has a smaller, different centre inside it.

Observation 2: What earthquakes already found

Card 2

Earthquake waves are how scientists found the core in the first place. In 1906, Richard Oldham noticed that waves travelling deep through the Earth arrived late, as if they had passed through something different. One kind of earthquake wave can't travel through liquid, and it never comes out the far side of the core, so scientists worked out that the outer core is liquid. In 1936, Inge Lehmann spotted faint waves arriving where none should be, and worked out that there was a separate inner core inside it.

Field notes

Observation 3: Same evidence, two explanations

Card 3

Waves that pass through the very centre of the Earth come out on the opposite side from the quake. Scientists agree on what these waves show: some arrive a few seconds earlier or later, depending on which direction they travelled through the centre.

What scientists disagree about is what those few seconds mean. Some teams say the times show a separate innermost core (interior B). Other scientists say the same times can be explained another way, without a separate centre (interior A). They are looking at the same evidence and interpreting it differently.

The argument is hard to settle because very few waves cross the very centre where sensors can catch them: most earthquakes happen in a few zones, and most sensors are on land. In 2023, a team in Australia tried a new trick: they used waves that bounced back and forth through the whole Earth up to five times. They say this supports interior B. Other scientists are not convinced yet.

For the very centre, scientists agree on the measurements but disagree about what they mean, because two explanations fit the same evidence. That is a “not yet”: more waves through the centre, or a new method, could rule one explanation out.TakeawayWhen two explanations fit the same evidence, knowers can't choose between them until they find evidence that only one explanation fits. “We can't tell yet” is not the same as “we can never know.”

Observation 2: How far the lab has got

Card 2

In 1952, a student named Stanley Miller filled a sealed flask with water and the gases he thought were in the Earth's early air: methane, ammonia and hydrogen. He sent electric sparks through it to copy lightning. After about a week, the water held amino acids, the building blocks of the proteins in every living thing. The flask in this tool is based on his experiment.

Since then, labs have made some of the building blocks of RNA (in 2009) and joined building blocks into short chains. But no experiment has gone all the way from simple chemicals to something alive.

Observation 3: What the rocks left behind

Card 3

Nobody recorded how life began, but the early Earth left some clues. Tiny crystals called zircons suggest there was liquid water on Earth about 4.4 billion years ago. Layered rocks built up by microbes are about 3.5 billion years old.

Clues in very old rocks also suggest what the early air was like. Most scientists now think it was mostly carbon dioxide and nitrogen, not the methane, ammonia and hydrogen that Miller used in his flask (Card 2). When the experiment is run with carbon dioxide and nitrogen, it makes far fewer building blocks. So the rock clues made Miller's version of how life began less likely, even though nobody can go back and check.

Field notes
  • No one can replay how life began. What clues did the early Earth leave behind?
  • How did clues from old rocks make Miller's version of the story less likely?
  • What can clues like these tell us about how life began? What can't they tell us?
