The Village
of Mysteries

A settlement has been excavated. The people who lived here left no writing that anyone can read, and no living descendant remembers them.

Four objects were recovered. That is all that survives.

Your job, in groups: work out what each object was for — and then what the four of them together tell us about the people who made them.

You will not be given the answer at the end of this. Neither was anybody else.

Do not soften the line above. Devices away before the next screen — one image search ends the lesson. Groups of three, one find each. Say nothing about where any object came from until screen 10.

Find I
Find I · twelve faces · cast metal
0 — 5 cm
Material
Copper alloy. One example in solid silver.
Form
Twelve five-sided faces. A round opening cut into every face — and the openings are all different sizes, from 6 mm to 40 mm. A small ball at each of the twenty corners.
Size
Between 4 and 11 cm across. Weight ranges from 35 g to a full kilogram — no two need be alike.
Manufacture
Cast in one piece by a skilled method. Outside polished. Inside left rough and unfinished.
Condition
Almost never any sign of wear. No numbers, letters or marks of any kind on any example.
Findspots
One from a buried hoard, packed in with coins. Others from graves, a bath-house, a theatre, a riverbed.
Distribution
About 130 known. All from the northern edges of this people's territory. Not one from the homeland.
What is missing

These people wrote a great deal, and much of it survives. Not one text mentions this object. Not one picture shows it.

Find II
Find II · knobbed stone · carved
0 — 5 cm
Material
Sandstone. Others in greenstone, quartzite, serpentinite — some from stone that had to be carried a long way.
Form
A sphere with raised knobs worked out of the surface. Most have exactly six — the one shown has four. A few have as many as 160. Some knobs carry carved spirals and concentric rings.
Size
About 70 mm across. Of 387 recorded, 375 are the same size to within a millimetre or two.
Weight
Varies considerably — the stones differ in density, so identical size does not mean identical weight.
Condition
No wear, chipping or damage of the kind a tool or a weapon picks up.
Findspots
Most turned up in ploughed fields with no record of context. Five came from inside houses. One from a tomb.
Distribution
Over 425 known, densely concentrated in one small district and thinning sharply beyond it.
What is missing

No two are decorated the same way. And nothing has ever been found that these were used on, with, or inside.

Find III
Find III · pierced rod · antler
0 — 5 cm
Material
Antler. A few in ivory.
Form
A curved shaft, forked at one end, with a hole bored straight through just below the fork. Sometimes a second, smaller hole beside it.
Size
15 to 20 cm long. One unusual example carries a row of eight holes, each a different size.
Decoration
Frequently engraved with animals — horses, deer — carved with real skill.
Condition
Some show wear around the inside of the hole. Others show none at all.
Findspots
Cave floors and dwelling sites, mixed in with everyday debris.
Age
Far older than the other three finds. These were made over a span of more than twenty thousand years.
What is missing

The people who made these left paintings on cave walls — animals, hands, signs. They never painted anyone holding one of these.

Find IV
Find IV · chalk cylinder · one of three
0 — 5 cm
Material
Chalk, quarried locally. Soft enough to scratch with a fingernail.
Form
Three cylinders, found together. Concentric rings carved on the flat top. On two of them, a pair of eyes — enough to read as a face. Chevrons and lozenges around the sides.
Size
Three different sizes. Their circumferences are exact whole-number fractions of one longer measurement — in the ratio 10 : 9 : 8.
Condition
Crisp and undamaged. The material could not have survived being struck, ground, or carried loose.
Findspot
Placed in the grave of a child. One behind the head, the others by the hips. Nothing else was buried with them.
Distribution
Vanishingly rare. A fourth, similar object was found in another child's grave — and nothing else like them anywhere.
What is missing

Chalk survives. Wood, leather and cloth do not. We have no way of knowing whether these were rare — or whether thousands like them were made in materials that rotted away.

Step two

Now — who were these people?

You have four objects and nothing else. Build the civilisation.

Each group answers

What did these people value? What did they spend effort on?

What did they believe?

Were some people more important than others? How can you tell?

What were they able to do that surprised you?

For every claim you make

Point at the specific feature of a specific object that supports it.

Then say what else that same feature could mean.

A claim you cannot anchor to an object is not a finding. It is a preference.

Watch for: the word ritual. It will arrive within about ninety seconds, and it will be used for whichever object is hardest to explain. Write every use of it on the board — do not comment yet. You need the tally for screen 11.
Step three

Present, and collide

Each group presents its object and its civilisation. The class listens for one thing only:

Where do two groups disagree — and are they disagreeing about the evidence, or about what the evidence means?

On the board, in two columns

AGREED — things every group concluded.

CONTESTED — things groups read differently from the same object.

Keep this board. It is the specimen for the rest of the lesson, and the AGREED column is what stops the whole thing collapsing into "nobody can know anything" at the end.
Step four · a warning from the future

The Motel of the Mysteries

In 1979 David Macaulay published a book. In it, the year is 4022. North America was buried by a catastrophe two thousand years earlier, and everything about it has been forgotten.

An amateur archaeologist named Howard Carson falls down a shaft and finds a sealed chamber, perfectly preserved, its door still hung with an inscribed seal. He excavates it with enormous care and publishes a full catalogue of the sacred burial site he has discovered.

It is a motel room.

Carson is not stupid, and he is not careless. He measures everything. He records findspots. Every object he catalogues is given a function that fits with every other object he catalogues. His reconstruction is internally consistent and supported by the evidence.

He is also completely wrong — and there is nothing available to him, inside his own account, that could tell him so.

The turn, immediately — do not let the laugh settle: "That was funny because it happened to him. Go back to your sheet. Find the reading you gave that has exactly the same shape."
Step five · the reveal

Your four objects are real

Every fact you were given is true. The only thing invented was the village — these come from four different peoples, separated by as much as thirty thousand years and half a continent.

And here is what the archaeologists say.

Find I — the Roman dodecahedron

c. 2nd–4th century CE · north-western Roman provinces
  • A surveying or range-finding instrument
  • A knitting device for glove fingers
  • A gauge for measuring coin diameters
  • A candle holder
  • A fortune-telling or religious object
  • A metalworker's showpiece — or a child's toy
Over fifty theories have been published. None is accepted. After nearly three hundred years of argument, nobody knows what it is.

Find II — Neolithic carved stone balls

c. 3200–2500 BCE · north-east Scotland, mainly Aberdeenshire
  • Objects of divination
  • Tokens granting the right to speak in council
  • Weapons — thrown, or slung as bolas
  • Standard weights (disproved: the weights vary too much)
  • Hide-working tools, or fishing and loom weights
  • Ball bearings for moving megalithic stones
The ball-bearing theory was tested experimentally, and it works — which proves it is possible, and not that it is true. Still unresolved.

Find III — the perforated baton

c. 35,000–12,000 years ago · Upper Palaeolithic Europe
  • A spear-thrower — tested, and it increases range by 127%
  • A tool for straightening spear and arrow shafts
  • A rope-making tool (chemical traces support this)
  • A fastener, a calendar, a leather smoother
  • A fertility object
For a century these were called bâtons de commandement — "staffs of command" — because the first excavator assumed they were badges of a chieftain. That guess was in the name, and the name shaped everything written afterwards. They are now called pierced rods: a name that describes the shape and refuses to guess the use.

Find IV — the Folkton Drums

c. 2600–2000 BCE · Yorkshire, England · excavated 1889
  • Ceremonial objects placed with the dead
  • Standard measures — physical templates for laying out monuments
  • Chalk copies of objects normally made in wood
They are called drums because of their shape. Nobody has ever thought they were drums. That is the second object here named after a guess — and both names stuck.
The knowledge question

What just happened

If the past is gone, and all that survives is what it left behind — what can a historian actually know, and how would they ever find out they were wrong?

  1. Go back to the board from the second screen. How long did it take the class to form an interpretation — before any evidence had been given at all?
  2. Every group anchored its claims to real features of real objects. So why did you still disagree? What does that tell you about what evidence can and cannot settle?
  3. Count the times "ritual" or "ceremonial" was used today. Why does that word feel like an explanation? What would have to be true for it to be wrong?
  4. Carson had the objects, the measurements and the findspots. What did he not have — and could he have got it?
  5. The pierced rod carried a guess inside its own name for a hundred years. What does a name do to the people who inherit it?
  6. Your AGREED column survived every group. What made those claims different from the contested ones? The traces do constrain — so what, exactly, do they constrain?

Write, before you leave: pick one claim you made about these people. State what it rests on, and state what would have to be found for you to give it up.

Guard the landing. The ditch is "so we can never know anything about the past." Counter it with the AGREED column: these were made by people, they were worked with skill, someone did place three chalk cylinders in a child's grave. The record is thin, not empty. Underdetermined is not unknowable.

Forward: next lesson gives you the instrument for saying which of these claims is better supported — and why the Roman dodecahedron may never be settled while other questions about the past have been.
