Whose Narrative
Survives?

Every event leaves behind more than one account. Usually only one of them becomes the story: the one in the textbook, the one in the painting, the one everyone repeats.

These two classes ask how that happens. Was the account that won the one with the best evidence?

Class 1 · We do it together

The First Thanksgiving

One paragraph from 1621, and how it became a national story.

Class 2 · You do it in groups

Jerusalem, 1099

Nine accounts of one day, written across four centuries.

Arc. Thanksgiving is the distanced worked example: this cohort was not raised inside it, so it is safe to take apart, and the teacher models every move. Jerusalem 1099 is where the charge is, and the groups do the work. The C cell (who decides what is kept, named and taught) is completed at the end of class 2.

The deliverable, in both classes: how each account justifies itself and what counts as evidence for it. Never a verdict on which side was right or worse. If the room starts arguing about morality, ask the TOK question instead: how did this account come to count as the accepted one?

Class 1 · The First Thanksgiving

The only eyewitness account

Edward Winslow, one of the colonists, wrote this in a letter in December 1621. It was printed in London in 1622. It is the only account written by someone who was there.

Edward Winslow · letter of 11 December 1621 · printed in Mourt’s Relation, London, 1622

“Our harvest being gotten in, our governor sent four men on fowling, that we might after a more special manner rejoice together, after we had gathered the fruits of our labors. They four in one day killed as much fowl as, with a little help beside, served the Company almost a week. At which time, amongst other recreations, we exercised our arms, many of the Indians coming amongst us, and among the rest their greatest king Massasoit, with some 90 men, whom for three days we entertained and feasted, and they went out and killed five deer, which they brought to the plantation and bestowed on our governor, and upon the captain and others. And although it be not always so plentiful as it was at this time with us, yet by the goodness of God, we are so far from want that we often wish you partakers of our plenty.”

Spelling modernised. Text: Plimoth Patuxet Museums, plimoth.org/yath/unit-4/eye-witness-account-of-the-1621-harvest-celebration
Pairs · 4 minutes
  1. Go back to your notebook list. Which things on it are actually in this paragraph?
  2. Which word would you expect to find here, and can’t?
  3. What does “we exercised our arms” mean? Why might ninety Wampanoag men have turned up?
R
Not in the only eyewitness account

The word thanksgiving.

An invitation to the Wampanoag.

Turkey (only “fowl”).

Any thank-you, from anyone, to anyone except God.

In it

A harvest celebration lasting three days.

Colonists firing guns (“exercised our arms”).

Massasoit and about ninety men, who brought five deer.

This is the E cell with almost nothing in it: one paragraph of about 150 words. Bradford’s history mentions “wild turkeys” that autumn, but in general terms, not at the feast, and he wrote years later.

Class 1 · The First Thanksgiving

The account that isn’t on the page

The other people at the meal

The Wampanoag did not keep written records in 1621. Their history was carried by oral tradition, passed down and retold.

Wampanoag tradition says the men came because they heard the colonists’ guns, and came to see what was happening. The visit was diplomacy, between two peoples who had signed an alliance that spring. Disease had killed a large part of the coastal population in the years just before 1620, and the alliance mattered to both sides.

Smithsonian American Indian magazine: americanindianmagazine.org/story/a-closer-look-at-the-thanksgiving-tale

Pairs · then share
  1. Is oral tradition evidence? What would make you trust it more, or less?
  2. Look again at Winslow. Is there anything in his paragraph that fits the Wampanoag account?
  3. In the 1800s, when the story was being built, why did Winslow’s account count and the Wampanoag account not?
R
Winslow, again

“…amongst other recreations, we exercised our arms, many of the Indians coming amongst us…”

Two accounts that never read each other, one written and one spoken, agree that the guns came first. That is the independence test from Lesson 2.

So the Wampanoag account was not left out because it was weak evidence. It was left out because the people building the story only counted what was written down, and the Wampanoag did not write it down.

This is the screen that stops the lesson becoming “written good, oral bad” or “oral good, written bad.” Oral tradition is evidence to be tested like any other. Here it passes one test: it agrees on an independent detail with a source it cannot have copied. The L2 callback should be said out loud.

Q3 is the C cell: a rule about what counts as evidence (writing only) decided whose account could enter the record. Ask who made that rule.
Class 1 · The First Thanksgiving

1970: the speech that was read first

What happened

In 1970 Massachusetts celebrated 350 years since the Pilgrims landed. The organisers invited Wamsutta Frank James, a Wampanoag leader, to speak.

They asked to read his speech in advance. Then they told him he could not give it.

Before the reveal
  1. What reason do you think they gave?
  2. If his speech had contained false claims, what would the organisers have needed to show?
R

“The theme of the anniversary celebration is brotherhood and anything inflammatory would have been out of place.”

— an official explaining the decision, 1970

The speech included the accusation that the Pilgrims had taken goods from Wampanoag graves. James gave it instead on a hill above Plymouth harbour. Native people have gathered there every Thanksgiving since, calling it the National Day of Mourning.

“What has happened cannot be changed, but today we must work towards a more humane America, a more Indian America.”

— Wamsutta Frank James, the speech he was not allowed to give

en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Day_of_Mourning_(United_States_protest)

Was the speech rejected because its evidence was weak?

The answer is no: it was rejected for not fitting the theme. Nobody checked the claim. This is the C cell on the record: who decides which account of the past may be spoken, and on what grounds. Keep it there.

Drift guard. Do not let this become “was James right to be angry?” or a debate about colonialism. The TOK question is narrower and sharper: what was the reason for excluding this account, and was it a reason about evidence?
Class 1 · The First Thanksgiving

Now test the other story

Once people learn the painting is a myth, a second story often takes its place. You may have seen it online.

Claim on trial

“Thanksgiving really began in 1637, as a celebration of a massacre.”

Pairs · use the same questions you used on the painting
  1. What evidence would this claim need?
  2. What would you need to check before you believed it?
R
True, and documented

In 1637 English forces attacked a Pequot village at Mystic and killed around 500 men, women and children.

Governor John Winthrop recorded: “There was a day of thanksgiving kept in all the churches for the victory obtained against the Pequots.”

Not supported

That this is where the holiday came from. English colonists declared days of thanksgiving all the time, for harvests, rain and victories. Historian David Silverman: “There were hundreds, if not thousands of thanksgivings.”

The national holiday comes from 1863 and the campaign traced on screen 4.

Snopes fact-check, with Winthrop’s journal and Silverman: snopes.com/fact-check/thanksgiving-massacre-pequot-tribe

Showing that a powerful story left things out does not make the opposite story true. Both have to pass the same test.

This is the anti-relativist turn, and it must not be skipped. The massacre is real and Winthrop’s line is real. The claim fails on one step: origin. A student who leaves thinking “the official story is propaganda, so the counter-story is the truth” has learned the opposite of the lesson.

This is a live risk: the 1637 story is often retold as if the killing followed the 1621 meal. It is sixteen years later, and the Pequot are a different people.

Class 2 · Jerusalem, 1099

Before we look at anything

In your notebook · 3 minutes · on your own
  1. What happened when the Crusaders took Jerusalem in 1099? Write what you know.
  2. If you have heard a number of people killed, write it down.
  3. Where did you learn it?
What nobody disputes

In June 1099 an army from western Europe, on what we now call the First Crusade, laid siege to Jerusalem. On 15 July 1099 they broke into the city. Many of the people inside were killed.

What is disputed is how many, and what the accounts of it were doing.

Collect any numbers on the board without comment. Some students will have one, often 70,000. Some will know Maalouf or a documentary. Write down where they say they learned it; that column pays off on screen 15.

Say this early, and mean it: nothing in this lesson questions that a massacre happened. The independent evidence confirms it. The question is what a historian can know about its size, and why the numbers in the sources differ so much.

Q5 is where the charge is. Let it be uncomfortable; don’t rescue. If a group says “so the Muslim sources lied,” redirect: did the number’s job change between 1100 and 1233? What job is a number like 70,000 doing? The next screen gives a historian’s answer, and a medieval Arab historian’s.
Class 2 · Jerusalem, 1099

The numbers, in the order they were written

R
Konrad Hirschler, historian of Arabic history-writing (then at SOAS, London), 2014

He read the Arabic accounts in order. The early ones, from Syria, Egypt and Iraq, “rarely agreed on what happened in the hours and days after the fall of Jerusalem.” About a century later, one version took over: full-scale massacre, told as part of a long war between Franks and Muslims. That became the version that was copied.

The figure 70,000 was not a count. Seventy thousand is a number with a long history in Islamic writing about martyrdom and the end of days. Hirschler points to a medieval Arab historian who said the same thing six hundred years ago:

“Numbers are not all to be taken literally; the intended sense is rather that of magnitude.”

— Ibn Khaldun, North Africa, 14th century
Revelation 14:20 · the Bible Raymond’s readers knew

“…and blood came out of the winepress, even unto the horse bridles…”

Raymond was not measuring the blood either. He was telling his readers that what they had done fulfilled scripture: “a just and splendid judgment of God.”

Hirschler, “The Jerusalem Conquest of 492/1099 in the Medieval Arabic Historiography of the Crusades,” Crusades 13 (2014): soas-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/347926

Balance is structural, not a courtesy. Both traditions turned the event into a sign: one of God’s judgment, one of martyrdom and a long war. And the source criticism comes from inside both traditions: Ibn Khaldun said it about numbers in the 14th century. Source criticism is not a Western import.

Modern historians still disagree about the total. One recent historian (Conor Kostick) thinks up to 40,000 is possible, because refugees had crowded into the city; others go far lower. What they agree on is that the later figures are not literal counts. If asked “so how many?”, the honest answer is the lesson: we know there was a massacre; we cannot know the number.
Class 2 · Jerusalem, 1099

So what do we know?

Known

The city fell on 15 July 1099 and many inhabitants were killed: Muslims, and Jews in the burning synagogue.

Captives were taken and ransomed.

Why we can say this: crusader eyewitnesses, Muslim historians and the Jewish letter, three lines that did not copy each other, all agree.

Not known

How many died.

Why not: the people who were there gave no count, the one fully independent source gives no count, and every big number comes later, from writers with a reason to make it big.

Whole class
  1. Somebody says: “Both sides exaggerated, so we can’t know anything about 1099.” What is wrong with that?
  2. Rank two cards: the Jewish elders’ letter and the 15th-century chronicle. Which is stronger evidence, and why? Is your reason about which side wrote it?
The directed landing. Underdetermined is not unknowable (the L1 line). The massacre is settled by convergence of independent lines (the L2 tool); the number is not. And the grid still ranks: the Geniza letter beats Ibn Taghribirdi on when and independence, not on side. If a student answers Q2 by side, that is the relativist ditch from the other direction.

Through-line from L4: the summary overreaches. Eight defendants became “the lower classes”, one lifeboat became “any one”, and here an event becomes a number that nobody counted.
Class 2 · Jerusalem, 1099

Who named it?

None of the nine writers in your dossier called this event part of “the Crusades.” The Muslim historians called the invaders al-Franj, the Franks.

Pairs · then share
  1. If the name for an event came from one side’s history books, whose story are you inside when you use it?
  2. Maalouf calls his book a “true-life novel.” What does that tell you about what he counts as evidence, and what he is trying to do?
  3. Look at where you said you learned about 1099 at the start of class. Whose story was that?
Language-unit callback (naming) and L1 callback (the pierced rod and the “drums”, named after a guess). Here a whole war was named from one side’s histories, and the name was then translated into the other side’s language. Do not turn this into a lecture on 19th-century colonialism; one sentence of context is enough.

Contested, for your reference only: some historians (e.g. Edward Peters) argue that Muslim writers largely stopped writing about the Crusades until the 19th century. Others point to Ibn Khaldun and to a 17th-century Jerusalem poet who called his son “a second Saladin” and call the “forgetting” thesis unproven (OUPblog, 2018). The naming dates on screen are not in dispute; how much was remembered is. If a student pushes, that disagreement is itself the C cell: historians disagree about the history of memory.

Class 2 · Jerusalem, 1099
The knowledge question

When one account of the past becomes the accepted one, is that because it was the best supported, or because of who got to write it down, name it and teach it?

Exit write · notebook · about 150 words
  1. Choose the First Thanksgiving or Jerusalem 1099. Name the account that became the accepted one.
  2. Was it the best-supported account? What, besides evidence, helped it survive?
  3. Say one thing a historian can know about the event and one thing they can’t, and explain why.
  4. Use at least two of these concepts by name: evidence, power, perspective, certainty, interpretation.
C/D line. A response that argues which side was right, or which side was worse, has argued the topic and cannot get above a C. A response that explains how one account came to count as accepted, and separates what is known from what is not, has made the TOK move.

Watch for the two ditches in the exit writes: “history is written by the winners, so it’s all propaganda” (relativism), and “the official story was a lie, so the other story is true” (the 1637 error).
