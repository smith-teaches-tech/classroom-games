3Research questionsnotes in your notebook
The story you heard
Tell it the way you first heard it. Where did you hear it, and how old were you?
Where it came from
When does the popular version first appear, and who wrote, painted, filmed or taught it? How long after the event was that?
What it leaves out
What does the popular version leave out, and who would tell the story differently? How does leaving that out change the perspective a knower gets?
The job it did
Who was the popular version useful to when it spread, and why at that moment? Where does power come in?
How you know
Take the most important fact in your correction. How does your source know it? Follow the evidence back as far as you can.
The wall
What about this event can nobody know? Could new evidence ever reach it, or never? How much certainty is possible?
The push-back
Find the detail in your story that does not fit your claim. Maybe the popular version is partly true. Maybe the correction also had a job to do. Maybe the people with power did not win. That detail is your test.

No paragraphs on slides: images, short quotes, dates and your claim only. Core concepts: you must use evidence and power, plus at least one of perspective, interpretation or certainty, and the word knower. Concepts must explain something, not decorate a sentence. Sources: at least three; at least one must be a primary source (a document, letter, record or object from the time) or the work of a museum, university or historian. Be ready to say how your source knows your most important fact.

Warning · two ways to get a C

The debunk. “Everyone thinks X. Actually it was Y. So the story is wrong.” That is a history fact-check, not TOK. However good the research is, a presentation that stops there cannot score above C.

The verdict. Arguing which side was right, wrong, good or bad. TOK asks how an account came to be accepted and what counts as evidence for it, not who deserves blame. A presentation built on a verdict cannot score above C.

The question is never just “was the story true?” It is “what does this story show about how knowledge of the past is made, kept and lost?”

Whose Story Survived? · What it sounds likeone story, three presentations

All three use the same research we did in class on the First Thanksgiving. Only one is TOK.

Presentation 1 · the debunkC

“Most people believe the Pilgrims invited the Indians to a feast to say thank you. Actually, Winslow’s letter never mentions an invitation, nobody called it ‘Thanksgiving’ until 1841, and the famous painting has log cabins that didn’t exist yet. So the story is a myth.”

Why it’s a C: every fact is right and the research is good. But there is no claim about knowledge, nothing is tested, and it stops at “myth”.

Presentation 2 · the verdictC

“The Thanksgiving story hides that the Pilgrims robbed graves and stole corn. They were thieves, and the holiday is a lie.”

Why it’s a C: it judges people instead of asking how an account came to be accepted. It also goes past the evidence: the colonists’ own book says they planned to pay for the corn, and their governor wrote that they later did.

Presentation 3 · TOKon track for an A
1 · Claim

An account of the past becomes the accepted one less because it has the best evidence than because of who names it and teaches it, at least when the people teaching it need a shared story about where they came from.

Slide 1: the claim, next to Brownscombe’s 1914 painting.

2 · The case

The “First Thanksgiving” is my example. The only eyewitness record is Edward Winslow’s 1621 letter: a three-day harvest celebration, about ninety Wampanoag men, five deer. It never uses the word “thanksgiving” and never mentions an invitation. The name came in 1841, in an editor’s footnote. In 1865 a magazine editor linked the meal to the new national holiday, and by 1870 schools were teaching it. None of those steps added evidence; each one gave a growing country a shared, gentle origin. And the same 1622 book holds a passage that never made it into the story: the colonists taking corn and goods from graves on Cape Cod. Their governor, Bradford, later wrote that this corn was their seed for the harvest. Leaving it out changes the perspective a knower gets: a story of friendship instead of a story of survival that cost someone else.

Slide 2: 1621 Winslow’s letter · 1841 the name · 1865 the editorial · 1870 the textbooks, and the Cape Cod passage beside it.

3 · The test

But my claim doesn’t fully hold. The accepted story isn’t invented: Winslow’s letter really does support the harvest, the three days and the shared meal. Power didn’t create the evidence; it chose which true passage to keep. And power didn’t get the last word. In 1970 Wamsutta James was stopped from giving a speech built on the colonists’ own book. He gave it anyway, and by 1998 the town of Plymouth had put up a plaque marking the Day of Mourning. The evidence was there all along; what changed was who was allowed to use it.

Slide 3: one line from Winslow’s letter · 1970 the refused speech · 1998 the plaque.

4 · Verdict

So power decides which evidence gets told far more than it decides what the evidence says. Knowers can be fairly certain about the harvest itself, but not about why ninety men came, and no story, official or not, can reach that.

Slide 4: the verdict in one line, with sources at the bottom.

What to notice in Presentation 3
  • The claim answers the knowledge question and says when (“at least when…”).
  • The story is evidence for the claim, not a retelling: where it came from, what was left out, the job it did.
  • The test comes from inside the same story and makes the claim smaller and sharper.
  • The verdict says where the line falls: power controls what is told more than what is true.
  • Concepts used: evidence, power, perspective, certainty, knower.
