Repetition
or Convergence?

Four sources say Richard III had his nephews killed. Five say Julius Caesar crossed the Rubicon.

Does saying something more times make it more likely to be true?

When two scientists in different labs, using different equipment, get the same result, their agreement is strong evidence. This is called convergence.

But agreement only counts if the sources could have disagreed. If three writers all copied the same earlier writer, that isn't three sources agreeing. It's one source, repeated. This is called repetition.

Scientists can usually see that their labs are separate. Historians have to check, because writers copy each other, work for the same people, and use older books that are now lost.

Your job: in each case, work out whether the sources really agree, or whether it's one voice repeated.

Students must finish Caesar before the Princes. Caesar teaches the test on a claim that passes it; the Princes uses it on a claim that doesn't. Do not preview the Pollio result. Plutarch and Appian collapsing into one line is the moment the lesson turns.
Case 1 · Step 1 of 2 · Trace the stories

Caesar at the Rubicon

The claim: “Caesar crossed the Rubicon with his army, and that started the civil war.”

What happened

In 49 BC Julius Caesar was governor of Gaul and commanded an army that was loyal to him. The Senate in Rome ordered him to give up his army and come home alone. The Rubicon was a small river at the edge of his province. Crossing it with soldiers meant going to war against his own government. A civil war followed, and the Roman Republic did not survive it.

3

Look at what did not save this claim. Three ancient writers agreeing looked like three sources. But two of them got their story from Pollio, so the three count once. If the writers were all we had, the claim would be much weaker than it looks.

What really holds it up is the evidence that couldn't have read Pollio: coins made at the time, inscriptions, the careers of the men who fought, and a war that definitely happened.

Agreement only counts when the sources could have disagreed. Coins and inscriptions can't be paid, persuaded or copied.

Say this before moving on: "You now have a test. Use it on a case where the answer isn't comfortable."
Case 2 · Step 1 of 2 · Trace the stories

The Princes in the Tower

The claim: “Richard III had the two princes killed.”

What happened

King Edward IV died in April 1483 and left two sons, aged twelve and nine. His brother Richard was put in charge of the boys and kept them in the Tower of London. Within weeks Richard made himself king. The boys were seen less and less, and then not at all. No body, no confession and no written order has ever been found. Two years later Richard was killed in battle by Henry Tudor, who became King Henry VII.

Mancini could have disagreed: he was in London in 1483 and had no Tudor patron. But he says he couldn't find out what happened to the boys. So my claim was too simple. New claim: agreement counts when the sources could have disagreed and each one actually knows something about what happened.

Whether the bones are the princes: not yet, because DNA tests could answer it if they were allowed. What Richard said about the boys in private: never, because nobody wrote it down.
