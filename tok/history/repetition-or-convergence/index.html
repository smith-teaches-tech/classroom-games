Repetition
or Convergence?

Four sources say Richard III murdered his nephews. Five say Caesar crossed the Rubicon.

Does saying a thing more times make it more likely to be true?

In science, when separate labs using separate methods reach the same result, that agreement is worth something. It is called convergence, and it is strong evidence.

But convergence only counts if the lines are independent — if they could genuinely have disagreed. Agreement that all flows from one origin isn't convergence at all. It is repetition.

Science mostly gets independence for free. Different labs, different instruments. A historian has to prove it — because sources copy each other, share patrons, and descend from originals that no longer exist.

Your job: find out whether the agreement in each case is real convergence, or one voice repeated.

Students must pass Caesar before the Princes unlocks. Caesar teaches the tool on a case that survives the test; the Princes applies it to one that doesn't. Do not preview the Pollio result — the collapse of Plutarch+Appian into one line is the moment the lesson turns.
Case 1 · Step 1 of 2

Caesar at the Rubicon

Claim on trial: “Caesar crossed the Rubicon with his army, beginning the civil war.”

What happened

In 49 BC Julius Caesar was governor of Gaul, commanding an army loyal to him. The Senate ordered him to give up that command and come back to Rome alone. The Rubicon was the little river marking the edge of his province — to cross it with soldiers was, in law, to make war on his own state. A civil war followed, and the Roman Republic did not survive it.

Case 1 · Result

Caesar survives the test

3
Independent lines
and one of them has no author to influence

Notice what did not save this claim. Three ancient historians agreeing looked like three lines; two of them turned out to be Pollio, twice. If the narrative tradition were all we had, the case would be far weaker than it looks.

What holds it up is the line that could not possibly have read Pollio: coins struck at the time, inscriptions, the recorded careers of men who fought — and a war that unquestionably happened.

Real convergence needs lines that could have disagreed. Physical evidence can't be persuaded, bribed, or copied.

Case 2 · Step 1 of 2

The Princes in the Tower

Claim on trial: “Richard III had the two princes killed.”

What happened

Edward IV died in April 1483, leaving two sons aged twelve and nine. His brother Richard was made Protector, took charge of the boys, and lodged them in the Tower of London. Within weeks Richard was king. The boys were seen less and less often, and then not at all. No body, no confession and no order has ever been produced. Two years later Richard was killed in battle by Henry Tudor, who became Henry VII.
