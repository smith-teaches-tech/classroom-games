When Witnesses Disagree

  8. Close. The grid, and the last question.

You are the inquiry

In 1912 two official inquiries tried to establish what had happened to the Titanic. They had no wreck, no photographs, no recordings. They had people talking.

You will do what they did, with the same material, in the same order. For each question you decide which evidence you rely on and which you set aside — and every time you set something aside you must say why.

You are not marked on your verdicts. You are marked on your rule.

One instruction that matters

Every card carries its provenance: who said it, where they were standing, when they said it, who they said it to, whether they were under oath. Read that block before you read the words. It is the historian's habit, and it is the whole skill.

Dossier A · The first reports

It is 15 April 1912. These four papers, in four cities, went to press the same day. How much should four newspapers agreeing raise your confidence?

Commit before you turn the page

Every one of them is wrong. Nobody was saved by the Parisian. She was not towed to Halifax. She was already on the seabed with about 1,500 people.

9:02am
White Star Line — "There is no danger that Titanic will sink. The boat is unsinkable, and nothing but inconvenience will be suffered by the passengers."
11:25am
P. A. S. Franklin, White Star vice-president — "We can not state too strongly our belief that the ship is unsinkable and passengers perfectly safe…"
3:15pm
Laffan News Bureau — "At 2 o'clock the Titanic, having transferred her passengers to the Parisian and Carpathia, was being towed to Halifax by the Virginian."
↓
Franklin had no information from the ship. He was asserting, not reporting. The company that owned the Titanic was the principal source for the news about the Titanic.
↓
No survivor reached a reporter until Carpathia docked on the evening of 18 April. Everything on the 15th and 16th came from one Marconi relay at Cape Race and from White Star. Four mastheads. One source.

VWrite this down now

What would you need to know about two sources before their agreement counts for anything at all?

Dossier B · Did she break in two?

Some survivors said the ship snapped in half before she sank. Others said that was impossible. You decide.

Mark every card. Set aside → choose a reason. Your reasons are what is being recorded.

Your finding

In one sentence — what is your finding resting on?

The finding of the Court

You have delivered your finding. So did they.

You have been overruled
In the matter of the formal investigation into the loss of the S.S. Titanic
Report of the Wreck Commissioner
The Right Honourable Lord Mersey · London · 30 July 1912
“The ship did not break in two;
and she did eventually attain the perpendicular.”
Report, “Description of the Damage to the Ship and its Gradual Final Effect”
And in Washington, two months earlier:
“The preponderance of evidence is to the effect that she assumed an almost end-on position and sank intact.”
Report of the United States Senate Committee on Commerce, 28 May 1912

That finding stood as the official account of how the Titanic sank for the next seventy-three years.

↓
The British report's account of the sinking is, almost line for line, Charles Lightoller's testimony. It cites his answer at Q14075 for the finding. One witness's answer became the official conclusion.
↓
In fact fourteen witnesses testified under oath that she broke — seamen, greasers, a lookout, stewards, a first-class woman. You saw some of them. The Court had all of them.
↓
The assessors made no effort to investigate the question. Most of those fourteen were never asked about it. Several who volunteered it had the subject changed.

CNow the real work. Answer all four.

1. Who did the Court believe? Name them, and say what it was about them that made them believable.

2. Who did it not believe? What did they have in common?

3. State the Court's rule in one sentence — the principle it must have been using to weigh evidence.

4. Now state your rule, in one sentence. Then say honestly: how different is it?

RBefore you go on

Could any historian in 1912 — careful, fair, unlimited time — have settled this question for certain? If yes, how? If no, what exactly would "certain" have required?

Do not turn the page until you have written an answer. It stops counting once you know.

1 September 1985, just after 1:00 a.m.

Robert Ballard of Woods Hole and Jean-Louis Michel of IFREMER, aboard the research vessel Knorr, are towing a camera sled called Argo two miles down. A boiler appears on the video feed.

The wreck is in two pieces.

VThe question this whole lesson exists for

Was your rule bad — or were you unlucky? The Court had your reasons available and used them. Rank, position, corroboration, distance in time. Those are not stupid criteria. They are the criteria.

RAnd what actually moved the wall

Compare your answer on the last page to what happened. The question was not settled by a cleverer historian, a better argument, or a re-reading of the transcripts. Name the thing that settled it — and say what that implies about the questions we cannot settle now.

Dossier C · The shots

Same instrument. Same night. But read the three questions before you read the cards — they are not the same question, and they do not have the same answer.

Three findings, not one

1. Were shots fired at all?

2. Were shots fired at people — was anyone hit?

3. Did an officer shoot himself?

Q1 · YES
Shots were certainly fired. Not because witnesses say so — because the man who fired them said so, under oath. Lowe: "I fired horizontally… I think I fired three times." Scarrott, in the same boat, confirms shots, and disagrees about how many and in what direction. This question is settled.
Q2 · ?
Cannot be determined. Watch Daly's account move. Aboard Carpathia: "I saw two dagos shot." Three weeks later in print: "I saw the officer shoot two men dead." Under oath in 1915: he heard two shots and was told the men had been shot. Same man, same night, three tellings.
Q3 · ?
Cannot be determined. Rheims wrote to his wife on 19 April describing an officer shooting a passenger and then himself. Under oath in November 1913 he said only: "I heard two pistol shots… about 40 minutes before she sank." The suicide is in the private letter and nowhere in the sworn record. A modern survey of every account: "There is no concrete evidence to confirm that any officer committed suicide on the Titanic."
↓
Neither inquiry adjudicated Q2 or Q3. No physical trace of a pistol shot on an open deck could survive a sinking, and the wreck cannot help. There is no Argo for this one.

EWhy you were asked three questions

One body of evidence. Three questions. Three different levels of certainty. What does that tell you about people who say "we can't really know what happened on the Titanic"?

RTwo kinds of wall

The break-up was unanswerable in 1912 and answerable in 1985. Questions 2 and 3 were unanswerable in 1912 and are still unanswerable. The difference is not that they are harder. What is it?

Dossier D · Panic, or order?

Last one. Same instrument.

Your finding

There was panic. And there was order. Both are well evidenced, and almost none of these witnesses is contradicting any other one.

↓
Gracie was on the boat deck early. Beesley was in lifeboat 13 and away from the ship before things deteriorated. Buckley was behind a gate on E deck. Different places, different times, on a ship that took two hours forty minutes to sink.
↓
And Dodge — first class — settles it from inside the "order" camp: "Although there was no panic when the ship first struck, there was afterward." The neat class story cracks, and so does the neat contradiction.
The error
Beesley wrote "no signs of alarm were exhibited by any one." That is true of lifeboat 13 at 12:40 a.m. and false as a description of the night. The witnesses did not contradict each other. The summary did. A claim about one place and one moment got quietly promoted into a claim about everything.
↓
You have seen this exact move twice already today. The Court: fourteen accounts become "the preponderance of evidence." And in our last lesson: eight defendants become "the lower classes." Same error, three times.
1912
Lord Mersey found "there appears to have been no truth in these suggestions… They were not unfairly treated." That rests partly on counsel for the third-class families conceding no evidence had been produced — on a failure to produce evidence, not on evidence of fair treatment.

EThree questions

1. Read Abelseth again. "This gate was shut," and then "no gates or doors locked… not that I could see." Is he contradicting himself? What are those four words doing?

2. Rewrite "Was there panic on the Titanic?" as a question this evidence can answer.

3. This dispute was straightforward once you fixed the question. The break-up needed a submersible. Why the difference?

What you actually did

If the past cannot be observed or repeated, what gives historical knowledge its authority — and what can it never reach?
EEvidence
Testimony was excellent evidence for "was there panic where I stood" and useless for "did the hull fail." Same witnesses, same honesty, different question. What does that do to the word "reliable"?
VValidation
Four newspapers agreeing was worth nothing. Fourteen sworn witnesses agreeing was worth a great deal — and was ignored. What separates the two cases?
CCommunity
Lightoller was believed because of his rank. Most of the fourteen were never asked. Who decides who counts — and by what procedure?
RReach
The break-up was not yet in 1912 and known in 1985. The shots are never. What moved the first wall, and why can nothing move the second?

While it is happening, nobody knows

On 15 April 1912 the loudest voice in the record belonged to the company that owned the ship. White Star was not reporting; it was asserting — and it went on asserting after it knew. Everything printed that day was wrong.

In a war, both sides have reasons to lie about the dead — to demoralise the enemy and to steady their own people. Casualty figures are released as weapons. Historians spend decades afterwards unpicking them.

So: if interested parties dominate the record while an event is happening, what has to happen before anyone can know what occurred? And why is "why did they say it" often easier to answer than "what happened"?

The last one

The 1912 inquiries got it wrong. Were they bad historians? Answer carefully — then say what your answer implies about the history you are being taught right now.
