When Witnesses Disagree

Knowledge questionTo what extent does what counts as historical knowledge depend on who is asked, and who does the asking?

You are the inquiry

In 1912 two official inquiries, one in Washington and one in London, tried to establish what had happened to the Titanic. They had no wreck, no photographs, no recordings. They had people talking.

You will do their job, with real testimony. For each question you decide which evidence you rely on and which you set aside, and every time you set something aside you must say why.

One difference matters, and it is part of today's question: you will hear from witnesses the London inquiry never asked, and from a few people who spoke after both inquiries had finished. Watch the When line on every card.

You are not marked on your verdicts. You are marked on your rule.

Provenance: read this before anything else

Provenance is where a piece of evidence comes from. Before you read what a witness said, read where it came from. Every card in this lesson gives you five things:

WhoTheir name, and their job or place on the ship. WhereWhere they were standing when it happened. What could they actually see from there? WhenWhen they said it. The next day? A month later? Seventy-eight years later? To whomWho they were telling. A court, a reporter who paid for the story, a husband or wife in a private letter? OathWhether they had sworn to tell the truth.

Each card also ends with a check it link, so you can read the words in the original record yourself.

↓
Provenance can't settle whether a claim is true, or whether someone is lying. But it is evidence about both. It tells you what a knower was in a position to know, how much weight a claim can carry, and what you should check next. Keep that in mind every time you set a card aside.

How much should four newspapers agreeing raise your confidence?

Every one of them is wrong about what mattered most. Some got part of it right: the Titanic had hit an iceberg, and some passengers were rescued. But nobody was saved by the Parisian. The Titanic was not towed to Halifax. She was already on the seabed, and about 1,500 people had died.

11:25am

3:15pm

Franklin had no information from the ship. He was asserting, not reporting. The company that owned the Titanic was a main source for the news about the Titanic.
↓
No survivor reached a reporter until Carpathia docked on the evening of 18 April. Until then, every paper drew on a handful of wire services and on White Star itself. Four mastheads, four cities, and not one account from anyone who had seen the sinking.

The finding of the Court

This page feedsCWhat is the difference between a claim being officially accepted and a claim being justified?

You have delivered your finding. So did they.

The Court has decided
In the matter of the formal investigation into the loss of the S.S. Titanic
Report of the Wreck Commissioner
The Right Honourable Lord Mersey · London · 30 July 1912
“The ship did not break in two;
and she did eventually attain the perpendicular.”
“Attain the perpendicular” means she ended up standing straight up on end.
Report, “Description of the Damage to the Ship and its Gradual Final Effect” · check it
And in Washington, two months earlier:
“The preponderance of evidence is to the effect that she assumed an almost end-on position and sank intact.”
Report of the United States Senate Committee on Commerce, 28 May 1912 · “Preponderance of evidence” means most of the evidence.

That finding stood as the official account of how the Titanic sank for the next seventy-three years.

London
To back up this finding, the report points to one question, asked of one witness: Second Officer Charles Lightoller. It gives no other reason. That question, and what came after it, is in the exhibit below.
Washington
Nine witnesses told the Senate committee, under oath, that the ship broke. The committee still called the "preponderance of evidence" intact. It does not say how it weighed them.
Both
Between them, the two inquiries heard at least fourteen sworn witnesses say the ship broke: seamen, greasers, a lookout, stewards, two first-class women. London heard five of them.
Not asked
Researchers who have gone through the London transcript (Bill Wormstedt and others) found that most witnesses were never asked whether the ship broke, and some who raised it had the subject changed.
Exhibit · The question the finding rests on · London inquiry, 20 May 1912 · Lightoller under questioning
Solicitor-General: …what did happen to the ship? Can you tell us at all?
Lightoller: Are you referring to the reports of the ship breaking in two?
Solicitor-General: Yes?
Lightoller: It is utterly untrue. The ship did not and could not have broken in two.
↑ This is the question the report points to. The whole question is one word, “Yes?”, and it is about a subject Lightoller raised himself.
· · · a little later in the same questioning · · ·
Solicitor-General: Your Lordship knows a lot of Witnesses have said their impression was the afterpart settled on the water.
Commissioner: I have heard that over and over again. (To the witness.) That you say is not true?
Lightoller: That is not true, My Lord. I was watching her keenly the whole time.
Commissioner: I had a difficulty in realising how it could possibly be that the afterpart of the ship righted itself for a moment.
Solicitor-General: Your Lordship may remember, perhaps, that the baker, who was on the ship at this moment we are now dealing with, and was climbing aft, said he heard the rending of metal – of metal breaking.
Solicitor-General: …Your evidence is that the ship remained stiff?
Lightoller: Yes.
Who is who. The Solicitor-General was one of the Board of Trade's lawyers at the inquiry. The Board of Trade had approved the Titanic as fit to sail. The Commissioner, Lord Mersey, was the judge running the inquiry. Words. "The afterpart" is the stern, the back half of the ship. "Settled on the water" means the stern dropped back down after rising. "Remained stiff" means stayed rigid, in one piece. · Check it: first part · second part

CJustified, or accepted?

The sworn testimony from Washington was printed and published in 1912. Anyone could read it. Your group worked from the same kind of testimony in a single lesson. Yet "intact" stayed the official finding until 1985.

If there were good reasons to think the ship broke in 1912, why did "intact" stay official for seventy-three years?

Dossier C · The shots

This dossier feedsECan the same testimony be strong evidence for one question and useless for another?

Same kind of evidence. Same night. Read the three questions in the box below before you read the cards. They are not the same question, and they may not have the same answer.

Three findings, not one

1. Were shots fired at all?

2. Were shots fired at people? Was anyone hit?

3. Did an officer shoot himself?

Why are your three answers different, or why are they the same?

1 · YES
Shots were certainly fired. Not because people nearby say so, but because the man who fired them admitted it under oath. Lowe: "I fired horizontally… I think I fired three times." Scarrott, in the same boat, confirms shots, and disagrees about how many and in what direction. This question is settled.Notice: in Dossier B, one reason for setting a card aside was "Had a reason to say this." Lowe had a reason to leave this out, and didn't. A reason can make a witness less believable, or more.
2 · ?
It depends which shots. For Lowe's shots, two sworn witnesses in the same boat say he fired between the boat and the ship's side, or into the water, so for those the answer is close to no. For the shooting Daly describes, it cannot be determined. Watch Daly's account move, and watch who he is telling. Aboard Carpathia, to a doctor: "I saw two dagos shot." Three weeks later, in a newspaper: "I saw the officer shoot two men dead." Under oath in 1915: he heard two shots and was told the men had been shot. Same man, same night, three tellings.
3 · ?
Cannot be determined. Rheims wrote to his wife on 19 April describing an officer shooting a passenger and then himself. Under oath in November 1913 he said only: "I heard two pistol shots… about 40 minutes before she sank." The suicide is in the private letter and nowhere in the sworn record. A modern survey of every account: "There is no concrete evidence to confirm that any officer committed suicide on the Titanic."
↓
Neither inquiry settled questions 2 and 3. A pistol shot on an open deck leaves no trace that could survive a sinking, so the wreck cannot help. There is no Argo for this one. But a wreck is not the only kind of new evidence. Rheims's private letter is exactly the kind of document that can still turn up in an attic or an archive. So: never by the wreck, perhaps not yet by paper.

EWhy you were asked three questions

One body of evidence. Three questions. Three different levels of certainty. What does that tell you about someone who says "we can't really know what happened on the Titanic"?

RTwo kinds of wall

The break-up could not be settled in 1912 and was settled in 1985. Questions 2 and 3 could not be settled in 1912 and still cannot. Is that because they are harder, or because of the kind of evidence each question needs? Could any kind of evidence still settle them?

Dossier D · Panic, or order?

This dossier feedsECan the same testimony be strong evidence for one question and useless for another?

Last one. Same kind of evidence. Read the Where line on every card carefully.

Your finding

What is your finding resting on?

There was panic. And there was order. Both are well evidenced, and almost none of these witnesses is contradicting any other one.

↓
Gracie was on the boat deck early, loading boats. Beesley left in lifeboat 13, at least forty minutes before the end. Buckley was below decks, behind a gate. Different places, different times, on a ship that took two hours and forty minutes to sink.
↓
And Dodge travelled first class, like most of the "no panic" witnesses. He says: "Although there was no panic when the ship first struck, there was afterward." The neat class story cracks, and so does the neat contradiction.
The error
Beesley wrote "no signs of alarm were exhibited by any one." That may be true of what he saw before lifeboat 13 was lowered. As a description of the whole night, it is false. The witnesses did not contradict each other. The summary did. A claim about one place and one moment got quietly turned into a claim about everything.
↓
You have seen this move before. Earlier in this unit, in the Spenser St. John reading, eight defendants became "the lower classes." Here, the view from one part of the ship became "any one." Same error, twice.
London
Lord Mersey found that third-class passengers "were not unfairly treated." The London inquiry called three passengers to give evidence, and all three travelled first class. No third-class passenger was asked anything. The lawyer for the families of third-class passengers who died then said that "no evidence has been given" that anyone held them back.

2. Rewrite "Was there panic on the Titanic?" as a question this evidence can answer.

Back to the question, together

To what extent does what counts as historical knowledge depend on who is asked, and who does the asking?

Look back over the four dossiers. In each one, who was asked, and who was doing the asking?

A
On 15 April the only voices available were the company that owned the ship and the wire services. Nobody who had seen the sinking had been asked yet.
B
London's finding cites one answer, given to one of the Board of Trade's own lawyers. Most witnesses were never asked whether the ship broke.
C
Daly told a doctor, then a newspaper, then a court. Rheims told his wife, then a lawyer under oath. Their stories changed with the listener.
D
London asked no third-class passenger anything, then found they were not unfairly treated.
But
In 1985 it stopped mattering who had been asked: the wreck was in two pieces whoever you asked. And in Dossier D, once the question was fixed, the testimony settled it. "It depends who you ask" is not the end of the story.

If there is time: while it is happening, nobody knows

On 15 April 1912 the loudest voice in the record belonged to the company that owned the ship. In a war, both sides have reasons to shape what is said about the dead, and casualty figures are released as weapons. Historians spend decades afterwards unpicking them.

If interested parties dominate the record while an event is happening, what has to happen before anyone can know what occurred? And why is "why did they say it" often easier to answer than "what happened"?
