Two Accounts of Vodou

Two men wrote about the same religion, in the same country, and reached opposite conclusions. Read both before class. Your notes are saved in this browser on this device — bring them with you, on screen or printed.

One question runs underneath the whole reading: each author believed he was describing Vodou accurately. So what, exactly, was each one counting as evidence — and how did he get hold of it?

Text A

From Hayti, or the Black Republic, Chapter V: “Vaudoux Worship and Cannibalism”

What you are told before reading

Author
Spenser St. John
Occupation
British diplomat; British representative in Haiti for roughly twelve years
Published
1884, London. A second edition followed in 1886.
Training
None in anthropology, ethnography or religious studies
Language
Wrote in English. Did not live among the people he describes.

That is all you get for now. Everything else about him is Stage 2, at the end of this text.

Before you start. This text uses openly racist language of its period, and the second half describes the murder of a child in detail. It has not been softened, because the language and the choice of material are the evidence you are being asked to examine. Read it as a document, not as a report of fact. If you need to stop, stop, and tell your teacher — it will not cost you anything.
§1

According to the Arada negroes, who are the true sectaries of the Vaudoux in the colony, who maintain its principles and its rules, Vaudoux signifies an all-powerful and supernatural being, on whom depend all the events which take place in the world. This being is the non-venomous serpent, and it is under its auspices that all those assemble who profess this doctrine. Acquaintance with the past, knowledge of the present, prescience of the future, all appertain to this serpent, that only consents, however, to communicate his power and prescribe his will through the organ of a grand priest, whom the sectaries select, and still more by that of the negress whom the love of the latter has raised to the rank of high priestess.

ERead the first five words again. “According to the Arada negroes…” — how did St. John come to know what they say? He does not tell you. Write down every way he might have got this.

§2

These two delegates, who declare themselves inspired by their god, or in whom the gift of inspiration is really manifested in the opinion of their followers, bear the pompous names of King and Queen, or the despotic ones of Master and Mistress, or the touching titles of Papa and Mama. They are during their whole life the chiefs of the great family of the Vaudoux, and they have a right to the unlimited respect of those that compose it. […] To disobey them, to resist them, is to disobey God himself, and to expose oneself to the greatest misfortunes.

This system of domination on the one hand, and of blind obedience on the other, being well established, they at fixed dates meet together, and the King and Queen of the Vaudoux preside, following the forms which they probably brought from Africa, and to which Creole customs have added many variations […]

E“Pompous,” “despotic,” “blind obedience,” “probably.” Which words here report something, and which ones judge it? Mark two of each.

§3

The reunion for the true Vaudoux worship, for that which has least lost its primitive purity, never takes place except secretly, in the dead of night, and in a secure place safe from any profane eye. There each initiated puts on a pair of sandals and fastens around his body a number, more or less considerable, of red handkerchiefs […] The King and Queen place themselves at one end of the room, near a kind of altar, on which is a box where the serpent is kept, and where each adept can see it through the bars of its cage.

When they have verified that no curious stranger has penetrated into the place, they commence the ceremony by the adoration of the serpent […] They renew, holding the hands of the King and Queen, the oath of secrecy, which is the foundation of the association, and it is accompanied by everything horrible which delirium could imagine to render it more imposing.

CHe states twice that the ceremony is closed to outsiders, and that they check. Then he describes what happens inside it. Can both of those be true at once? What would have to have happened for both to be true?

§4

Then the crowd separates, and each one who may desire it […] approaches to implore the aid of the Vaudoux. Most of them ask for the talent to be able to direct the conduct of their masters. But this is not enough: one wants more money; another the gift of being able to please an unfeeling one; another desires to reattach an unfaithful lover; this one wishes for a prompt cure or long life […] There is not a passion which does not give vent to its vow, and crime itself does not always disguise those which have for object its success.

At each of these invocations the King of the Vaudoux appears absorbed in thought. The spirit seizes him; suddenly he takes hold of the box in which the serpent is confined, places it on the ground, and commands the Queen to get on it. As soon as the sacred ark is beneath her feet, the new Pythoness is filled by the spirit of their god; she trembles, all her body is in a state of convulsion, and the oracle speaks by her mouth. […] she dictates as decrees without appeal everything which she is pleased to prescribe, in the name of the serpent, to this imbecile crowd, that never expresses the slightest doubt of the most monstrous absurdity, and that only knows how to obey what is despotically dictated to it.

ENotice what people are asking for: money, health, love, long life, influence. Hold onto this paragraph. You will meet the same list in Text B, described by a man who reaches the opposite conclusion. What does St. John add to the list that turns it into something sinister?

§5

After all these questions have received some kind of an answer from the oracle […] his followers bring as tribute the objects they think most worthy […] It is from this collection that the expenses of the meetings are paid, that aid is afforded to absent members, or to those present who may be in want […] A fresh oath, as execrable as the first, engages each one to be silent as to what has passed, to aid in what has been settled; and sometimes a vase, in which there is the blood of a goat, still warm, seals on the lips of those present the promise to suffer death rather than reveal anything, and even to inflict it on any one who may forget that he is thus so solemnly bound to secrecy.

EOne sentence in this paragraph describes a mutual-aid fund for members who are ill or poor. Find it. Why do you think it passes almost unnoticed?

§6

After these ceremonies commences the dance of the Vaudoux. […] The King places his hand or his foot on the box in which the serpent is confined, and soon becomes agitated. This impression he communicates to the Queen, and from her it gains the whole circle, and every one commences certain movements, in which the upper part of the body, the head and shoulders, appear to be dislocated. […] This is augmented by the use of spirituous liquors, which the adepts do not spare. With some, fainting fits follow, with others a species of fury; but a nervous trembling seizes them all, which they appear unable to master. They go on spinning round, and in their excitement some tear their clothes, others bite their own flesh; then again many fall to the ground utterly deprived of sense, and are dragged into a neighbouring dark apartment. Here in the obscurity is too often a scene of the most disgusting prostitution.

At length lassitude puts an end to these demoralising scenes, to be renewed again at a date which is carefully settled beforehand.

C“Too often.” He is now describing a dark room he cannot have been in, at a ceremony that excludes strangers. Ask the awkward question plainly: on what is this sentence based?

The text now changes character. St. John stops describing ceremonies in general and narrates one specific criminal case: the killing of a girl named Claircine in December 1863, tried in Port-au-Prince in February 1864. Watch what kind of source he is now drawing on — and watch for the one moment he tells you where he was standing.

§7

On the 27th December 1863, Jeanne invited her sister, the mother of Claircine, to accompany her to Port-au-Prince, and the child, a girl of about twelve years of age, was left at home with Congo. Immediate advantage was taken of the mother’s absence, and Claircine was conducted to the house of Julien, and from thence to that of Floréal, where she was bound, and hidden under the altar in a neighbouring temple. In the evening, the mother, returning home, asked for her child, when her brother Congo told her it had strayed away; a pretended search was made by those in the plot, and another Papaloi was consulted. This man told the mother not to be uneasy, as the Maître d’Eau, or the spirit of the water, had taken her daughter, but that in a short time her child would be restored to her. The woman believed, or pretended to believe this story, and, by the papa’s recommendation, burnt candles before the altar of the Virgin Mary for the prompt return of her offspring, — another proof of the strange mingling of Catholicism and Vaudoux worship.

E“The woman believed, or pretended to believe.” He cannot know which. Why does he include both — and what does the phrase do to how you read her?

§8

On the evening of the 31st of December a large party assembled at the house of Jeanne to await the arrival of the child, who had remained for four days bound under the altar. When the chief members of the plot came to the temple to bring her out, she, guessing the fate reserved for her, gave two or three piercing shrieks, which were soon stifled, and gagged and bound, she was carried to Jeanne’s house, where preparations were made for the human sacrifice. She was thrown on the ground, her aunt holding her by the waist, whilst the Papaloi pressed her throat, and the others held her legs and arms; her struggles soon ceased, as Floréal had succeeded in strangling her. Then Jeanne handed him a large knife, with which he cut off Claircine’s head, the assistants catching the blood in a jar; then Floréal is said to have inserted an instrument under the child’s skin, and detached it from the body. Having succeeded in flaying their victim, the flesh was cut from the bones, and placed in large wooden dishes; the entrails and skin being buried near to the cottage. The whole party then started for Floréal’s house, carrying the remains of their victim with them. On their arrival Jeanne rang a little bell, and a procession was formed, the head borne aloft, and a sacred song sung. Then preparations were made for a feast.

VFind the four words “is said to have.” They appear once, in the middle of a paragraph otherwise written as plain fact. What is that phrase doing there — and why is it not attached to any of the other sentences?

§9

Roused by the noise caused by the arrival, a woman and a girl sleeping in another chamber looked through some chinks in the wall and saw all that passed, — Jeanne cooking the flesh with Congo beans, small and rather bitter (pois congo), whilst Floréal put the head into a pot with yams to make some soup. Whilst the others were engaged in the kitchen, one of the women present, Roséide Sumera, urged by the fearful appetite of a cannibal, cut from the child’s palm a piece of flesh and ate it raw (this I heard her avow in open court).

The cooking over, portions of the prepared dish were handed round, of which all present partook; and the soup being ready, it was divided among the assistants, who deliberately drank it. The night was passed in dancing, drinking, and debauchery. In the morning the remains of the flesh were warmed up, and the two witnesses who had watched the proceedings were invited to join in the repast: the two woman [sic] confessed that she had accepted the invitation, but the girl did not.

CThis is the one sentence in the whole text where he tells you where he was: “this I heard her avow in open court.” A courtroom in Haiti in 1864 — a confession, in a capital trial, from a defendant. Is that a strong source or a weak one? Give a reason either way.

§10

Fortunately for her, the inquiries which Claircine’s mother had made on her first arrival home and the disappearance of the second girl had roused the attention of an officer of police, and a search being made, the freshly-boiled skull of the murdered girl was found among the bushes near Floréal’s house […] Fourteen persons were arrested, against eight of whom sufficient evidence could be obtained, and these were sent to prison to answer for their crime before a criminal court. The trial commenced on the 4th of February 1864, and lasted two days. Incidents were related in the course of the evidence which showed how the lower classes are sunk in ignorance and barbarity, and renewed the proofs, if any fresh proofs were required, that the Vaudoux worship is associated by them with the ceremonies of the Catholic religion […]

VWatch the size of the claim change in one sentence. Eight people stood trial. What is the conclusion he draws — and about whom? Write down the exact step where the reasoning stops being about eight people.

Before Stage 2 — fill these in now

E — What counted as evidence for St. John?
List every kind of source behind this chapter. For each one, say how he got access to it. Be honest about the ones you can only guess at.
C — Who made him an expert?
He had no training in the study of religion. So why was he believed? What in his position gave the book its authority in London?

Stage 2 — do not open until your teacher gives you the word

You now know things about St. John that you worked out from the text itself. Stage 2 tells you things about him that you could not have worked out. Opening it early does not make you cleverer — it makes the next twenty minutes pointless, because you will find yourself repeating a label instead of reading.

Answer this first, in one line: what is the single strongest criticism you can make of this chapter using only what is printed above?

What the text did not tell you

  • His stated purpose. St. John wrote that he had come to “agree with those who deny that the negro could ever originate a civilisation.” The book is an argument that Haiti proves Black people cannot govern themselves.
  • What Haiti was. Haiti became independent in 1804 through the only successful large-scale slave revolt in history — the first Black republic in the world.
  • What was being done to it. France demanded 150 million francs in 1825 as “compensation” for lost slave property. The United States refused to recognise Haiti until 1862 — during St. John’s own posting — for fear of encouraging revolt at home. European powers worked to isolate it.
  • Who he worked for. A British diplomat, at the height of European imperial expansion into Africa and Asia. A Haiti described as barbaric was useful.
  • The intellectual climate. The 1880s were the height of “scientific racism” — Social Darwinism, phrenology, and theories of biological inferiority presented as science.
  • What happened next. The book caused a sensation. He repeated and expanded the cannibalism allegations in the 1886 edition. His account shaped Western opinion of Haiti for decades and fed into the US occupation of 1915–34 and a century of film and fiction.

Now the real question

The easy move is to say “he was biased, so ignore him.” That is not an analysis, and it is not what a historian does — his book is still a source about something. So:

The E question, sharpened
Given his purpose, what did his position let him count as evidence — and what did it make invisible to him? Name one thing that was happening in front of him that he could not see.
The 1890 test
A historian in 1890 has only this book. What would they have had to do to catch the problem — and was the material to catch it actually available to them? If not, what follows?
The hardest one
Something did happen to Claircine. A trial was held; a skull was found. Does establishing St. John’s purpose tell you the case was invented? Where exactly does his evidence stop supporting his conclusion?

Text B

Excerpts from Voodoo in Haiti, translated from the French by Hugo Charteris

What you are told before reading

Author
Alfred Métraux (1902–1963)
Occupation
Swiss anthropologist, working for UNESCO
Published
1958 in French; English translation 1959
Training
Professional ethnographer; had studied religion in several societies
Language
Learned Haitian Creole. Lived in the Marbial Valley and elsewhere in Haiti, 1949–50.

Same deal. Stage 2 comes at the end.

How to read this one. These are excerpts, joined by gaps marked […]. Métraux is not simply “the good one.” He is a European writing about Haitians too, and he says some things you may find cold. Apply exactly the same questions you applied to Text A. Do not let a sympathetic tone do your thinking for you.
§1

From the foreword.

Certain exotic words are charged with evocative power. Voodoo is one. It usually conjures up visions of mysterious deaths, secret rites — or dark saturnalia celebrated by “blood-maddened, sex-maddened, god-maddened” negroes.

EHe opens by quoting the thing he is arguing against. Why start there rather than with his own findings? What does it tell you about who he thinks he is writing for?

§2

A talented but rather fanciful American writer, W. H. Seabrook, has given the most complete account of the black legend of Voodoo. […] Even so, the few allusions to Voodoo which may be found in documents and books little known to the general public, could not have raised this rural paganism into the legendary terror it became, had not a British Consul, Spencer St. John, written a book, Haiti or the Black Republic (published in 1884), in which he described the most blood-curdling crimes committed by the Voodoo sect.

Spencer St. John’s revelations of alleged cannibalism in Haiti provoked, according to his own admission, very strong feeling in Europe and the United States. […] Although faced with an outcry in Haiti he nevertheless thought good to repeat his allegations in the second edition (1886), and even to add new details.

VMétraux is doing to St. John what you were just doing. Look at the words he chooses: “legend,” “alleged,” “thought good to.” He does not say St. John lied. What is the more precise charge he makes — and why is it a stronger move than calling him a liar?

§3

On how the book was made.

My information therefore derives to a great extent from what I learnt from Lorgina during my numerous visits to Haiti. […] Nor should I forget the young Haitians — Remy Bastien, Lamartinière Honorat, Michelson Hyppolite, R. Mortel, Jeanne G. Sylvain — who went with me to Marbial and took part in research.

[…] Certain Haitians will no doubt be saddened that a foreigner whom they welcomed so warmly has, like so many before him, felt the need to write a book on Voodoo […]

CHe names a Vodou priestess and five Haitian researchers. St. John named no informants at all. What changes when sources have names? And ask the harder half: whose book is it, if five other people did the research?

§4

On what the spirits (loa) are asked to do.

He is expected to effect cures, so he must touch the sick and improvise treatment. […] The possessed — or to be more precise, the gods — prophesy, threaten sinners and gladly give advice. […] Usually he dispenses small favours among the onlookers — clasping both hands of some rather roughly, anointing the faces of others with his sweat, or shaking their clothes to bring them luck.

EGo back to §4 of Text A. Cures, advice, luck, protection, settling disputes. The two men report almost the same activity. So where does the difference between the two accounts actually live — in what they saw, or in what they did with it?

§5

On possession.

Trance usually occurs during religious ceremonies, private as well as public. Spirits must take part in the homage which is paid to them and must themselves receive the sacrifices offered. Their appearance is expected and takes place at the desired moment. […] Song, or more often drumming, has an undeniable effect on certain subjects.

The individual in a state of trance is in no way responsible for his deeds or words. He has ceased to exist as a person. […] It would be improper to make the “horse” responsible for the actions or utterances of his invisible rider. […] Someone possessed can express with impunity thoughts which he would hesitate to utter aloud in normal circumstances. […] The possessed hold opinions or give free rein to aggression which can only be explained by repressed grudges. Their indiscretion is sometimes shocking.

ECompare this with St. John’s convulsing “Pythoness” (§4 of Text A). Both describe a person shaking and speaking as a god. What has Métraux added that St. John did not have? One word will do it: he explains what possession is for.

§6

When a man becomes the vehicle of a god, has he lost all sense of reality, or is he simply an actor speaking a part? […] Apart from chaotic preliminaries there is in most cases of possession a theatrical element which unavoidably suggests a certain amount of simulation or at least of intentional delusion. […] For a person to admit that he remembers what he said or did as a god, is to admit that he was not genuinely possessed.

VThis is the coldest passage in Text B. He is raising the possibility that people are acting. Is this the same judgement St. John makes, or a different one? Look at what each man does after the doubt: what does St. John conclude, and what does Métraux conclude?

§7

Two moments where he tells you where he was standing.

I shall base my description of this on what I saw at Lorgina’s in 1949.

[On possession by the marassa, the divine twins:] She had gone back to being a child. She asked for sweets in childish tones and for a bit of stuff to make a dress for her doll. […] Those whom they do seize become like little children, “tyrannical and capricious”. They roll on the ground, get up, walk uncertainly and ask for food.

CCount them across both texts. How many times does each author tell you where he was standing when he saw the thing he is describing? Write the two numbers down. That difference is the whole lesson.

Before Stage 2 — fill these in now

E — What counted as evidence for Métraux?
List the kinds of source behind this book, and how he got access to each. Then: is there anything St. John could reach that Métraux, arriving in 1949, could not?
C — Who made him an expert?
A university, a discipline, UNESCO, and a priestess who let him in. Which of those four does the most work — and which one would a historian find hardest to check?

Stage 2 — again, wait for the word

Answer first, in one line: using only what is printed above, what is the strongest criticism you can make of Métraux?

What the text did not tell you

  • He was sent. Métraux went to Haiti on a UNESCO project, in the years when the UN was building an official position that racial hierarchy had no scientific basis. His conclusions were welcome to his employer.
  • The moment. 1949–50: the beginning of decolonisation. Anthropology was rebuilding itself in public against exactly the assumptions St. John wrote from.
  • How it was received. The anthropologist Sidney Mintz called it the most authoritative general account of Vodou available. It became the standard scholarly work — and remains far less widely known than St. John’s.
  • The access. Métraux was admitted to ceremonies by Lorgina Delorge, a priestess. Someone decided to let him in. That decision is part of his evidence base, and he could not control it.

Now the real question

The symmetry test
You have just been told Métraux’s employer, moment and politics — the same kind of information that damaged St. John. Does it damage Métraux equally? If not, say precisely why not, without using the words “he was right.”
The gatekeeper
Lorgina let him in and shaped what he saw. Is that a weakness in his evidence, a strength, or both? What would it take to find out?

Three questions to arrive with an answer to

1. The overlap
Find one thing both men describe in roughly the same way. Given that they agree on it, why do they end up so far apart? Locate the exact point where the two accounts separate.
2. The ranking
You are going to be tempted to say “they are both just perspectives.” Resist it for one minute and answer instead: is one of these accounts better than the other? If yes, say what standard you are using — and where that standard came from.
3. The uncomfortable one
Métraux had access St. John never had, and 74 more years of method. In 2050, what will someone say about his account that he could not have seen? What does that do to the idea of historical progress?

If the past cannot be observed or repeated, what gives historical knowledge its authority — and what can it never reach?

Everything above feeds one cell or another of the grid we are building across this unit. Text A and Text B are not a story about a bad man and a good man. They are two answers to the question what counts as evidence — and the difference between those answers is where historical knowledge is actually made.
