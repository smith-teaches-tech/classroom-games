Warm-up · evidence or proof?

Fifty tries. Fifty failures.

Commit first Your friend tries to solve a puzzle 50 times and fails every time. Does your friend now know the puzzle can’t be solved?
Evidence

Examples. “I tried 50 times and it never worked.” Evidence makes a claim likely. Try number 51 could still work.

Proof

A reason that covers every case, including the cases nobody tried. Every step is forced by the step before, so the conclusion is forced too.

Remember 2 = 1 from Lesson 5? Checking a proof does not mean testing examples. It means checking that every step is allowed. One illegal step, and the whole proof fails.

Our failures are evidence, not proof

There are millions of ways to lay the dominoes, and nobody can try them all. However many times we fail, a cleverer layout might still exist.

The proof · six steps

Why nobody will ever do it.

1Squares that sit side by side on a chessboard are always different colours.Check: look at any chessboard.
2So every domino covers one dark square and one light square.A domino covers two side-by-side squares (step 1).
3The two corners that were cut off are the same colour: both dark.Check: look at the board.
4So 30 dark squares and 32 light squares are left.A full board has 32 of each. 32 − 2 = 30.
531 dominoes would cover exactly 31 dark squares and 31 light squares.Step 2, done 31 times.
6But only 30 dark squares are left. So the board can never be covered: not by you, not by anyone, not in any order.Steps 4 and 5 can’t both be true.

Nobody’s

You checked every step yourself. You didn’t need to trust your teacher, a textbook or an expert.

That is what a proof does. Every step is forced, so the conclusion is forced, for anyone who can follow the steps. Hold on to that last part.

Infinite, but still checkable · Euclid, about 300 BC

Do the prime numbers ever run out?

The chessboard has only 62 squares. What about a claim about infinitely many numbers? A prime number divides only by 1 and itself: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13 … Claim: the primes go on forever.

Try it Pick some primes. Multiply them together and add 1. Is the answer always a prime? Try all six.
1Suppose someone hands you a list that claims to contain every prime.
2Multiply every prime on the list together, then add 1.
3Divide the new number by any prime on the list: there is always 1 left over. So no prime on the list divides it.Watch the calculator: remainder 1, every time.
4Every whole number bigger than 1 is either a prime or divides by a prime. So some prime is missing from the list.
5So no list of primes is ever complete. The primes go on forever.
So size isn’t the problem

Infinitely many cases, five steps, and you can check every one. A problem can be infinite and still have a short proof. What matters is whether you can follow every step.

Same question, different answer

With the chessboard, I could show you a six-step reason. This time I can’t, and neither can anyone else: nobody has ever found a short proof that four colours are always enough.

The claim does have a proof, though. Here is how it was found.

The story · 1852 to 1976

124 years to prove a colouring rule.

1852

Francis Guthrie, colouring a map of the counties of England, notices that four colours always seem to be enough. He can’t prove it. Nor can anyone else.

1879

Alfred Kempe publishes a proof. Mathematicians accept it.

1890

Percy Heawood finds a mistake in Kempe’s proof, 11 years later. The claim is unproven again. Heawood does prove that five colours are always enough, in a short proof a strong student can follow.

1976

Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken prove that four colours are enough. Their proof shows that every possible map must contain one of nearly 2,000 special patterns, then has a computer check every pattern. The checking takes more than 1,000 hours of computer time. No person has ever checked the whole proof by hand.

Compare it with the chessboard What could you do with the chessboard proof that you can’t do with this one?
You can’t check it

With the chessboard you checked every step yourself. With the Four Colour proof you would have to trust the computer, and the people who wrote its program.

When the proof came out, some mathematicians refused to count it. Others accepted it but were unhappy: the proof showed that four colours are enough, but not why.

And remember 1879: mathematicians accepted a wrong proof for 11 years.

2005 · checking the checker

A machine checks the machine.

In 2005, Georges Gonthier had the whole proof checked again by a computer program built for one job: checking proofs, one step at a time (it is called Coq).

What changed

You no longer have to trust the programs from 1976. You only have to trust the small core of the checking program.

Discuss Did the need for trust disappear, or did it move somewhere else?
Notebook 2
When you can’t check a proof yourself, what are you really trusting?
People and tools

You are trusting the programmers, the checking program, and the mathematicians who say they checked. The proof is still a chain of forced steps. What changed is who can check the chain.

The proof nobody can certify · abc

Six hundred pages. One step.

The abc conjecture is one of the most famous unsolved problems about whole numbers. You don’t need to know the maths. Almost no one on Earth does, and that is the point.

2012

Shinichi Mochizuki, a respected mathematician at Kyoto University, posts hundreds of pages claiming to prove the abc conjecture. He invented a new kind of mathematics to do it. Almost no one can follow it.

2018

Peter Scholze and Jakob Stix, two leading mathematicians, spend five days in Kyoto with Mochizuki. They report a gap in one step, called Corollary 3.12, so serious that, in their view, small fixes will not rescue the proof. Mochizuki says they have misunderstood it.

2021

The proof is published in PRIMS, a journal where Mochizuki is the chief editor. He stepped back from the review and did not attend the meetings about his paper. Scholze says his concerns were never addressed.

2026

Project LANA, a team checking the argument with a proof-checking program called Lean, reports that it has not found the theory wrong. But it has narrowed the dispute to one precise point, which nobody has yet written out in full.

Today the abc proof is accepted as a theorem in Kyoto and treated as an unproven conjecture almost everywhere else.
Commit · who decides?

Is the abc conjecture now known to be true?

Then discuss Who gets to decide? And why doesn’t the abc proof force anyone to agree, the way the chessboard proof forced you?
A proof only forces people who can follow it

The chessboard proof forced you to agree because you could check every step. When almost nobody can follow the steps, the proof can’t do that job by itself.

Then a community has to check the proof and certify it. A community can get stuck (abc), and it can be wrong for a while (Kempe).

Note the recusal. Nobody cheated here: the journal followed its process, and most mathematicians outside Kyoto still don’t accept the proof. Publication is not the same as certification by the whole community.

The cut · proof ≠ acceptance

New checks don’t overturn a correct proof. They test claimed proofs: they expose a flaw (Kempe, 1890), confirm a proof (Coq, 2005), or narrow a dispute (LANA, 2026). What gets overturned is the community’s acceptance.

You’ve met this cut before

Lesson 1: being art ≠ being good. Today: being proved ≠ being accepted. In both, a community’s verdict and the thing itself can come apart.
