The fill · E · Maths

So where does proof's rigor actually live?

Not in looking convincing. In every step being licensed — traceable back to axioms and rules agreed in advance. Break one licence and the whole chain is worth nothing, however elegant the rest.

E · Maths — what counts as a reason here?

Proof. Deductive demonstration from axioms. Given the axioms, the conclusion isn't supported, isn't made likely — it is necessitated. There is no stronger relation between a reason and a conclusion anywhere in the whole grid.

Parked, 60 seconds, then dropped: someone will ask whether maths is discovered or invented. It's a real question and it belongs to philosophy, not here — it has no second-order payoff for us, because whichever answer you take, the justification structure on the board is identical. Note it, park it, move.

By hand · before any screen

Build a triangle with three right angles.

Euclid: the angles of a triangle sum to 180°. You have known this since you were eleven. Test it.

You need: an orange (or any ball), a marker, string
  1. Mark a dot at the top of the orange. Call it the pole.
  2. Draw a line all the way round the middle — the equator.
  3. Mark two dots on the equator, a quarter of the way round from each other.
  4. Join all three dots with string, pulled taut. Taut string on a sphere is the straightest possible path — that is what a straight line is here.
  5. Measure the three corners.
Record before you look at the screen Angle at the pole: ____°   Angle 1: ____°   Angle 2: ____°
Sum: ____°
Write the sum down. Don't round it to what it ought to be.
Then answer Is this a real triangle — three straight sides, three corners? If yes, Euclid is wrong. If no, say precisely which of his words you are refusing to apply.

The part that matters for later

The excess grows with the triangle. Shrink it toward a point and the excess vanishes — a small triangle on a sphere is, to any measurement you could make, 180°. Locally, a curved world looks flat. Remember this in two screens.

What you actually broke

Not a theorem. A choice made in 300 BC.

Euclid built geometry on five postulates. Four are unarguable. The fifth is the one about parallels — and it always looked less obvious than the rest. For two thousand years mathematicians tried to prove it from the other four, believing it must be a hidden theorem.

On your orange

Lines of longitude cross the equator at right angles — by Euclid, that makes them parallel. Follow them. They meet at both poles. On a sphere there are no parallel lines at all. The fifth postulate isn't false here; it simply was never assumed.

The nobody-proved-it ending

They failed because it cannot be done. Gauss saw it by 1817 and published nothing. Bolyai put it in a 24-page appendix to his father's book in 1832; Lobachevsky published in 1829 — drop the fifth postulate, assume many parallels, and no contradiction ever appears. Riemann, in 1854, took the other road: assume none. That is your orange.

In 1868 Beltrami closed it: he built a working model of non-Euclidean geometry inside Euclidean geometry. If one is consistent, so is the other. You cannot rule either out by logic. The axioms were never discoveries about space. They were a choice — and a different choice gives you a different geometry, equally rigorous, equally certain, equally unbreakable.

The obvious objection

"Then just measure a big triangle and see."

Someone did have the biggest triangle anyone had ever measured. Around 1820 Gauss surveyed between three German peaks — Hohehagen, Brocken and Inselsberg — sides of roughly 69, 85 and 107 kilometres.

The result

180°, within the limits of his instruments. Which settles precisely nothing — remember the last screen. Any curvature of space is so slight that a triangle a hundred kilometres wide is nowhere near big enough to show it. A null result here is exactly what you'd get either way.

And a reading the evidence won't carry — sound familiar?

The story that Gauss did this in order to test whether space is curved is repeated everywhere. Historians of maths mostly reject it: no documentary evidence supports it, and his biographer argues the great triangle was a control for checking the smaller survey triangles inside it. A famous, satisfying, widely-taught reading — with nothing anchoring it. You have a name for that from Lesson 4.

The fill · R · Maths

Physics could. Proof never can.

Einstein's general relativity, 1915, describes gravity as the curvature of spacetime — and to write it he needed the geometry Riemann had built with no application in mind. In 1919 an eclipse expedition measured starlight bending as it passed the sun. The prediction held.

R · Maths — what can't it reach?

Which axioms are true of the world. Euclidean and non-Euclidean geometries are each perfectly consistent; mathematics certifies both and cannot choose between them. Which one describes the space we live in was settled empirically — by telescopes, during an eclipse. Internal certainty: total. World-application: a separate question, and not a mathematical one.

So — certain about what?

Certain about what follows from what. Given these axioms, that conclusion, with a force nothing else in the grid can match. What maths does not give you, and never claimed to, is a reason to believe the axioms describe anything outside the system. The hardest gate in the whole grid, and it does not open onto the world either.

Not a claim that maths is unreliable. Bridges stand. The point is narrower and stranger: the certainty is internal, and the step from a consistent system to a true description of the world is taken by evidence, not by proof — which puts it back on science's side of the table, not maths'.
