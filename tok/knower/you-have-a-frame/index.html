Horace Miner, Body Ritual Among the Nacirema (1956)

While you read

Keep a pen in your hand. Four things.

  1. Underline any practice that strikes you as strange.
  2. Next to each one, write a single word for how it struck you. Be honest — whatever word actually came to mind.
  3. Mark one sentence where you thought: do these people really believe that?
  4. When you finish, write one sentence at the bottom of the page describing the Nacirema to someone who has not read the essay.

The map is not the territory.

The Underground map is true. It still misleads — because of what it leaves out.

Miner left nothing out. He describes things people really do.

So why did you still get the wrong idea?

“…scraping and lacerating the surface of the face with a sharp instrument.”

“…women bake their heads in small ovens for about an hour.”

“…inserting a small bundle of hog hairs into the mouth, along with certain magical powders.”

Is any of that wrong?

Find one sentence about what these people do that is actually false.

Read out the sentence you wrote at the bottom of your page.

Miner never told you what these people were like. You wrote that sentence yourself.

So where did it come from?

Miner calls the dentist a holy-mouth-man.
He calls fillings magical materials.
He says the mouth is believed to have a supernatural influence on friendship.

Do Americans think their dentist is holy?

So what has Miner done here that he did not do when he described shaving?

The ancient Egyptians preserved their dead with chemicals and buried them with objects they would need.

So do we.

Why is one of those a ritual and the other a funeral?

We say Egyptian society was steeped in tradition. We say a tribal society is governed by custom. What are we steeped in — and why is that harder to name?

Some of Miner's claims you could check by watching someone.

Some you could only check by asking them what they believe.

Sort a few. Which kind did he get right? Which kind did he get wrong?

Where did the wrong ones come from, if he never watched them?

Miner sounds completely sure of himself from the first line to the last.

Did his confidence make you trust him?

Should it have?

What would you have needed in order to check him?

In what way do you look at other cultures the way Miner looks at yours?

How might they look at you?

What does that say about your framework?

Miner ends his essay by quoting another anthropologist — and leaves it there, without comment.

“Looking from far and above, from our high places of safety in the developed civilization, it is easy to see all the crudity and irrelevance of magic. But without its power and guidance early man could not have mastered his practical difficulties as he has done, nor could man have advanced to the higher stages of civilization.” Bronislaw Malinowski, quoted by Miner, 1956

What is Miner warning us about?

How might he be mocking us — the readers — and not the Nacirema at all? What does that say about our own frames?

Four questions, in order.

1. Could a dentist learn to fill a tooth from Miner's essay?

2. Could a visitor learn from it why Americans go to the dentist?

3. So what can this essay not do?

4. It did do something to you. What?

Now the one that matters: if a description can be useless for understanding people and still worth reading, what makes a description a good one?

A framework is what you assume without noticing. What is normal. What counts as a good reason. What is not even worth asking.

Name one thing you assumed was normal until today.

Now name three more.

Why did that get hard so fast?

Miner got the actions right and the meanings wrong.

So what decides the picture a description leaves you with — and can you ever check your own framework?

If you are stuck For the first half, three things were doing work all lesson: the words chosen, what was left in and left out, and who was describing — from where.

For the second half: you caught Miner's framework because you already knew the Nacirema. So what would you need in order to catch your own?

Write a paragraph in your notebook.

Now you do it

Pick something you do regularly. Describe it in 60–80 words the way Miner would. Every sentence must be true — describe only what someone could watch you do.

Do not name the thing. The class is going to guess.

Ideas: the exam hall · the bus queue · graduation · handing in your phone · the canteen · a football crowd · a birthday party · the gym · queueing for new trainers · a family photo · a group chat · a haircut · the last day of term
