The AI Knows Me

A first class activity

Take your seats. We're starting with a question.

What does it know?

⏳ You have 3 minutes. Be honest. No phones, no looking around.
Write down three things:
1. What does an AI chatbot (ChatGPT, Claude, whatever you use) know about you?
2. How did it come to know that?
3. Is it knowledge?
TEACHER: Circulate and read. You need 2–3 specific answers for Phase 3. Pick ones that show different reasoning (pattern / behavior / preference).

Watch this.

📱
Query: "I'm an 18-year-old IB student in [your city]. What do I probably care about? What stresses me?"

AI Output: You likely care about college admissions, grades, university choices, peer pressure, social media image, balancing activities. You're stressed about workload, competition, whether you're "doing enough," family expectations…

Weird, right?

Another example.

📊
Your Netflix "For You" page knows:
  • What you watch (and don't finish)
  • How long you pause
  • What time you watch
  • What you rewind
  • What you search for

It shows you what comes next. It's right. But is that knowledge?

First question.

Turn to someone near you. One minute.
Does the AI know you the same way your best friend knows you? How is it different?
⏳ Don't raise your hand yet — think. Then turn. Then share.
TEACHER: After pair-share, take 2–3 answers. Listen for: "AI has more data" / "Friend understands me" / "AI doesn't care" / "It doesn't know why I do things."

So what does the AI know?

Say it in one sentence. What does the AI know about you?
⏳ Think first. Hands up at the top of your head.
You're looking for: Patterns. Preferences. Behavior. What you like.
TEACHER: If they say "everything," push: "More data than your teacher, right? So does more data = knowledge? Or is 'know' doing different work?"

But wait.

Your teacher knows your patterns too. 100 hours of class. The AI has your Instagram for 8 years. Who knows more about you?
⏳ Think. Then hands.
Next move: "Right. But here's the question: Does 'more data' mean the same thing as 'knowledge'? Or are we using the word 'know' differently?"
TEACHER: This is the collision. Let silence sit. This is where they feel the grid forming.

Here's a nastier one.

Two things could both be true:

  • The AI says you'll doomscroll for 45 minutes at midnight (pattern)
  • Your best friend says you do that when you're anxious (reason, meaning)
How can both be right about the same behavior if they're knowing it two different ways?
⏳ Don't answer. Just write one sentence in your notebook. How are those two different kinds of knowing?
TEACHER: Give them 90 seconds. Collect the notebooks at the end (they're your diagnostic). You're looking for: Do they see this as two ways of knowing the same thing? Or do they rank them (real vs fake)?

This is your year.

This question: What counts as knowledge? Who gets to decide? And does the answer change depending on what you're trying to know?

You'll ask it in science, religion, language, art, math. Same tool. Different answers.

And today you just did it without the tool.

Before you go.

⏱ You have 5 minutes.
Write 3–4 sentences on a fresh page.

The AI knows your patterns. Your teacher knows your thinking. Your parent knows your fears. They all know you. But they know you in different ways.

Pick two of those (or pick your own pair). Write: What's the difference between what each one knows? And what would have to be true for them to 'know' the other way?
TEACHER: Collect these. You're diagnosing: Do they think one is "real knowing" and the other is fake? Or have they spotted that "knowing" has different structures depending on the context? That diagnostic tells you where to pitch the grid.
