    Part 1 · 15 minutes

    Farmer John and the cow

    Read this out loud in your group. All of it. Then argue about it before you touch a button.

    A farmer called John owns a cow named Betsy.

    A neighbour walks past and says, “I didn't see Betsy in the field just now.”

    John goes to the window and looks. In the distance he sees a brown-and-white shape. Fine, he thinks. Betsy is in the field after all.

    Now the twist. The brown-and-white shape John saw was not Betsy. It was a bush. But Betsy was in the field — standing out of sight, behind a hill.

    Do this before you reveal anything

    Question: Did John know that Betsy was in the field?

    Vote as a group. Then every person circles the same answer and writes their own reason.

    WORKSHEET Q1

    No. John did not know.

    Go through it slowly:

    • Was it true? Yes. Betsy really was in the field.
    • Did he believe it? Yes.
    • Did he have a reason? Yes — he saw a brown-and-white shape and it looked like a cow.

    All three boxes are ticked. And he still didn't know.

    Here is why. The thing John looked at was a bush. The thing that made his belief true was Betsy, hidden behind a hill. The bush and Betsy have nothing to do with each other. His reason and the truth ended up in the same place by accident. They never touched.

    Being right by accident is not knowing. Luck is not knowledge.

    Stop and write — two questions

    Q2. Fill in the three boxes for Farmer John: was it true, did he believe it, and what was his reason — plus what was wrong with that reason.

    Q3. Finish the sentence in your own words: John's reason and the truth ended up in the same place, but…

    WORKSHEET Q2 WORKSHEET Q3

    The tool you just earned

    For about two thousand years the standard answer to “what is knowledge?” was three conditions. It is usually called justified true belief, or JTB. You know something when:

    1. It is true. (You can't know something false.)

    2. You believe it. (You can't know something you don't think is so.)

    3. You have a good reason. (A lucky guess doesn't count.)

    Farmer John ticks all three boxes and still fails. So the three conditions are not quite enough. Something is missing, and it is this:

    The move for the whole lesson Your reason has to reach the claim. It has to connect to the thing that makes the claim true — not just happen to land nearby.

    The man who first pointed out this crack was Edmund Gettier, in a three-page paper in 1963. Philosophers have argued about it ever since. We are not going to. We are going to use it.

    Two more of the same shape — read them, they take 30 seconds each

    The stopped clock. You look at the wall clock. It says 3:00. You believe it is 3:00. It is 3:00 — but the clock stopped twelve hours ago and nobody noticed. You did not know the time. You read a broken clock at the one moment it was accidentally right.

    The aching knee. Your knee aches. You say it will rain tomorrow. It rains. But the ache came from a long walk and the rain came from a weather system. Nothing joined them. You guessed and got lucky.

    Q4. In all three cases — the bush, the clock, the knee — the belief was true and the reason was useless. Same shape every time. Put that shape into one sentence of your own.

    Part 2 · 10 minutes

    Back to the Elder

    You met him last class and you already wrote about him. Now you have a tool you didn't have then. Use it on him.

    If you were away last class, this is everything you need

    A Committee sat for four hours trying to decide which of six knowers knew the most — among them a sushi chef, a satnav, a physicist, a four-year-old, a sheepdog, and the Elder. They never managed to agree, because they never agreed on what they were measuring. That was the point of last lesson. Today only the Elder matters.

    The Elder can recite four hundred years of his family's history from memory — who was born to whom, in what order, generation by generation, all the way back.

    He never read it anywhere. There was nothing to read. He learned it from the elder before him, who learned it from the elder before her, who learned it from the one before her. That is how it has always been passed on.

    The Committee decided to test him. They checked his whole recitation against the village's written archive, line by line. Every single name matched. The Committee was very pleased with itself.

    Then the Fourth Member ruined the mood:

    “So did the archive make it knowledge? Or did he know it all along, and the archive just agreed with him? Because those are not the same, and I'd like it minuted that they are not the same.”

    Last lesson you were allowed to argue for any answer you liked. Today there is a right one, and you now have the tool that finds it.

    Do this before you reveal anything

    Question: Before the archive was checked, did the Elder have a justified true belief?

    Run all three boxes: true? believed? good reason? Everyone writes before anyone clicks.

    WORKSHEET Q5

    Not yet.

    True? Yes — we found that out afterwards. Believed? Absolutely. Good reason? This is where it breaks.

    Ask the Elder how he knows. The honest answer is: “I was told.”

    Ask who told him. “Someone who was told.” Ask who told them. Same answer, four hundred years deep.

    The chain never lands on anything you could go and check. It just keeps handing you back to another person saying the same thing. That is a full stop: it ends the conversation instead of sending you somewhere.

    The archive changed that. Now there is a second source — written down, made by different hands — that agrees. Suddenly the reason points at something outside the Elder's head that you could actually go and look at.

    The Elder was right the whole time. He was not justified until his reason had somewhere to point.

    Stop and write

    Q6. What exactly did the archive add that the Elder did not already have? (Careful — it was not a new fact. He had all the names already.)

    WORKSHEET Q6

    Now the hard one — three or four sentences, not one line

    Nothing inside the Elder's head changed on the day of the check. He knew exactly the same names on Wednesday as he did on Tuesday. So if the archive is what turned it into knowledge — does that mean a community that keeps no written records holds no knowledge at all?

    Write what you actually think. Don't be lazy about it. Mr. Smith will ask, and he will know.

    Part 3 · 20 minutes

    Sort the cards

    Each card makes a claim and gives a reason. Judge the reason, not the claim. The cards come in pairs — same claim, different reason. That's deliberate.

    A — ReachesThe reason connects to the thing that makes the claim true. This is knowledge.
    B — HollowThe reason sounds impressive but points at nothing you could check. It's a full stop.
    C — LuckyA Farmer John. The claim may well be true — but this reason is not what makes it true. It only stands nearby.

    Stuck between B and C?

    Ask: if the claim turns out to be true, was this reason the thing that made it true — or was it just standing nearby when it happened? Standing nearby is C. Pointing at nothing at all is B. Some cards are arguable. Argue about them.

    How to run this

    For each pair: read both cards aloud → agree as a group → write your letters in the worksheet grid → then click to check. Put the actual answer in the next column, and if you got it wrong, write why in the last one.

    You are aiming to be able to say why, not to get a high score.

    Part 4 · 30 minutes

    The Authority Repair

    “Because an expert said so” is the most common reason anyone gives for anything. Sometimes it's a brilliant reason. Sometimes it's worthless. Here is how to tell the difference — and how to fix it.

    A full stop says: the textbook says so. Full stop. End of conversation. It doesn't matter whether the source is a textbook, a sacred text, a scientist, a teacher or your mother — if the reason stops at the source, no checking has happened.

    A pointer says: the textbook says so, AND here is why this textbook is trusted on this particular topic, AND here is what would show it to be wrong. A pointer points past itself to something you could check.

    The three moves — you will use these three times

    1. Name the authority, specifically. A vague authority can't be checked.
    2. Say why that authority is trusted on this kind of claim. This is the hard one, and it is the one that matters.
    3. Say what would show the authority is wrong. A real authority can be corrected.

    Stage 1 — Watch it done · the Harvard MBA

    Read this together. Don't skim it. Stage 3 gives you no help at all, so take what you need now.

    The claim: This stock will go up this year, because the man giving the advice has a Harvard MBA.

    Move 1 — Name the authority
    Who exactly is being trusted here?

    A Harvard MBA — a two-year graduate degree in business from a very selective university. Note what we are not doing: we're not laughing at it. It is a real qualification and hard to get. If we want to reject this appeal, we have to actually show why it fails, not sneer at it.

    Move 2 — Why trusted on this claim?
    Does the qualification actually cover the thing being claimed?

    Here's where it falls apart. An MBA trains you to read financial statements, analyse a business, and run a company. It does not train you to predict which particular stock will rise in the next twelve months — nobody is trained to do that reliably, because if anyone could do it reliably they would already be extremely rich and extremely quiet about it. The qualification is real. It just doesn't cover this claim.

    Move 3 — What would show it's wrong?
    Is there anything we could check?

    Pull his last twenty stock predictions and see how they did. If they're no better than flipping a coin, the appeal is dead. And notice: the person making the appeal didn't offer us that record. They offered us the degree instead.

    Say this out loud in your group A pointer is an evaluation, not an endorsement. Sometimes it shows the appeal works. Sometimes it shows it fails. Either way you've done the work that “because he said so” refused to do.

    The three moves did not make the stock advice sound better. They killed it. That is what the tool is for. If you find yourself rewriting sentences to make them sound more reasonable, you have misunderstood the task.

    Look back at Part 3: the physicist card and the “scientifically formulated” card fail in exactly this way. Real expertise, wrong subject. Same move.

    Stop and write

    Q10. In one sentence: why did the three moves reject this appeal even though the qualification is real? And copy down the line about evaluation and endorsement — you will want it.

    WORKSHEET Q10

    Stage 2 — Do it together · the approved medicine

    The claim: This new medicine is safe, because the national health authority approved it.

    Same three moves, different shape of authority. Last time it was one person with a qualification. This time it is an institution — and institutions are trusted because of the process running underneath them.

    Rule for this stage

    Q11 has a row for each of the three moves. Every person writes their own answer to a move before the group reveals it. If you reveal first you have learned nothing, and you will be found out in Stage 3.

    WORKSHEET Q11
    Move 1 — Name the authority
    Which health authority? Approved when? On the basis of what?

    “The government” is not good enough — you couldn't go and look at that. Something like: the national medicines regulator, which approved this drug in a named year after reviewing the trial data submitted by the manufacturer. A pointer has to point somewhere a stranger could actually go.

    Move 2 — Why trusted on this claim?
    Is the institution itself the thing that knows — or does it know because of something behind it?

    The institution isn't authoritative because it's the institution. It's authoritative because of the chain behind it. Copy this chain onto your worksheet:

    the regulator → its approval process → clinical trials → how those trials were designed → published results → checked by other scientists

    Every arrow is a place you could stop and look. That is the difference between an institution and a rumour.

    Move 3 — What would show it's wrong?
    List as many as you can. Don't stop at one.
    • Reports of harm appearing after the drug went on sale
    • Other laboratories running the trial again and not getting the same result
    • Finding the company that makes the drug also paid for the trial
    • The approval being withdrawn somewhere else

    Every item on that list is something you could in principle check. That is exactly what makes this an authority worth appealing to.

    The pairing that matters With the MBA, the three moves rejected the appeal. Here they accepted it — with conditions. The moves didn't change. The chain did.

    We are not training you to trust authorities, and we are not training you to distrust them. We are training you to trace what they are standing on.

    Stop and write

    Q12. The moves rejected the MBA appeal and accepted this one. The moves did not change. What did?

    WORKSHEET Q12

    Stage 3 — On your own · Jupiter

    Two sentences. They make the same kind of claim and back it the same way, from two different sources.

    Sentence A. The planet Jupiter has 79 moons, because the textbook says so.

    Sentence B. The planet Jupiter has 79 moons, because the sacred text says so.

    First: do not argue about Jupiter

    Whether Jupiter has 79 moons is not the point (the count keeps changing as astronomers find more, which is a hint you may want later). The point is the shape of each sentence. Both make a factual claim. Both back it with three words: X says so. Both treat the source as a full stop. They fail in the same way.

    Your task — on the worksheet, on your own, no reveal button

    Pick ONE sentence. Rewrite it so the appeal to authority becomes a pointer instead of a full stop. You may keep the claim about Jupiter exactly as it is. What has to change is whether the sentence shows that any evaluating happened.

    If you pick Sentence B: a strong answer talks about the community's tradition of interpretation and who is qualified to interpret the text. It does not argue about whether the religion is true. That is not what is being asked and it is not what TOK does.

    Q13 gives you a box for each of the three moves first, then space for the rewritten sentence. Work the moves before you write the sentence.

    WORKSHEET Q13

    Check yourself honestly. Does your rewrite explain why this source is trusted on this particular kind of claim?

    That's Move 2, and it is the one people skip. A rewrite like “the textbook says Jupiter has 79 moons, and if astronomers find more the textbook would be wrong” has Move 1 and Move 3 and a hole where the thinking should be.

    What Move 2 sounds like: “Textbooks are treated as authoritative on settled astronomical facts because they summarise observations made by professional astronomers using telescopes and spacecraft.” That sentence explains the trust instead of assuming it.

    If your rewrite is missing Move 2, fix it now. Move 1 and Move 3 are housekeeping. Move 2 is the lesson.

    Exit · 5 minutes

    One question before you go

    Worksheet Q14 · on your own · no discussion What makes a reason good enough — rather than just convincing?

    (The formal version, for your notes: what makes a justification adequate, rather than merely convincing?)

    Before you answer, look back

    • Farmer John was convincing. He was not good enough. Why not?
    • The Elder was right for four hundred years. What did the archive add?
    • “Scientifically formulated” is convincing. What is it missing?

    A good answer will name at least one of these two ideas. A strong answer names both:

    1. A good enough reason reaches the claim through a chain you could trace — it doesn't just sit near it.

    2. A good enough reason can be corrected — it tells you what would show it to be wrong.

    Careful: “it has evidence” is not yet an answer. Everyone in Part 3 had evidence. The knee had evidence. The question is what makes evidence reach.
