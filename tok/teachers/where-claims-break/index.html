When is an A good evidence that a student understood?

Take a minute before anyone answers.

Some possibilities

  • The task made them apply the idea to something new.
  • They did the work on their own.
  • It was recent.
  • The task covered the whole unit.
  • Teachers agree on what an A looks like.
3:00 to 5:30 Give them real thinking time, then take three or four answers and type them in. Show the list afterwards. If someone says you can never be sure, stop there for a moment. No set of conditions makes it certain; they only make it good evidence.

When does an A not mean the student understood?

Click an answer to sort it. Click again to change it.

Now sort them. Does each one sink the claim, or does it just tell us when the claim holds?

Tells us when it holds

  • They memorised the answers, and a new question shows the gap.
  • They understood it on Tuesday and forgot it by June.
  • The quiz only covered part of the unit.
  • They're good at multiple choice, or at matching the rubric's wording.
  • An A from one teacher isn't the same as an A from another.

Close to sinking it

  • Someone else understood it. A tutor, a parent or AI did the work.
  • "Understand" can mean two things. They can follow the steps but can't explain why the steps work.
5:30 to 9:00 Take five or six answers and sort them together by clicking them before you show the two columns. The sorting is the part you want them to see. Keep asking where it breaks and stay away from what we should do about it, or it turns into a conversation about grading and AI.
"I've had students who understood it and still got a B."

Does that break our claim?

It breaks a different one: that every student who understands gets an A. It's worth checking exactly what a claim says before we try to break it.

Only use this if someone says it, which they usually do. If nobody does, skip past it.
What we started withA student who gets an A understands the material.
Where we ended upAn A on a task that made students apply the idea to something new, done on their own and recently, is good evidence that they understood it at that point.

So what does this tell us about knowing?

We can't see understanding directly. We infer it from what students do on a task, so the inference is only as good as the task.

9:00 to 10:30 The new claim is more accurate, and it's still a claim we can stand behind. We just know where its edges are now.

That was a TOK discussion

I only asked you three questions.

  1. When does the claim hold?
  2. When does it break?
  3. What does the break tell us about how we know?

Students have to do this in the exhibition and the essay. They take a claim and work out where it holds and where it doesn't. They'll get much better at it if they practise with evidence from your subjects as well as in my class.

10:30 to 12:00 Point out that this took about ten minutes and no preparation, and nobody needed any TOK training to do it.

What we're asking you to do

Choose one to three exhibition prompts for each unit.

One prompt as a thread

Pick one prompt and come back to it a few times during the unit.

Two or three prompts

Use each one once, at different points in the unit.

Start
Students give a first answer before the unit begins.
Middle
Something in the unit pushes back on that answer.
End
Ask what they'd say now and what changed their mind.

None of this needs much prep. A bell-ringer, a quick-write or a five-minute discussion like the one we just had is enough.

12:00 to 14:00 The middle visit matters most. If nothing in the unit could change a student's first answer, it's probably the wrong prompt for that unit.

Turning a prompt into a claim

Prompt 5What counts as good evidence for a claim?

The prompts are questions, and it's hard to find where a question breaks. It's easier if you turn the prompt into something your subject claims. For example:

Sciences
"A result that's been repeated is reliable."
English
"A quotation proves an interpretation."
History
"A primary source tells us what happened."
Global languages
"If native speakers say it, it's correct."
Human sciences
"A survey of 1,000 people tells us what people think."
Mathematics
"If it works for every case we've tried, it's true."
These are only examples. Departments should write their own, ideally a claim their students already believe.

Prompt 5 in your subject

Work with your department, using the front of the handout.

  1. Write one claim your subject makes about evidence.
  2. When does it hold?
  3. When does it break? Does each break just set a condition, or does it nearly sink the claim?
  4. Rewrite the claim so it includes where it holds.
  5. Which upcoming unit could you use it in, and what moment in that unit would break your students' first answer?

One caution

Different standards in different subjects doesn't mean anything goes. Each subject has a real standard for evidence; they just settle different kinds of questions. Students only notice that if we each say out loud what counts as evidence in our subject.
