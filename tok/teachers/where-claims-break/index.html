Thirty minutes. One claim you make every week. One question you can take back to any unit.

A claim we all make

A student who gets an A understands the material.

We make this claim every time we enter a grade. Every report card says it for us.

True or false? Thumbs up or thumbs down. Don't look around first.

Step 1 · Flip the question

When is an A good evidence that a student understood?

Think first. Don't raise your hand yet.

It holds when…

  • the task asked students to use the idea in a new situation, not repeat it
  • they did the work on their own
  • it was recent
  • the task covered the whole unit, not one corner of it
  • the standard for an A is shared across teachers
3:00–5:30 Wait time, then collect 3–4 answers live. Reveal the list after. If someone says "never definitively," stop on it: no conditions give certainty, only good evidence. That's a TOK point in itself.

Step 2 · The cut

When does an A not mean the student understood?

Click a break to sort it: shows a condition → nearly kills it → unsorted

Now sort them. Does this break kill the claim, or does it show us where the claim holds?

Shows a condition

  • Memorised the answers. A new question exposes the gap.
  • Understood it on Tuesday. Gone by June.
  • The quiz covered one corner of the unit.
  • Test-wise: good at eliminating options or matching rubric language.
  • An A from one teacher isn't an A from another.

Nearly kills it

  • Someone else's understanding. A tutor, a parent or AI did the work. Real evidence, wrong person.
  • "Understands" means two things. They can run the procedure but can't explain why it works.
5:30–9:00 Collect 5–6 breaks and sort them together by clicking the chips. Sorting is the move to model, so do it before revealing the two columns. Ask only "where does it break?", never "what should we do about it?", or this becomes a grading/AI complaint session.

Someone will say this

"I've had students who understood it and got a B."

Does that break our claim?

No. It breaks a different claim: every student who understands gets an A.

Before you can find where a claim breaks, be exact about what it claims.

Only show this if it comes up (it usually does). If nobody raises it, skip with the right arrow twice.

Step 3 · Refine the claim

Where we startedA student who gets an A understands the material.
Where we endedAn A on a task that required applying the idea in a new situation, done independently and recently, is good evidence that the student understood it at that point.

What the break tells us about knowing

We never observe understanding directly. We infer it from performance, so the inference is only as good as the task that produced it.

Every subject has its own version of this gap.

9:00–10:30 The refined claim isn't weaker; it's more accurate. The claim survived, with its boundary drawn.

Name the move

That was TOK.

I asked you three questions.

  1. When does the claim hold?
  2. When does it break?
  3. What does the break tell us about how we know?

This is exactly what students must do in the TOK exhibition and essay: test a claim, find where it holds and where it breaks. They need to practise it in your subject, with your evidence.

10:30–12:00 No TOK training needed for what you just did. It took ten minutes and no preparation.

What we're asking

One to three exhibition prompts per unit

Option A · a thread

One prompt, revisited across the unit.

Option B · stops along the way

Two or three prompts at different points in the unit.

Start · commit
Students give a first answer before the unit.
Middle · the break
Something in the unit breaks that first answer.
End · refine
"What would you say now, and what changed your mind?"

Low prep: a bell-ringer, a quick-write, or five minutes of discussion like the one we just had. Not a new lesson.

12:00–14:00 The middle visit matters most. If nothing in the unit can break the first answer, it's the wrong prompt for that unit.

The step that makes it work

You can't break a question. Turn the prompt into a claim.

Mathematics

These are examples, not answers. Departments should write their own claim, ideally one their students actually believe.
