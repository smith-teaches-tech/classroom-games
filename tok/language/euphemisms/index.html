The Decoder

Two releases written entirely in the language institutions use when they would rather you didn't picture the thing. Nothing in either one is false. That is the problem.

Work in pairs. One device between two is better than one each.
Follow the stages in order. Each one is timed, and each one is built on the one before it.
Write before you reveal. Every reveal on this page is locked until you have written your own version first.

Notebooks open before you start

Decoding happens on the screen — that is rough working and it disappears when you close the tab, which is fine. The takeaways go in your notebook, and that is the part you keep.

Head a page L2 · The Decoder and leave room for six numbered takeaways. The page tells you when each one is due.

1

Where we left Orwell

10 minutes · pairs

Take out the essay you annotated. You marked passages K where Orwell was making a claim about knowing, and T where he was complaining about ugly writing. You need the K ones now.

  1. Read each other your K passages. Where you marked different ones, say why.
  2. Between you, choose the single passage that makes the strongest claim about what language does to a reader.
  3. Turn it into a prediction, in your own words, and write it down:
    "If Orwell is right, then language of this kind should ______________ to a reader."
  4. Keep that sentence where you can see it. Everything after this stage is a test of it.

In your notebook — takeaway 1

Write your prediction out in full. You will come back to it twice today and again when you write the exit slip, so give it a line of its own.

Not answers — comparisons

"…should make a reader feel calm about something they would otherwise be angry about."

"…should stop a reader picturing anything at all."

"…should make a reader accept a judgement without noticing they were handed one."

These three are not the same claim. The second and third can be tested on a page of writing. The first is about how a reader feels, and feeling is not knowing. If your prediction is about feelings, add a second one that isn't.

2

Which one is false?

8 minutes · pairs

Two sentences. Same company, same decision, same 850 people. Decide together, commit, then reveal.

Sentence A

"Approximately 850 team members will be affected by this reduction in force."

Sentence B

"We are firing 850 people."

Commit to an answer first.

Answer

Neither. Both sentences are true. The same 850 people, the same decision, the same company. "Reduction in force" is the standard term for cutting headcount, and headcount is being cut. Nothing false has been said.

So if you were expecting to catch someone lying, that option has just closed. Something else is happening, and naming it is the rest of this lesson.

Now the same test on the second release.

Sentence A

"Our forces have maintained operational superiority while minimizing collateral damage."

Sentence B

"Our forces stayed in control while killing and wounding fewer civilians than they might have."

Commit to an answer first.

Answer

Neither, again. "Collateral damage" does mean dead and injured civilians and destroyed civilian property. The official sentence is not a lie. It is a true sentence about dead civilians that does not contain a dead civilian.

In your notebook — takeaway 2

Both sentences in each pair are true. Write one thing you can do with sentence B that you cannot do with sentence A.

Three answers that keep coming up

You can picture it. B puts a person in a room. A puts a category in a report.

You can count it. B tells you there are bodies to count. A does not tell you there is anything countable at all.

You can judge it. Once you can picture and count it, you can decide what you think about it. Until then there is nothing to have a view about.

In your notebook — takeaway 3

Copy the three questions below. You need them for the next forty minutes, and you need them again in the exit slip.

The three questions — use these for the rest of the lesson

  1. Is it false? Usually no. Check anyway.
  2. Can you picture what happened? An actual scene, with people in it.
  3. Could you go and count or check it? If a journalist wanted the number, do these words give it?

Hold on to this

A euphemism does not lie. It blurs. Question 1 lets it through, and questions 2 and 3 catch it.

3

One worked example

6 minutes · read together

Read this one all the way through before you start decoding. It is the pattern you will use nineteen more times.

The phrase

"involuntary career transition"

1. Is it false? No. These people's careers are changing, and it is not their choice. Every word of it is accurate. There is nothing here to fact-check.

2. Can you picture it? No. A "transition" is a movement from one state to another — you cannot see one. Compare fired: a meeting, a badge handed over, a box carried to a car. One of these has a picture in it and one does not.

3. Could you count or check it? Much harder than it looks. If a journalist wanted to know how many people were dismissed, "career transition" does not distinguish between people who were fired, people who left, people who retired, and people who moved departments. The number has not been hidden. The category that would let you ask for the number has been dissolved.

The finding

Nothing false was said, and a reader has still lost the picture and the count. That is what these phrases do, and it is why calling them lies gets it wrong.

6

Where is the line?

6 minutes · pairs

Everything so far has been true and blurred. These three may not be. There is no answer key for this stage.

"humanitarian intervention"  ·  "enhanced interrogation protocols"  ·  "police action"

Each of these does something the others in the release do not: it puts the event in a category. Not a blurrier word for the same act — a different kind of act altogether.

Take a position on each. Is it still a true description at a blurry level of detail? Or has it crossed over and become simply false?

Pick the one you feel most confident about and be ready to defend it against the pair next to you.

In your notebook — takeaway 4

Write the phrase you chose and your position on it, in a sentence you would be willing to defend out loud.

7

Why English is so good at this

10 minutes · pairs

Twelve words. For each one, decide whether it is a plain word or a grand word. Go on instinct — it takes about ninety seconds. Then check.

Sort all twelve first.

The pattern

The plain words are Old English. The grand words came in from Latin and French, most of them after 1066. English never chose between them — it kept both.

So the language now carries two words for almost everything: a short one you can see, and a long one you cannot. Kill and neutralize. Burn and incinerate. Death and casualty. Nobody has to invent a euphemism. The grand twin is already sitting there, and it is free.

"Bad writers, and especially scientific, political, and sociological writers, are nearly always haunted by the notion that Latin or Greek words are grander than Saxon ones."
— Orwell, under PRETENTIOUS DICTION

Go and look at that passage in your essay. Did you mark it T? Most people do. It reads like snobbery about word choice — a complaint about taste. It isn't. It is the mechanism for everything you decoded today, and Orwell filed it under a heading about style.

Before next class

Toki Pona has 137 words. No grand twins. No Latin layer. If you want to say something, there is one way to say it and everyone can see what you said.

8

Exit ticket

10 minutes · notebook, individually

In your notebook, under the four takeaways you already have. Your own words, no device, and leave the notebook open on your desk when you finish.

The question — takeaway 6

Nothing in Article A or Article B is false. Using two specimens from today, explain how the language still changes what a reader can know.

  • Quote your two specimens exactly.
  • Use at least two core concepts, and name them.
  • At least one of those concepts must not be power, culture, or perspective.
  • If you want to argue that one of your specimens is false, you may — but then you have to prove it.

Now flip both releases to Plain and read them end to end. Same events, twice. That is the last thing to look at before you write.

The TOK turn

This language doesn't decorate a reality everyone already sees — it decides which reality a reader can assemble. Whoever controls the words holds that power.
