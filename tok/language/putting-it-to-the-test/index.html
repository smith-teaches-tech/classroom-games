Putting a Claim to the Test

In the timed essay, most of you made a claim and then gave more evidence that agreed with it. That's support. It isn't a test. This page shows you the difference, walks you through a paragraph that would score 9 or 10, and then asks you to revise one of your own paragraphs.

Read it in order. Each section is short.
Try before you reveal. Where there's a button, decide your answer first.
Finish with section 6. That's the part you hand in.

01

What a test is

A test is the detail that makes your claim smaller.

If your claim comes out of the paragraph the same size it went in, you haven't tested it.

Think of a bridge. You don't prove a bridge is safe by walking across it once. You load it until something bends, and then you know exactly how much weight it holds.

Your claim is the bridge. The case is the weight.

02

You already did this in History

In The Village of Mysteries, the exit question asked: what would have to be found for you to give up your claim? That's a test.

Claim

The carved stone balls were used as rollers to move heavy stones.

Test

Experiments show the balls work as rollers. But working proves the use was possible, not that it happened, and the balls have no wear of any kind.

Verdict

We can know the balls could have been rollers. We can't know they were.

And "ritual"?

You couldn't test it, because nothing could ever prove it wrong. A claim that can't be tested explains everything, so it explains nothing.

03

Support or test?

Both of these come from your fake news essays. Which one is the test? Decide, then reveal.

Paragraph A

"Politicians used 'fake news' for real reporting they disagreed with, which made the public doubt it. This shows that control over language gives control over knowledge."

Paragraph B

"But a factual report doesn't become fake because someone calls it fake news. Once knowers notice that the label no longer separates true from false, they stop trusting the label, not the report. So the control lasts only while knowers don't question the label."

B is the test

A agrees with the claim. B finds the place where the claim stops working, and the claim comes out smaller: control lasts only while knowers don't question the label.

04

Three questions that find the test

Ask these about your case. One of them will usually find the detail you need.

1Who does my claim NOT work on?

Which knower sees through it?

2What detail in the case doesn't fit my claim?

Look for a date, a person who objected, or a decision that went the other way.

3What did the language change, and what did it NOT change?

The word, or the thing itself? What people believe, or what is true?

05

A 9 or 10 paragraph, step by step

To what extent does control over language give control over knowledge?

Case H · Partners or workers?

Uber called its drivers "partners." Its contracts said the drivers were self-employed and that "nothing shall create an employment relationship between Uber and the partner." Drivers took Uber to court, arguing they were "workers," which in UK law means a right to the minimum wage and paid holidays. In February 2021 the UK Supreme Court agreed with the drivers. It looked at how Uber actually controlled the work, such as setting fares and rating and removing drivers, not at the words in the contract.

The paragraph

Control over language gives control over knowledge when the people choosing the words also write the documents that others have to accept. Uber wrote the contracts, so it could call its drivers "partners" and state that "nothing shall create an employment relationship." A driver who signed was told, in Uber's own words, what the job was: a partnership, with no right to the minimum wage or paid holidays. As long as drivers and the public accepted the word, Uber controlled what they believed about the job. But the drivers didn't accept it. They took Uber to court, and the Supreme Court didn't decide what the drivers were by reading the word "partner." It looked at what Uber actually did: it set the fares, rated the drivers and removed them. The evidence about the work beat the word on the page. So Uber's control over language gave it control over knowledge only while nobody checked the word against the facts. Control over language can shape what people believe, but not what is true, and it lasts only until someone with the power to examine the evidence tests the word against the world.

1

The claim, with a "when"

It doesn't just say yes. It says when: when the people choosing the words also write the documents others have to accept.

2

The case, kept short

Three sentences show the claim working. No long retelling.

3

The test

Question 1 found it: who does the claim NOT work on? The drivers. Question 3 sharpened it: what did the word NOT change? What the job really was.

4

The verdict

The claim is rewritten after the test, and it's smaller and more accurate.

Claim before the test

Control over language gives control over knowledge.

Claim after the test

Control over language shapes belief, not truth, and only until someone checks it against the evidence.

Notice the core concepts

Power (who writes the contract), evidence (what Uber actually did), truth (what the job really was). They explain the case. They aren't decoration.

06

Revise your own paragraph

Hand this in

Take one body paragraph from your timed essay and make it a test.

1Copy one paragraph into a new document.

Highlight your claim.

2Reread your case and ask the three questions.

Your feedback comment names the detail you missed, so start there.

3Write two to four sentences that test your claim.

Start with the detail from the case, not with "Some people might argue."

4End with a verdict that rewrites your claim.

Is it smaller and more accurate than the claim you started with? If it's the same size, go back to step 2.

5Check your new sentences.

Do they agree with your claim? Then they're support, and you haven't tested it yet.

Hand in

Your revised paragraph, under 300 words, with your test sentences highlighted.
