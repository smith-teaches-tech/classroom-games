Now test your ruleIf your rule was "it uses words": what are the signs in sign language, if not words?

Many linguists use this test: can you use it to say something nobody has ever said before?

Sentence: yes. Sign language: yes, just as fully as speech. The bee: no, only direction and distance to food. The traffic light: no, three messages, forever. The emoji and the toilet sign are where people argue.

Push itThat is one group of experts' rule. Who gave them the right to decide what counts? And what happens when experts get it wrong?

Opening discussion · who decides what counts

In 1880, a meeting of teachers of deaf children voted on sign language.

The International Congress on the Education of the Deaf met in Milan. Almost all the voters were hearing teachers.

PredictWhat do you think they decided? What would that mean for deaf children in school?

They declared speech superior to signing. For decades, schools in many countries banned sign language and made deaf children learn to lip-read and speak.

Deaf communities kept signing anyway. In 1960, the linguist William Stokoe published research showing American Sign Language has its own full grammar. In 2010, the same Congress formally rejected the Milan decision.

DiscussDeaf people were producing a full language the whole time. So who produced the language, and who decided whether it counted?
What changed in 1960: the language, or what the experts knew about it?
What did it cost deaf children that their language didn't count as knowledge?

Opening discussion · who produces language

In 2023, Oxford chose "rizz" as its Word of the Year.

CommitWas "rizz" a real word before Oxford picked it? Yes or no, and why?
DiscussWho made the word? Who made it "count"?
Does a dictionary create words, or record words people already use?
If it only records them, why do people say "it's in the dictionary, so it's real"?

Language is produced by the people who use it. Whether it counts is often decided by someone with authority: a dictionary, an academy, a school, a congress of experts.

Today we take away the words completely: pictures, colours, gestures. The same two questions follow us. Who made the meaning? And who decides what it counts as?

Specimen 1 · the turn

Is the "Person" figure neutral, or does it take a side?

Argue it in pairs first. Then reveal.

The "Person" figure is identical to the "Men" figure. The default human wears trousers. The "Women" figure is that same body with a skirt added: a variation on a male starting point.

The sign claims to speak to everyone. To do that, it quietly decided who the ordinary person is. That hidden decision is a presupposition: an assumption built into the sign that nobody says out loud.

Push itWhose perspective got built into the "neutral" figure? Who would have to notice they'd been left out before anyone else could see it?

The instrument · four questions for any sign

You just did this in your head. Here are the steps.

How to use it: ask the questions in order. Questions 1–3 get one line each in your notebook. Spend most of your time on question 4 and the knowledge check. That is where the TOK is.

1
WHAT'S THE MESSAGE? What is the mark itself, and what is it supposed to tell you?Saussure: signifier (the mark) → signified (the idea)
  • What exactly do you see? Describe the shapes, colours or letters.
  • What are you meant to understand, or do, when you see it?
2
WHY THIS MARK? Is there any reason it had to be this mark?Saussure: arbitrary (a different mark could have done the job) · differential (it means by contrast)
  • Does the mark look like the thing it stands for? If not, how did you learn what it means?
  • Could a different mark have done the same job? Name one.
  • What is it next to? Would it mean the same thing on its own?
3
WHAT ELSE DOES IT SAY? What does it suggest beyond its message?Barthes: connotation · myth (a choice that looks like nature)
  • What does it suggest about people, places, or what counts as normal?
  • Does that feel like a choice someone made, or just "how things are"?
  • Who would be most likely to notice it? Who would never notice?
4
WHO DECIDED? Did an institution set this meaning (imposed), or did people using it drift into it (emergent)?Authority · power
  • Can you name a person, company or organisation that set this meaning?
  • If not, how did people come to agree on it?
  • What happens to someone who uses it "wrong"? Who corrects them?
?
KNOWLEDGE CHECK. Who would be misled by this sign?Which core concept is regulating our knowledge here?
  • What would they wrongly believe?
  • Why would they feel sure they had read it correctly?
  • What evidence would show them they had misread it?

Worked example · the instrument on the "Person" sign

Person / Men

1 · WHAT'S THE MESSAGE?
  • What exactly do you see?
  • What are you meant to understand?
— answer it first, then step —

See: a round head, a block body, two straight legs.

Understand: "a person", or on a toilet door, "men".

signifier → signified
2 · WHY THIS MARK?
  • Does it look like the thing?
  • Could another mark do the job?
  • Would it mean the same alone?
—

Does it look like a man? Not really. It has no face and no hands, and here in the Gulf many men wear a thobe, which is closer to the "Women" shape. So we don't recognise it because it looks like men. We learned it, the way we learned that the letters M-E-N spell a word.

Could another mark do the job? Yes: a different outline, a colour, the letter M. Any of them would work once people learned it.

Would it mean the same alone? No. Alone on an exit sign, this figure means "anyone". Put the skirted figure next to it, and the same figure now means "man". Its meaning comes from the contrast with the figure beside it.

arbitrary (another mark could have worked) · differential (it means by contrast)
3 · WHAT ELSE DOES IT SAY?
  • What does it suggest is normal?
  • Choice, or "how things are"?
  • Who would notice?
—

Suggests: the normal human is male, and a woman is a variation on him.

Choice or nature? It doesn't feel like a claim at all. It feels like "just what a person looks like". That is a choice passing itself off as nature.

Who notices? Mostly the people the default leaves out.

connotation · myth
4 · WHO DECIDED?
  • Can you name who?
  • How did everyone come to agree?
—

Who: designers working for institutions, such as the US Department of Transportation's symbol set (1974) and the international standard ISO 7001. So the meaning was imposed.

How it spread: airports and buildings everywhere copied the figures, and repetition made them feel natural.

authority: imposed
? · KNOWLEDGE CHECK
  • Who is misled, and into believing what?
  • Why do they feel sure?
  • What would show them?
—

Misled: almost everyone, in the same way. We read "person" and take in the idea that the default person is male, without any evidence for it.

Why sure: the sign looks self-explanatory, so nobody questions it.

What would show them: putting "Person" and "Men" side by side, as we did. The concept regulating our knowledge here is perspective: one perspective was built in and presented as no perspective at all.

Specimen 2 · block the easy answer

"So East and West just think in opposites."

It's the obvious conclusion. Argue whether it holds. Then reveal.

Hong Kong, a Chinese city, uses the Western code: green up, red down. So culture alone doesn't decide it.

If culture doesn't fix the meaning, what does? Which exchange, which trading software, which regulator adopted which code. Someone with authority fixed it.

We do · the instrument on stock-board colours

Answer each question as a class first. Then step to check.

1 · WHAT'S THE MESSAGE?
  • What exactly do you see?
  • What are you meant to understand?
— class answers first —

See: numbers in red or green, with an arrow.

Understand: the price rose, or the price fell.

2 · WHY THIS MARK?
  • Does it look like the thing?
  • Could another mark do the job?
  • Would it mean the same alone?
—

Does red look like "up" or "down"? No. A colour has no direction.

Could another mark do the job? Yes. Swap the two colours and the board works just as well. Shanghai does exactly that.

Would it mean the same alone? No. One red number tells you nothing until you know what colour the board uses for the other direction. Red means "down" only because green means "up" on the same board.

3 · WHAT ELSE DOES IT SAY?
  • What does it suggest?
  • Choice, or "how things are"?
—

Suggests: red brings other meanings with it: luck and celebration in China; danger and debt ("in the red") in Western accounting.

Choice or nature? Those extra meanings make each code feel natural to its own users. Hong Kong shows the feeling isn't what fixes it.

4 · WHO DECIDED?
  • Can you name who?
  • What happens if you use it "wrong"?
—

Who: exchanges, trading-software makers and regulators. The meaning is imposed.

Using it wrong: a trader can't choose their own colours on a shared board. Their screen follows the exchange's code.

? · KNOWLEDGE CHECK
  • Who is misled, and into believing what?
  • Why do they feel sure?
  • What would show them?
—

Misled: a New York trader glancing at a Shanghai board. He would believe the market had crashed on a day it actually rose.

Why sure: to him, red has always meant "down", so the board looks self-explanatory. He would feel certain, but feeling certain doesn't make a belief true.

What would show him: checking a single price against the day before. The numbers on the board were accurate; his interpretation of the colours was wrong.

The two cuts TOK keeps

If the same sign means opposite things and neither is wrong, where does the meaning live?

Part of the meaning is in the sign. Part is in the community reading it. The mark alone never carries it the whole way.

So a reader who doesn't know whose code is running will misread reliably, and won't know they're misreading. The sign looks self-explanatory the whole time.

FOR YOUR ESSAY TOOLKIT: two cuts to use on unseen cases.
① Was the meaning imposed by an authority, or did it drift in through use?
② Is the meaning in the sign itself, or in the community that reads it?

→ The arts. Some signs carry what no statement can: a colour, a chord, a title that renames the image beneath it. Meaning that can't be paraphrased without loss.
