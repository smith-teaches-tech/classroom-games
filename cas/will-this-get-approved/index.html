CAS · Experience Approval

Will this get approved?

Most proposals aren't rejected because the activity was bad. They're rejected because the proposal never said how the outcome would actually happen. Here is the gate, the standard, and fourteen real verdicts.

Use this before you submit: run your proposal through the five questions, apply the swap test to your outcome sentence, then check it against the model on this page.

00 / THE GATE

Five questions. Miss one and it comes back to you.

Your coordinator reads every proposal against the same five questions, in this order. If any one of them is missing, the proposal isn't approved — it's returned. Nothing is being judged about you; only about whether the paragraph in front of them answers these.

What?

A clear, specific description of the experience. What exactly will you do, with whom, where, how often?

Fails on: "I will volunteer at a charity." — Which charity? Doing what? How often?

Where?

Which strand — Creativity, Activity, Service? One or two. If you claim all three, you probably haven't thought hard about any of them.

Fails on: naming a strand the activity doesn't actually sit in, to make your record look balanced.

So what?

At least one of the seven learning outcomes — named by number and official wording, and explained. Not listed. Explained.

Fails on: "This will develop my collaboration skills." That's a label, not an explanation.

Why?

Personal significance. Why you, why now? Connect it to growth, a challenge you expect, an ethical question, an emotion, a past success or failure.

Fails on: "Because it will look good on my CAS record."

How?

A concrete reflection plan. What will you produce, when, and what will it be about? Journal, voice memo, photo log, blog, supervisor feedback — pick one and say when.

Fails on: "I will reflect at the end." Reflection at the end is a summary, not a reflection.

Scope

Long enough or deep enough that there is something to reflect on. A single hour rarely changes anyone. A CAS project must run at least one month.

Fails on: one-off attendance, or anything that is already your routine.

Approved

All five answered, scope holds, and at least one learning outcome is connected to the experience with a stated mechanism — the specific thing you will do that produces that outcome.

Not approved

Trivial or routine · no connection to an outcome · no opportunity for reflection or growth · passive · too short · already required of you for a DP grade.

The 150-hours myth There is no hours requirement in CAS. Nobody is counting. What is required is regular, sustained engagement across all three strands over 18 months, evidence, and reflection. Logging forty hours of something you'd do anyway proves less than eight weeks of something that was hard.
01 / THE SEVEN OUTCOMES

Use the official wording. Every time.

"Perseverance," "empathy," "planning," "teamwork" are not learning outcomes — they're paraphrases, and a proposal built on a paraphrase is hard to approve because nobody can tell which outcome you mean. There are seven. They have exact names. Tap any card for the IB descriptor and what proving it actually looks like.

02 / THE STANDARD

Naming the outcome is not claiming it

This is the single thing that separates an approved proposal from a returned one. You must say how — the specific mechanism by which doing this thing produces that outcome, and what it will look like when it has.

The swap test. Cover the name of your activity. Read your outcome sentence. If you could drop any other activity in the world into it and the sentence would still make sense, you haven't explained how — you've written a label. Rewrite until the sentence only works for your experience.

Label — fails the swap test

"Tutoring will help me develop collaboration skills and I will learn a lot from working with others."

Mechanism — passes

"LO5. The learning-centre coordinator sets the syllabus and I decide the method, so every session starts with a five-minute negotiation about what my student actually needs next. By week six I'll have a record of three times I gave up my plan for hers, and what happened."

Label — fails the swap test

"Training for the half-marathon will teach me perseverance and commitment."

Mechanism — passes

"LO4. Commitment isn't proved by race day — it's proved by the weeks I don't want to run. I'll log every session for sixteen weeks including the ones I miss, with the reason, so the evidence for LO4 is the missed-week entries and what I changed to get back on schedule."

Label — fails the swap test

"Volunteering at the centre will make me more aware of global issues and more empathetic."

Mechanism — passes

"LO6. By week eight I will be able to explain how the resettlement process works in this country, who decides, and where it stalls — and trace one specific policy to one specific consequence I watched a family live through. That written piece is my evidence."

Notice what the strong versions share: a named point in time, a thing that will exist because of the experience, and a claim specific enough to be wrong. If your outcome sentence couldn't turn out to be false, it isn't saying anything.
