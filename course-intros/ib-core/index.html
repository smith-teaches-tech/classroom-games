A closer look at the hardest, most interesting piece

What is TOK?

Theory of Knowledge asks one deceptively simple question:

“How do you know that’s true?”

Not what to think, but how we come to know things — and where that knowledge can be trusted, doubted, or misused. Let’s try two quick examples.

Presenter
This is the sell for TOK. It’s not more content to memorise — it’s a habit of asking for justification. The next two slides show it in action.
A knowledge claim
“There is intelligent life on other planets.”
How would you decide? What would count as evidence — and is the absence of it evidence of anything? TOK examines how we reason about what we can’t yet observe.
Presenter
The goal isn’t an answer — it’s noticing how we handle uncertainty and what standards of proof we demand. Let a couple of parents react.
A different kind of claim
“Climate is caused by human activity.”
Here we do have mountains of evidence. So TOK asks a different question: how does a whole community of experts build confidence, and why do people weigh the same evidence so differently?
Presenter
Contrast with the previous slide: one claim is short on evidence, one is rich in it. Same TOK move — interrogate how the knowing works. Keep it neutral; the point is method, not politics.
