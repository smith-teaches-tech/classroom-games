The Discussion

Why It Worked

You were not reading for meaning. You were reading for a hook — the words at the start of each sentence that reach back and grab the end of the one before it.

The hook and eye

Each sentence ends with a new action. The next sentence begins by repeating that action, then adds one more. Watch the seam.

The name for this is anadiplosis — ending one clause and beginning the next with the same word or idea. The whole story is a single unbroken chain of it, twenty-two links long.

Notice what it buys the writer: the reader never has to hold anything in memory. Every sentence hands you its successor. That is what a line of reasoning feels like from the inside.

The obvious objectionIf you wrote a body paragraph this way, it would sound like a picture book. Real repetition of exact words gets juvenile fast — your reader will hear the echo and stop trusting you.

So here is the real question. The hooks are what made the frog chain solvable. If a serious writer cannot repeat the same words over and over — how does the chain survive?

The Payoff

How He Did It

Every hook Machiavelli used, named. Not one of them is a repeated word — and yet the chain is as tight as the frog's.

Demonstrative Logical connector Synonym / restatement Time marker Frame reference

The two words he never stops turning over

The virtue that becomes a vice liberality → generosity → a generous man → works of ostentation → a name for generosity → this virtue of liberality → the more liberal man → acting liberally
The vice that becomes a virtue the opposite vice → labeled a miser → being known as a miser → his parsimony → in a stingy way → consider him a miser → one of the vices that enable him to reign

He never repeats a word — he keeps re-naming the same two ideas. Each re-naming is slightly different, and the difference is the argument: liberality curdles into ostentation; miser ripens into parsimony. By the final sentence the two words have swapped moral places entirely, and he never once said "as I mentioned earlier."

The shape of the whole thing

Eight sentences, then three, then two. The paragraphs get shorter as the argument tightens. Paragraph one builds the trap at length because a trap needs every step shown. Paragraph two reverses it in three moves. Paragraph three needs only a verdict.

That taper is itself a line of reasoning. A reader feels an argument closing.

Steal these five

1. Point backwards with a demonstrative. This step, that failure, a prince of this character. One word does what a whole clause of repetition would.

2. Re-name instead of repeat. Give the same idea a new noun each time — and make the new noun carry a judgment the old one didn't.

3. Let connectors do the reaching. Hence, Thus, For, Since all point back at the sentence before. If you can't place one, your sentences aren't actually connected.

4. Sequence out loud. "The first step…" then "Then…" A reader will follow a numbered road anywhere.

5. Close the exits last. Machiavelli finishes the disaster, then handles the objection. Answering "but couldn't he just…" is the final move, not a detour.

The passage, restored

Now write. In your own words, explain why liberality — usually a virtue — is a vice for a prince, and why stinginess — usually a vice — is a virtue. Begin: "Machiavelli believes that…"
