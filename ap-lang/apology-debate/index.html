AP Language · Day Two · 45 Minutes
Move the Situation
First · 5 minutes

Where the Room Stands

Five of the nine, hands up, one at a time. Your teacher or sub taps the counts in as they go. We are looking for the ones we disagree about — those are the ones worth arguing over.

The row highlighted in yellow is the most divided one in the room.

Second · 5 minutes · whole class

Warm-Up: One Change

Before you argue about tiers, one quick experiment. Nothing about the words below changes. Only the man saying them.

Third · 12 minutes to build, 10 to share

Build Both Cases

Your group gets one apology. You are not arguing for your own opinion. You are building the strongest possible case both ways — so that whatever anyone else says, you have already thought of it.

In your notebook, two headings.

Best case for EFFECTIVE. Two choices this speaker made that work. Quote both. For each, name who it works on and why it was available to this speaker.

Best case for FAILED. Two choices that fail. Quote both. Name the dodge.

Then, at the bottom: which case is stronger, and what would have to change about the situation for the other one to win? Hold on to that last sentence. You will need it in ten minutes.

The four up for debate

  • United Airlines — Oscar Munoz, letter to employees
  • John Crist — written statement to a Christian audience
  • Reese Witherspoon — statement and TV interview
  • Will Smith — Instagram, the morning after

Ninety seconds per group when you share: the strongest case each way, and which one you landed on.

Fourth · 10 minutes

Now Move the Situation

Open the card for your apology. Every word of the original stays exactly as it was written. One thing about the situation changes. Decide, with your group, whether your tier still holds — and be ready to name the exact choice that started working, or stopped.

You just moved rankings up and down without changing a single word.

So a tier is not a property of the text. It is a relationship between a text and its situation — the speaker, the audience, the offense, the place it was published, the moment it was said.

That is what the definition has been getting at the whole time. Available is not a fixed list of good writing moves. It is whatever this particular speaker, in this particular situation, in front of these particular people, can actually reach for and be believed.

✎ In your notebook
  1. Your apology, and the one thing that changed about its situation.
  2. Did your tier move? One sentence naming the exact choice that started working or stopped working — quote it.
  3. One sentence: finish this — “A choice is available to a speaker when…”
Last · 5 minutes

Thursday: You Fix One

Two classes telling me what is broken. Thursday you repair one of them, and then you explain your own choices the same way you have been explaining theirs.

What you will do Thursday. Choose one apology you put in Failed or Mixed. Rewrite it — in that person's voice, as if you were writing it for them. Then analyse two or three of your own choices using Choice, Unpacking, Effect on the Audience.

✎ Before you leave — write these two things
  1. Which apology you are rewriting. It has to be one you tiered Failed or Mixed.
  2. The one specific failure you are repairing. Not “make it better.” Quote the phrase that fails and name the dodge. For example: “I was disrespectful to the officer” — the Downgrade. She never names the actual offense.

Come Thursday with those two lines written and you spend the whole period writing instead of deciding.

AP Language · Mr. Smith · Substitute Notes

Move the Situation — 45 minutes

Students run the arguing. You run the clock and the poll. There is nothing to teach and nothing to collect — it all goes in notebooks.

They spent last class sorting nine celebrity apologies into Effective, Mixed and Failed. Today they argue about the ones they disagreed on, and then find out that the answer changes when the situation changes.

How the period runs

First five minutes, you run a poll from the front. The first tab lists five apologies. For each one, ask for hands — who put it in Effective, who in Mixed, who in Failed — and tap the numbers into the buttons on screen. The row that turns yellow is the one the class disagrees about most. It takes about a minute per apology, so keep it moving.

Next five minutes is a whole-class warm-up on the second tab. Read the card, give them a minute to talk to a partner, take a couple of answers, then press the reveal button. You don't need to add anything to it.

Then about twenty-two minutes of group work. Put them in groups of four and give each group one of the four apologies listed on the third tab — if the class is large, two groups can take the same one. Each group builds the best argument both ways, for and against, which means nobody has to be right and you never have to referee. Twelve minutes to prepare, then ninety seconds per group to share.

Last ten minutes or so, each group opens the card on the fourth tab for their own apology. It changes one thing about the situation — who was speaking, or where it was posted — and asks whether their ranking still holds. They discuss, press the reveal, and write three lines in their notebooks. If there is time, ask a couple of groups whether their ranking moved.

Final few minutes, the fifth tab. They write down which apology they are rewriting on Thursday and the one failure they are fixing. Both lines go in the notebook. That is the only thing that has to happen before they leave.

If they ask you something

If a group asks whether they have the right answer, tell them there isn't one and the reveal button will give them our reasoning to argue with. If a group finishes early, have them build the case for a second apology. If two groups disagree loudly, that is the lesson working.
