Fix the Apology
The Assignment

What You Are Doing Today

You have spent two classes explaining why other people's apologies fail. Today you repair one, and then you analyse your own choices exactly the way you have been analysing theirs.

Two pieces of writing

  1. The rewrite. Take one apology you put in Failed or Mixed and rewrite it. Not a note about how it should go — the actual apology, in that person's voice, ready for them to publish. It does not have to be long. It has to work.
  2. The analysis. One paragraph explaining two or three of the choices you made, using Choice → Unpacking → Effect on the Audience. This is the piece that is assessed.

The rule that decides everything: you are not making the apology nicer. You are making it work on that speaker's audience, using only what was actually available to that speaker. A rewrite that turns Ye into a press release has not fixed anything — it has just changed who is talking.

There Are Two Different “I”s

This is the one thing that goes wrong every year, and it is worth thirty seconds now to save you rewriting later. You are producing two pieces of writing, and the word I means a different person in each of them.

I
In the rewrite

“I” is the celebrity. You are ghostwriting. Every word is theirs, in their voice, as if they were about to post it.

“I asked that officer whether he knew who I was, and I meant it as a threat.”

I
In the analysis

“I” is you. You are explaining the choices you made and why. The celebrity is he or she — never “I”.

“I cut that line because her audience needed to see her as the person who created the danger.”

The mistake to avoid. Writing the new lines yourself and then giving the credit to the celebrity: “Through these lines, Reese is assuming full accountability…”

Reese did not choose those lines. You did. So say so: “I had her name the offence directly, so that her audience…” The analysis is about your decisions, not hers.

10 minutes · read all of it

A Worked Example

Reese Witherspoon. The failure being repaired is the one we close-read on Monday.

Step one · name the failure

What she wrote — and why it fails

“I was disrespectful to the officer who was just doing his job.”

The Downgrade. Everyone listening already knew she had asked, “Do you know my name?” She apologises for rudeness and never once for trying to use her fame to escape an arrest — which is the offence the audience was actually angry about.

Step two · the rewrite (I = Reese)

The replacement passage

I want to be specific about what I did, because everybody already knows it. When that officer was arresting my husband, I asked him whether he knew my name. I said it to make him back off. I was trying to use the fact that people recognise me to get out of something that anyone else would have been arrested for, and that is the part I am ashamed of. I was not simply rude to him. I tried to put myself above the law he was there to enforce.

Step three · unpack your own choices

ChoiceWhat did I actually do? Quote it. UnpackingWhat does that choice suggest, and why was it available to this speaker? Effect on the Audience & Link to PurposeWhat does it do to them, and how does it move toward forgiveness?
1 I had her quote her own worst line back: “I asked him whether he knew my name. I said it to make him back off.” The original works around the one sentence the entire country had already read. Repeating it in her own voice removes any suggestion that she is hoping it gets forgotten, and commits her to the exact wording rather than a summary of it. Her audience already has that sentence in their heads, so an apology that leaves it out invites them to notice the gap. Volunteering the most damaging fact instead of managing it makes everything after it believable, and belief is the thing she needs before forgiveness is possible.
2 I replaced “disrespectful” with “I tried to put myself above the law.” Disrespectful describes a tone. Above the law describes an act. Swapping the small adjacent offence for the real one shows she understands the accusation rather than a softened version of it. What her audience resented was not rudeness — it was the idea that fame buys exemption. Naming that directly answers the grievance they actually had, instead of apologising for something nobody was angry about.
3 I cut “I was frightened for my husband.” Offered as context, it functions as an excuse, and it arrives before she has named what she did. It quietly casts Reese as a person in danger. Removing it keeps her in the position of the person who caused the problem rather than someone it happened to, so the audience is never asked to feel sorry for her in the middle of an apology.

Step four · turn the table into a paragraph (I = you)

The analysis — this is what gets assessed

Reese apologises for being “disrespectful to the officer,” which downgrades what she did, since everyone listening already knew she had asked, “Do you know my name?” So I had her quote that line back herself: “I asked him whether he knew my name. I said it to make him back off.” I chose to make her repeat the most damaging sentence in the story because her audience already had it in their heads, and an apology that avoids the thing everyone is thinking about invites them to notice the omission; volunteering it instead of managing it makes everything after it believable. I also replaced “disrespectful” with “I tried to put myself above the law,” because “disrespectful” describes a tone while the real offence was an act. Her audience — ordinary people who could never talk their way out of an arrest — resented exactly that, so naming it is the only choice that answers the grievance they actually had. Both of these are available to her because she is famous: the offence was an abuse of fame, so only naming it plainly can settle the account.

Notice what that paragraph never does. It never says the original was badly written. It never says the new one sounds more sincere. Every sentence names a choice, says what the choice does, and ties it to a specific audience with a specific reason to be angry.

✎ Answer these three first — they decide everything else
  1. Who has to forgive this person? Be specific. Not “the public.” Which people, and what do they already know?
  2. What do they already believe about this speaker before a word is said? That is what you are working with or against.
  3. What is available to this speaker that is not available to anyone else — and what is closed off to them because of who they are?

A sentence frame, if you need one to start.

I [choice] because [speaker]'s audience [already knows / needs / believes X], which [effect], and that moves them toward forgiving [him / her].

Use it to get going, then write your way out of it. Three sentences in a row with the same shape will read as a form.
