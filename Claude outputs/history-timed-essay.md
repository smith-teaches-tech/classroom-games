# History Unit — Timed Essay (design + paste-ready text)

Design decisions (Michael, 2026-10-05):
- Format: a timed essay that is "the History half of a TOK essay". It replaces the short-answer + transfer test in the unit plan.
- Titles: three titles in the style of the prescribed titles are released one week ahead. Students unpack their chosen title into their own knowledge question at home, the way they will in January.
- Cases: each section gets its own set of SIX unseen cases (A day: Set A; B day: Set B), two of each of three published types. The case types are released with the titles, so nothing useful can leak between the A-day and B-day sections.
- Prep sheet: students bring one handwritten A4 side, made at home, with AI allowed while preparing. It is stapled to the essay and handed in. Copy rule: any sentence that appears word for word on both the sheet and the essay earns nothing.
- On the day: typed in class, 500–600 words, 60 minutes (suggest 10 minutes reading and planning). No notebook; the prep sheet is the only aid.
- Taught in November; the real TOK essay starts in January. All three titles are written for this course, not taken from IB lists, so no model essays for them exist online.

Rubric: holistic, out of 10, the same instrument as the Language timed essay with one added line for unpacking the title.

====================
STUDENT SHEET (release one week ahead)
====================

TOK History: Timed Essay
The History section of a TOK essay. 500–600 words, typed in class, 60 minutes.

What happens on the day
1. Choose one of the three titles below.
2. You will get a sheet of six short cases you have not seen before. You may only use these cases. Each paragraph uses one case as its example. You can use the same case in both paragraphs, seen from two different perspectives, or a different case in each paragraph.
3. You may use your prep sheet. Nothing else. No devices except your Google Doc.

The three titles
1. Historians never have the past itself, only what it happened to leave behind. To what extent does this limit what historians can know?
2. "When many sources tell the same story, we should believe it." Discuss with reference to history.
3. "The account of the past that survives is the account that powerful people wanted to survive." To what extent do you agree?

The three kinds of case
Your case sheet will have two cases of each kind. You do not know which cases.
- Testimony meets a new trace: people said something happened, and later a new kind of evidence was found.
- A document that fooled the experts: a document was accepted as genuine, then exposed.
- Who controlled the story: someone with power tried to decide what people would know about an event.

What your essay must do
Opening (two or three sentences)
- Your knowledge question: turn the title into a question about knowledge. Name the knowledge concepts it is really asking about.
- Your answer, qualified: say when your answer is true.

Paragraph 1: Claim
- Claim, qualified. Answer your knowledge question and say WHEN it is true.
- The case. Show how your claim works in the case. Keep the retelling short.
- The test. Find the detail in the case that pushes back on your claim. Where does your claim stop working? Name the core concept that explains why.
- Verdict. How much of your claim is still standing after the test?

Paragraph 2: Counterclaim
- The same four steps, from a second perspective. A second perspective is not just "the opposite." It is a different cut: a different condition under which the answer changes.

Closing (one or two sentences)
- Answer the title by saying where the line falls.

The cuts: use these to qualify your claims
- Is this a trace of the event, or a story about the event? (L1: Village of Mysteries)
- Do these sources agree because they are independent, or because they all go back to one source? (L2: Repetition or Convergence?)
- What did the writer's position let them count as evidence, and what did it hide? (L3: Two Accounts of Vodou)
- Is this good evidence for THIS question? The same source can be strong for one question and useless for another. (L4: When Witnesses Disagree)
- Was the account believed because it was right, or because of who said it? (L4: the overrule)
- Is this beyond our reach "not yet" (new evidence could settle it) or "never"? (L2, L4, L5)
- Is the question underdetermined (the evidence fits more than one answer) or unknowable? (L1, L6)
- Is the accepted account the earliest, the best supported, or the one that had a job to do? (L6: Whose Narrative Survives?)
- Does the summary say more than the evidence does? (L3, L4, L6)

Core concepts: evidence, certainty, truth, interpretation, power, justification, explanation, objectivity, perspective, culture, values, responsibility

Your prep sheet
- One side of A4, handwritten, made at home. You may use any help you like to prepare it, including AI.
- Bring it on the day. It is stapled to your essay and handed in.
- The copy rule: if a sentence appears word for word on your prep sheet and in your essay, that sentence earns nothing. Your sheet should hold tools, not paragraphs.

How to prepare
- For each title, write the knowledge question you think it is really asking. Which two cuts would give you two different perspectives on it?
- For each kind of case, practise on a case from the unit. "Testimony meets a new trace": the Titanic and the 1985 wreck. "A document that fooled the experts": the four front pages from one wire report. "Who controlled the story": Thanksgiving, or the 70,000.
- Practise finding the test: take a claim, then find the detail in the unit case that pushes back on it.
- Do not write paragraphs. The case on the day will be new, and a paragraph written for a different case will not fit it.

SET A CASE SHEET (A day — do not release early)
====================

Testimony meets a new trace

Case A: Vinland
Two Icelandic sagas describe Norse voyages, around the year 1000, to a land west of Greenland they called Vinland, where grapes grew. The sagas were oral histories, written down centuries later, and many scholars treated them as legend. Indigenous peoples already lived in that region. In 1960 Helge Ingstad, guided by local people, found grass-covered mounds at L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland, Canada. Digs led by the archaeologist Anne Stine Ingstad (1961–68) found Norse-style buildings, iron boat rivets and a bronze cloak pin. In 2021 scientists dated wood cut with metal tools to the year 1021: "the only secure calendar date for the presence of Europeans across the Atlantic before the voyages of Columbus." But grapes have never grown at L'Anse aux Meadows. Butternuts found there grow only much further south. Whether this site is the sagas' Vinland is still debated.

Case B: Madison Hemings
In 1873 Madison Hemings, who was born enslaved on Thomas Jefferson's plantation, told a newspaper that Jefferson was his father. He said Jefferson also fathered the other children of his mother, Sally Hemings, a woman Jefferson enslaved. For over a century most Jefferson historians rejected the account. In 1996 one wrote that among specialists "there seems to be a clear consensus that the story is almost certainly not true." In 1998 a DNA study found that a descendant of Eston Hemings, Sally's youngest son, carried the Jefferson family's male-line DNA. The test could not show which Jefferson man was the father, and Madison's own family line could not be tested. In 2000 the Thomas Jefferson Foundation concluded that Thomas Jefferson was very probably the father. In 2001 a separate group of scholars disagreed. A new DNA study announced in 2026 has not yet been checked by other scientists.

A document that fooled the experts

Case C: The Donation of Constantine
A document claimed that the Roman emperor Constantine, in the 4th century, gave Pope Sylvester I power over Rome, Italy and the western provinces of the empire. Popes cited it for centuries, starting with Leo IX in 1054. In 1440 the scholar Lorenzo Valla argued that it was a forgery, using its language: it contained words no Roman of Constantine's time would use, such as "satrap," a Persian title. "Who ever heard of satraps being named in Roman councils?" he asked. Valla worked for King Alfonso of Aragon, who was fighting the Pope over land in Italy. Nicholas of Cusa had raised similar doubts about seven years earlier. Valla's work was not printed until 1517, and the forgery was not almost universally accepted as one until around 1600. Historians now think it was written in the 8th century.

Case D: The Vinland Map
In 1965, the day before Columbus Day, Yale University announced a map said to date from about 1440. It showed "Vinland" west of Greenland, which would make it the earliest map to show part of America. A donor had bought it for Yale. In 1972–74 a laboratory found a form of titanium dioxide in the ink that was only made from the 1920s. In the 1980s another laboratory found only traces of titanium, and some experts argued again that the map was genuine. The parchment itself was dated to 1423–1445. In 2021 Yale announced new analysis: "a titanium compound used in inks first produced in the 1920s pervades the map's lines and text." Its curator said: "The Vinland Map is a fake." No one knows who made it.

Who controlled the story

Case E: Great Zimbabwe
Great Zimbabwe is a stone city in southern Africa built between the 11th and 15th centuries. In 1871 the German explorer Karl Mauch decided Africans could not have built it and linked it to the Queen of Sheba. Later diggers, one of them backed by Cecil Rhodes, damaged the layers of soil that could have dated the site. In 1905 the archaeologist David Randall-MacIver concluded that the ancestors of the Shona people built it. In 1929 Gertrude Caton-Thompson agreed: there was "not one single item that is not in accordance with the claim of Bantu origin and medieval date." In the 1960s and 1970s the white-minority government of Rhodesia still promoted the claim that non-Africans built it. An archaeologist working at the site said he was told "the government was pressurising them to withhold the correct information." In 1980 the newly independent country named itself Zimbabwe, after the site.

Case F: The Gulf of Tonkin
On 2 August 1964 North Vietnamese boats attacked the US destroyer Maddox. On 4 August US ships reported a second attack. That night the Maddox's commander, Captain John Herrick, cabled: "Review of action makes many reported contacts and torpedoes fired appear doubtful. Freak weather effects on radar and overeager sonarmen may have accounted for many reports." Three days later the US Congress gave the President broad power to use military force in Southeast Asia, by 416 votes to 0 in the House and 88 to 2 in the Senate. In 2005 the National Security Agency released a study by its own historian, Robert Hanyok, written in 2001. It found that no attack happened on 4 August: "The overwhelming body of reports, if used, would have told the story that no attack occurred."

SET B CASE SHEET (B day — do not release early)
====================

Testimony meets a new trace

Case G: Tulsa, 1921
On 31 May and 1 June 1921, white mobs attacked Greenwood, the Black district of Tulsa, Oklahoma, and destroyed more than a thousand homes and businesses. The official death count was 36. Survivors and their families said many more had died and been buried secretly in unmarked graves. For decades "the violence became something of a taboo subject, particularly in Tulsa." A state commission searched in the late 1990s and found no clear mass grave. Historians' "credible estimates of deaths range from fifty to three hundred." In October 2020 archaeologists at Oaklawn Cemetery found a mass grave with coffins, and more than 120 graves have been found since. By 2024 one person, C.L. Daniel, a First World War veteran, had been identified as a victim, using DNA and a 1936 letter from his mother in government files.

Case H: The date of Pompeii
For centuries the eruption that buried Pompeii was dated to 24 August AD 79. That is the date in almost all copies of a letter by Pliny the Younger, who watched the eruption from across the bay. His original letter is lost. The earliest copies were made in the 9th century, and a few later copies give other dates. Excavators found autumn fruit such as pomegranates and chestnuts, and charcoal heaters. In 2018 archaeologists found a charcoal inscription on a wall giving the date 17 October, with no year. The site's director said charcoal "does not last long," so the eruption must have come in October. In 2023 archaeologists made test marks in charcoal on the same wall; ten months later they were still "perfectly legible." In 2024 the next director backed 24 August again, calling the other dates "modern conjecture."

A document that fooled the experts

Case I: The Hitler Diaries
In April 1983 the German magazine Stern announced that it had found Adolf Hitler's secret diaries: 60 volumes, for which it had paid 9.3 million marks. Stern decided that no forensic scientist or historian would examine them until it had every volume. The British historian Hugh Trevor-Roper was told, falsely, that the paper had been tested and was from before the war. He wrote: "I am now satisfied that the documents are authentic." Handwriting experts compared the diaries with samples of Hitler's handwriting, but some of those samples had been forged by the same man. On 6 May, two weeks after the announcement, the German Federal Archives reported that the paper contained a whitening chemical and the bindings contained polyester, both made after the war. The forger, Konrad Kujau, had put the initials "FH" on one cover instead of "AH."

Case J: The Zinoviev Letter
Four days before the British general election of October 1924, the Daily Mail published a letter that seemed to come from Grigory Zinoviev, head of the Soviet-run Communist International. It urged British communists to prepare for revolution. The headline read: "Civil war plot by socialists' masters." The governing Labour Party lost the election: it lost 40 seats, although its total vote went up. One route by which the letter reached the press ran through a former intelligence officer with links to the Conservative Party. In 1999 a Foreign Office historian, Gill Bennett, concluded that the letter was a forgery, probably from anti-communist Russians, and not likely to be the direct work of British intelligence officers. She later wrote: "The important point was not the authenticity of the document but the use to which the document was put."

Who controlled the story

Case K: Katyn
In spring 1940 the Soviet secret police shot about 22,000 Polish prisoners: army officers, police and other educated Poles. In April 1943 Nazi Germany announced that it had found mass graves in Katyn forest and blamed the Soviet Union. In 1944 a Soviet commission blamed the Germans. Britain and the United States, allied with the Soviet Union, did not publicly challenge the Soviet account, though in 1943 a British ambassador wrote privately that the evidence threw "serious doubt on Russian disclaimers of responsibility." In 1946 the Soviets had Katyn charged against the Germans at the Nuremberg trials; the final judgment did not mention it. In April 1990 the Soviet Union admitted that its secret police were responsible. In 1992 Russia gave Poland the 1940 order to shoot the prisoners, signed by Stalin.

Case L: The Congo files
From 1885 to 1908 the Congo Free State was the personal property of King Leopold II of Belgium. His regime forced people to collect rubber, using killings, mutilation and hostage-taking. In 1908 he handed the Congo over to the Belgian state. Before he did, he had a large part of the state's records destroyed. The historian Adam Hochschild writes that furnaces in Brussels burned for days, and that, according to his aide, Leopold said they had no right to know what he did in the Congo. Records from before 1906 are still scarce. But the story was not lost. In 1904 a British official, Roger Casement, published a report on what he saw travelling in the Congo. Missionaries, including Alice Seeley Harris, took photographs and showed them to audiences in Europe and America. Historians still disagree about how many people died.

TEACHER MAP ("Test" = the detail inside the case that pushes back on the obvious claim)
====================

Which title each case serves best: T1 traces, T2 agreement, T3 power. Every case can carry all three.

SET A

Case A (Vinland). T1: the trace confirmed the testimony, so it is not true that historians "only have what was left behind." Test: the trace confirms the general claim (the Norse crossed) but not the specific one (that this is Vinland, with grapes). The new trace reaches part of the testimony and no further: a "not yet" wall partly fell. T2: two sagas agree, but they are oral histories written down later. Are they independent? T3: whose dismissal kept the sagas as legend? Watch for: "the sagas were proved true"; "this IS Vinland"; "the Norse arrived in 1021" (they were there in 1021).

Case B (Hemings). T3 is the strongest: whose testimony counted. An enslaved man's account was dismissed by a "clear consensus" of specialists; being believed is not the same as being right (L4). Test: the 1998 DNA showed a Jefferson male, not Thomas, and tested Eston's line, not Madison's. The conclusion rests on DNA plus documents plus the testimony together: no single trace settled it. The 2026 study is not peer reviewed yet, a live "not yet." T2: the consensus was many historians agreeing. Were they independent? Sensitivity: an enslaved teenager and the man who enslaved her. Keep to evidence and testimony; never "affair." Do not let students call it settled by DNA alone.

Case C (Donation). T2: popes cited it for centuries, which is repetition, not convergence. T3: power kept it alive. Test: the debunker was also paid by power (Alfonso against the Pope), yet his method (anachronistic language) was sound. A motivated source can still be right; the motive does not decide the question. Also: Cusa got there first, and acceptance took a further 150 years. Watch for: "Valla single-handedly exposed it"; "Valla dated it to the 8th century" (later scholarship did). Valla's line is a translation of his Latin. Frame as source criticism, not a judgment on religion.

Case D (Vinland Map). T2: experts agreed, then disagreed, then agreed again. Test: the scientific tests themselves conflicted for decades, so "science settled it" is too fast; the parchment really is medieval. Old material, new ink: good evidence for which question (L4)? T3: Yale had an interest in the map being real. Watch for: "the map is fake, so the Vikings never reached America" (Case A is genuine). Note: Cases A and D both concern Vinland; a strong essay could use them as a pair.

Case E (Great Zimbabwe). T3 is the obvious one. Test: the power failed. Archaeologists settled the question in 1905 and 1929, and the censorship came after it was settled. Power could delay and suppress, but not change the traces; and the new nation named itself after the site. Also: the colonial diggers destroyed the evidence that could have settled it sooner (T1: what survives depends on who dug). Sensitivity: the colonial theories are racist; treat them as the object of analysis. The archaeologist's quote is Paul Sinclair, quoted in Frederikse, None But Ourselves (1982).

Case F (Tonkin). T2: many reports, one conclusion. Test: the doubt was in the record from the first night (Herrick's own cable), and the truth was recovered by the same agency's own historian. T3: power acted on the account before it could be checked. "Not yet" here was a matter of declassification, not new evidence. Also: the 2 August attack was real, so "it was all made up" fails. Watch for: claims that Johnson deliberately invented it (Hanyok's finding is mishandled and skewed intelligence). Keep off US politics and the Vietnam War itself.

SET B

Case G (Tulsa). T3: an official count of 36 against survivors' testimony; a "taboo." T1: the traces were underground. Test: the 1990s search found nothing, so absence of evidence was not evidence of absence. But the graves found since have so far confirmed one identified victim: the testimony is partly confirmed, and the toll is still underdetermined. Watch for: "proved 300 dead"; "the commission found the grave" (2020). Sensitivity: racial violence; no images; keep the trigger incident out.

Case H (Pompeii). The reversal case, and the hardest. T1: the testimony is a copy of a copy. Test: the new trace (charcoal) may not date the eruption at all; it could be from the year before. A new trace can be over-read, just as testimony can. T2: "almost all copies agree," but are copies independent? (L2). Watch for: "now known to be October." Thinnest for T3; weaker students may struggle to use it for power.

Case I (Hitler Diaries). T2: the handwriting matched, but the comparison samples were forged by the same man. Agreement with a corrupted reference is not convergence (L2). T3: commercial secrecy blocked checking. Test: the expert was not a fool. He was given false information, and the method itself was reasonable. A reasonable rule can produce a false conclusion (L4). And experts exposed it within two weeks. Keep to verification. No imagery, no content of the diaries.

Case J (Zinoviev). T3 seems obvious: a forgery swung an election. Test: Labour's vote rose, and the official historian found no institutional conspiracy. Bennett's own line, that what mattered was the use, not the authenticity, moves the question from "true or false" to "what job did it do" (L6). Also: the official history was written by the Foreign Office about its own agencies. Who checks the checkers? Watch for: "MI6 forged it"; "the letter cost Labour the election."

Case K (Katyn). T3 runs in an unexpected direction: the Nazis told the truth, for propaganda, and the democracies sat on it to protect an alliance. A source with a motive to lie can still be right (compare Case C). Test: the Soviet attempt to fix the story at Nuremberg failed in court, and the 1990 admission still controlled the story (it blamed only two officials). T1: the "not yet" wall fell to archive access, not new digging. Watch for: "all were officers"; "all were killed at Katyn forest." Some Russian state media again dispute Soviet guilt; students may meet this online.

Case L (Congo). T3 and T1 together: power destroyed the archive. Test: the story survived anyway, through other traces (a consul's report, missionary photographs, testimony). Power controlled the official record but not every trace. T2: the death toll is still disputed among historians. Attribution: the burning and Leopold's remark come from Hochschild, citing the aide Gustave Stinglhamber. Keep the hedge "according to his aide," and check the wording against a print copy before quoting directly. One reference source dates the main destruction to 1906, not 1908; the case text avoids giving the year of the burning. Sensitivity: atrocity photographs, so describe them, don't show them.

Built-in blockers: A blocks "the sagas were proved true"; B blocks "DNA settled it"; C blocks "a biased source must be wrong"; D blocks "science settled it at once"; E blocks "power decides the story"; F blocks "it was all invented"; G blocks "no grave found means no grave"; H blocks "physical traces beat testimony"; I blocks "the experts were incompetent"; J blocks "the forgery won the election"; K blocks "the side with the motive to lie was lying"; L blocks "destroy the archive and you erase the past."

====================
RUBRIC (holistic, modelled on the IB TOK essay assessment instrument, marked out of 10)
====================

Assessment question
Does the student provide a clear, coherent and critical exploration of the title, unpacking it into a knowledge question and testing a claim and a counterclaim against the cases?

What the marker looks for
- Unpacking. The opening turns the title into a knowledge question and gives a qualified answer.
- Qualified claims. Each claim says when it is true. The claim and counterclaim rest on different cuts.
- The test. Each claim is pushed against a detail in the case until it bends: where does it hold, and where does it stop?
- Use of the case. The case is evidence for the argument, not a story to retell.
- Core concepts. Concepts explain how historical knowledge is working in the case; they are not decoration.
- Verdict. The essay answers the title by saying where the line falls.

Marking is holistic. Read the whole essay, choose the band that best describes it overall, then choose the mark within that band. Apply the copy rule before marking: sentences copied word for word from the prep sheet earn nothing.

9–10 Excellent
- The title is unpacked into a clear knowledge question, with a qualified answer.
- Claim and counterclaim are both qualified: each says when it is true, and they rest on different cuts.
- Each is tested against a specific detail in the case. The essay shows exactly where each holds and where it stops.
- The case is used as evidence, not retold.
- Core concepts explain how historical knowledge is working in the case.
- The verdicts follow from the tests, and the closing says where the line falls.
Possible characteristics: Insightful, Convincing, Accomplished, Lucid

7–8 Good
- The title is unpacked, though the knowledge question may stay close to the title's wording.
- Both claims are qualified, though one qualification may be vague, or the counterclaim is mostly the opposite of the claim.
- There is a real test in both paragraphs. The verdicts are stated but not fully earned by the tests.
- The case is relevant and explained, with some retelling.
- Core concepts are used accurately and mostly do real work.
Possible characteristics: Pertinent, Relevant, Analytical, Organised

5–6 Satisfactory
- The essay answers the title as written, without unpacking it. Claims are mostly unqualified (a flat yes or no), or the discussion drifts toward the history in the case.
- A test is attempted in one paragraph only, or the "test" just gives more support for the claim.
- The case is mostly summarised, or appears in only one paragraph.
- Core concepts are named but decorative.
Possible characteristics: Acceptable, Mainstream, Adequate, Competent

3–4 Basic
- Connected to the title, but the claims are about the events in the case, not about knowledge. The essay is largely descriptive.
- Claims are stated and illustrated, not tested.
- The case is barely used or is misread.
- Core concepts are missing or misused.
Possible characteristics: Underdeveloped, Basic, Superficial, Limited

1–2 Rudimentary
- Weakly connected to the title. Points are descriptive or unsupported assertions.
- No case is used, or there is no counterclaim.
Possible characteristics: Ineffective, Descriptive, Incoherent, Formless

0
- The essay does not reach the standard described above, does not respond to one of the three titles, or does not use a case from the case sheet.

The key line: between 4 and 5
Did the student test the claim against a detail in the case, or argue about the history (who was guilty, who was right, how terrible it was)? An essay that never tests a claim cannot score above 4, however well it is written.
