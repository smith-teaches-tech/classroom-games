UNIT-PLAN ROW
====================

Lesson cell:
L6: The Proof That Stalls
When can you know a maths claim is true by checking it yourself, and when do you have to trust other people?
(Teacher version: If a proof forces everyone to agree, why does a community still have to accept it?)

Framework & Concepts cell:
certainty, justification, power · Grid focus: V · Maths (how a claim moves from asserted to accepted) and C · Maths (who certifies, and what). Pays off the L3 forward link: certification is a social act that can fail to complete.

Activity cell:
The Proof That Stalls (interactive, 17 screens). Commit before every reveal.
1. Warm-up (5 min): evidence or proof? Fifty failed tries is evidence, not proof. Callback to the 2 = 1 proof from L5: checking a proof means checking that every step is allowed.
2. The broken chessboard (15 min): students try to cover a board with two opposite corners removed using 31 dominoes, on an uncoloured on-screen board. Nobody can. Commit: does failing prove it can't be done? Then the colours are turned on, and the six-step proof rules out every layout at once. Notebook 1: whose word did you need to be certain? (Nobody's.)
3. Euclid (8 min): the primes go on forever. A calculator multiplies chosen primes and adds 1. With all six primes, 30,031 = 59 × 509: the pattern fails but the proof holds. An infinite claim with a five-step proof, so size isn't the problem. What matters is whether you can follow every step.
4. Colour the map (12 min): on-screen maps (warm-up, Four Corners, Nevada, Benelux), then on paper: draw a map that needs five colours. Nobody can. Commit: does that prove four is always enough? This time there's no short proof to show them.
5. The Four Colour story (12 min): 1852 Guthrie; 1879 Kempe's proof accepted; 1890 Heawood finds the flaw 11 years later and proves five colours are enough; 1976 Appel and Haken, computer checks of nearly 2,000 patterns, over 1,000 hours. Commit: is it proven? 2005 Gonthier: the Coq proof checker re-checks it. Notebook 2: when you can't check a proof yourself, what are you really trusting?
6. abc (15 min): 2012 Mochizuki; 2018 Scholze and Stix report a gap at Corollary 3.12; 2021 published in PRIMS (Mochizuki chief editor, recused); 2026 Project LANA narrows the dispute to one point. Commit: is abc known to be true? Who decides? A proof only forces agreement on people who can follow it.
7. Fill V · Maths and C · Maths (5 min), plus the cut: proof ≠ acceptance (callback to L1, being art ≠ being good).
8. The objection (8 min): so maths is just opinion? My Bed (L3) vs abc, compared. Same structure ≠ same validity. Notebook 3.
Exit ticket.

Assessment / Notes cell:
Exit ticket (3 to 4 sentences, using certainty and justification): The chessboard proof needed no one to trust. The abc proof needs a community. So where does certainty in maths come from: the proof, the people, or both?
A/B: certainty sits in a proof that forces anyone who can check it; the community certifies correctness, and that certification can stall over access (abc) or be wrong for a while (Kempe); proof ≠ acceptance.
C/D: "maths is certain, full stop" · "maths is just opinion too" · "Mochizuki cheated" · retells the four-colour story with no claim about knowledge.
Guards: no abc maths, ever. Keep the recusal in (prevents the corruption drift). Only the chessboard and Euclid proofs are worked through; the four-colour proof is never explained. "Complex proofs are less certain" is the wrong takeaway: the certainty doesn't shrink, it moves from the individual to the community.
Materials: paper and coloured pencils for the five-colour challenge. Not yet class-tested.

====================
TEACHER PRIMER: WHAT A PROOF IS (for review before teaching)
====================

A proof is a chain of steps where each step is forced by the one before. It starts from things everyone already accepts (definitions and basic rules), and if every step is allowed, the conclusion can't be false.

Evidence vs. proof: evidence is examples ("I tried 50 times"), which makes a claim likely. A proof is a reason that covers every case, including cases nobody tried.

Checking a proof means checking that each step is allowed, not testing examples. That's the 2 = 1 proof in L5: line 5 divides by zero, and one illegal step breaks the whole chain.

Why size isn't the issue: Euclid's proof covers infinitely many numbers in five steps. The four-colour proof turns an infinite problem into nearly 2,000 finite cases that only a computer can check. The line is whether a person can follow every step.

Why some proofs need a community: every step of a long proof is still simple in principle, but no single person can check them all (four colour) or read them (abc, written in new mathematics). Certainty then rests on people who say they checked, and they can stall or be wrong for a while.

Proof vs. acceptance: new checks never overturn a correct proof. They test claimed proofs: they expose a flaw (Kempe, 1890), confirm a proof (Coq, 2005), or narrow a dispute (LANA, 2026). What changes is the community's acceptance.

One line to hold onto: a proof is an argument where every step is forced, so the conclusion is forced too, for anyone who can follow it.
