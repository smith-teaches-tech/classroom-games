What makes a Language A EE different (guide p. 43–44, 47)

A language A extended essay analyses one or more texts. You look at two things: the effects your texts create, and the devices (techniques) your texts use to create those effects. Your essay is your chance to study a topic in language and/or literature in depth, write your own literary criticism, and engage with published criticism or literary theory through close analysis of your texts.

Your essay can take one of three shapes: a study of one literary or language text originally written in the language of your essay, a comparison of more than one literary or language text, or a comparison of a literary text with a language text. You can choose from a wide range of texts. Besides novels, poems and plays, the guide lists speeches, advertising campaigns, songs, films, blogs, tweets and transcripts of recorded conversation.

Your analysis has two steps. First, you identify a feature or element in your text. Then you explain the significance or effects of that element, which answers the 'so what?' question. Your argument rests on close reading of your primary texts. Your secondary sources give you a framework for analysing how language, culture and context shape meaning.

Primary and secondary sources (guide p. 44)

Primary

Your primary sources are the novels, poems, stories, plays or essays by the author or authors your research focuses on.

Secondary

Your secondary sources are materials written about the author's work: books (such as studies and biographies), chapters, journal articles, essays, reviews of the work, studies of the genre, and literary theory. You must build a review of the existing literature on your topic into your essay.

How researchers in Language A answer questions (guide p. 44–47)

  • Close reading of your primary text: identify a feature, such as a metaphor, a stage direction or a type of camera shot, then explain its significance or effects.
  • Comparative study: analyse two or more texts side by side. Have a clear purpose for the comparison that goes beyond listing similarities and differences.
  • Using secondary scholarship: read literary criticism and literary theory, and question those sources instead of summarising them.
  • Limited background research: reading about the period or setting, such as Victorian society, gives you context. Keep the focus on your primary text.

Choosing a topic (guide p. 43–44)

  • Base your topic on one or more clearly defined texts in the target literature and/or language. Set time limits on your texts where it makes sense, for example one person's tweets on a specific topic from January to March 2021.
  • You may choose a social, political or philosophical issue that comes from your text. Your essay must then analyse how the text treats the issue. Don't use the text as factual evidence about the issue.
  • Avoid topics that just apply a standard pattern or theory to a text. The guide calls such topics self-limiting and gives the example "How does the Harry Potter series reveal the Hero’s Journey?".
  • Choose texts with enough literary merit, original style and complexity to support in-depth analysis. A recently published work may have little criticism written about it, and some "young adult" novels may not have enough depth.
  • For short stories or poetry, start by looking for texts that work well together, such as three to four stories by the same author, or three to four poems by the same poet on one theme. Your selection must support a 4,000-word essay.
  • Avoid biographical, historical, psychological or sociological topics. Topics like these pull you away from literary or language analysis, and they tend to produce essays that retell or describe.

Examples from the guide

Topic and research questionWhy it works
A critique of social expectations in Victorian drama
How effectively does Wilde both present and critique social expectations in The Importance of Being Earnest?		You identify social expectations in the play, such as marriage, manners, religion, duty and gender. Then you use close reading of Wilde's language to judge how he presents and critiques those expectations. Background research on Victorian society gives context, but quotations from the play carry the argument. Secondary sources are used critically. (p. 45)
Human and artificial relationships in Klara and the Sun
To what extent and to what effect can Artificial Friends emulate humans to build relationships in Klara and the Sun?		Close reading of characterisation and language shows how the Artificial Friends copy the humans around them. The essay then asks what those relationships reveal about technology and humanity. The essay also asks whether Ishiguro favours one over the other or treats the two as equal. (p. 45)
The role of satire in Adventures of Huckleberry Finn and Candide
How and to what effect is satire used in the travels of the main characters in Adventures of Huckleberry Finn and Candide?		A comparative essay. You investigate how each author creates satire and where satire appears in each novel. You also ask whether the two authors use satire for similar or different purposes, and whether satire gets each author's message across well. (p. 45)
The function of ghosts in American novels
What is the function and effect of the different approaches to ghosts in Beloved by Toni Morrison and The Turn of the Screw by Henry James?		Close reading and background research compare the kinds of ghosts each author creates and how the ghosts haunt the characters. Reading on the history of slavery, and on the turn of the 20th century in the context of modernism, gives you the context for the comparison. (p. 45)
The effect of sexism in vintage advertisements
How, and to what effect, do Van Heusen menswear ads (1949–1951) use sexism to promote their clothes?		A language-based essay on print advertisements from a set period. You analyse the style of the language and images to show how the ads portray men, women and clothing, and how language and image work together. Sources on rhetoric and visual language support your analysis. (p. 46)
The use of rhetorical techniques in motivational speeches
How effectively do Steve Jobs and Michelle Obama use rhetorical techniques to inspire their particular audiences?		You analyse the rhetorical and emotional devices in Steve Jobs's 2005 Stanford commencement speech and Michelle Obama's 2015 Tuskegee commencement speech. Research into the settings and the people mentioned helps explain how each speaker motivates an audience. Then you compare the strategies the two speakers share and the ones they don't. (p. 46)

How the essay is usually built (guide p. 45–46)

  • Introduction: explain your topic and the focus of your essay, and tell the reader which texts you'll write about.
  • Body paragraphs: build an argument with evidence from your primary and secondary sources.
  • Conclusion: give your answer to your research question, based on your close reading of your primary and secondary sources.
  • You may use sections and subsections with headings. Make sure the sections don't break up your essay and the headings don't distract the reader.
  • In a comparative essay, state the purpose of the comparison, why you chose the texts, and the months or years the texts were published. You can briefly explain what limited your choice of texts, but don't describe the other options you considered.

Common problems to avoid (guide p. 44–47)

  • Using your essay mainly to share your personal thoughts and opinions. Analyse the ideas in the text first. Then give your considered view, supported by quotations and examples.
  • Giving a general overview of the topic, or repeating what established critics say. The guide says these essays won't score well.
  • Summarising secondary sources, or presenting secondary sources without questioning them.
  • Retelling the plot instead of analysing it. Pointing out actions, relationships and concepts isn't enough: you must explain their significance or effects.
  • In a comparative essay, only listing similarities and differences, or giving one primary text more attention than the other.

Rules for Language A EEs (guide p. 43–44)

  • Your essay must be written in the language your essay is registered in. You may not submit a language A essay in the language of your language B subject.
  • At least one of your primary texts must have been originally written in the language of your essay. In a comparative essay, the other texts may be translations.
  • Your essay can't be based on a text you studied in your language and literature course. You may use a different text by an author you studied in the course.
  • If your text is a film, your focus must be on the screenplay (the written or spoken word), how the words are delivered, and their effect.
