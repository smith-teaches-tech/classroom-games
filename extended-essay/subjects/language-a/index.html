What makes a Language A EE different (guide p. 43–44, 47)

An extended essay in language A analyses one or more texts. You explore two things: the effects your texts achieve and the devices your texts use to create those effects. The guide describes the essay as a chance to produce an in-depth study of a topic in language and/or literature, to carry out your own literary criticism, and to engage with established critical commentary or literary theory through textual analysis.

Your essay can take one of three shapes: a study of a single literary or language text originally written in the language of your essay, a comparison of more than one literary or language text, or a comparison of a literary text with a language text. The guide accepts a wide range of texts. Alongside novels, poems and plays, the guide lists speeches, advertising campaigns, songs, films, blogs, tweets and transcripts of recorded conversation.

Analysis in language A has two parts. First you identify a characteristic or element in your text. Then you explain the significance or effects of that element, which answers the 'so what?' question. Your argument rests on close reading of your primary texts, and your secondary sources give you a framework for analysing how language, culture and context shape meaning.

Primary and secondary sources (guide p. 44)

Primary

Your primary sources are the novels, poems, stories, plays or essays by the author or authors whose work is the focus of your research.

Secondary

Your secondary sources are materials written about the author's work: books such as studies and biographies, chapters, journal articles, essays, reviews of the work, studies of the genre, and literary theory. You must integrate a review of the existing literature on your topic into your essay.

How researchers in Language A answer questions (guide p. 44–47)

  • Close reading of your primary text: identify a characteristic or element, such as a metaphor, a stage direction or a type of camera shot, then explain its significance or effects.
  • Comparative study: analyse two or more texts side by side, with a clear purpose for the comparison beyond listing similarities and differences.
  • Engagement with secondary scholarship: consult literary criticism and literary theory, and treat those sources critically rather than summarising them.
  • Limited contextual research: background reading on the period or setting, such as Victorian society, gives context, but the focus stays on your primary text.

Choosing a topic (guide p. 43–44)

  • Base your topic on one or more clearly defined texts related to the target literature and/or language. Give your texts temporal boundaries where appropriate, for example an individual's tweets on a specific topic from January to March 2021.
  • You may choose a social, political or philosophical issue arising from your text, but your essay must analyse how the text treats the issue. Do not use the text as documentary evidence about the issue.
  • Avoid topics that simply apply a generic or theoretical convention to a text. The guide calls such topics self-limiting and gives the example "How does the Harry Potter series reveal the Hero’s Journey?".
  • Choose texts with enough literary merit, stylistic originality and complexity to sustain in-depth analysis. A recently published work may have little criticism written about it, and some "young adult" novels may lack the depth needed.
  • For short stories or poetry, look for complementary texts first, such as three to four stories by the same author or three to four poems by the same poet on a particular theme. Your selection must sustain a 4,000-word essay.
  • Avoid biographical, historical, psychological or sociological topics. Such topics pull you away from literary or language analysis and tend to produce narrative or descriptive essays.

Examples from the guide

Topic and research questionWhy it works
A critique of social expectations in Victorian drama
How effectively does Wilde both present and critique social expectations in The Importance of Being Earnest?		You identify social expectations in the play, such as marriage, manners, religion, duty and gender, and use close reading of Wilde's language to judge how he presents and critiques them. Background research on Victorian society supplies context, but quotations from the play carry the argument, and secondary sources are used critically. (p. 45)
Human and artificial relationships in Klara and the Sun
To what extent and to what effect can Artificial Friends emulate humans to build relationships in Klara and the Sun?		Close reading of characterisation and language shows how the Artificial Friends copy the humans around them. The essay then asks what those relationships reveal about technology and humanity, and whether Ishiguro privileges one over the other or treats them as equal. (p. 45)
The role of satire in Adventures of Huckleberry Finn and Candide
How and to what effect is satire used in the travels of the main characters in Adventures of Huckleberry Finn and Candide?		A comparative essay: you investigate how each author creates satire, where satire appears in each novel, whether the two authors use satire for similar or different purposes, and whether satire communicates the authors' messages effectively. (p. 45)
The function of ghosts in American novels
What is the function and effect of the different approaches to ghosts in Beloved by Toni Morrison and The Turn of the Screw by Henry James?		Close reading and contextual research compare the kinds of ghosts each author creates and how the ghosts haunt the characters. Background on the history of slavery and on the turn of the 20th century in the context of modernism supplies the comparative context. (p. 45)
The effect of sexism in vintage advertisements
How, and to what effect, do Van Heusen menswear ads (1949–1951) use sexism to promote their clothes?		A language-based essay on print advertisements from a defined period. You analyse the stylistic features of language and image to show how men, women and clothing are portrayed and how language and image work together, supported by sources on rhetoric and visual language. (p. 46)
The use of rhetorical techniques in motivational speeches
How effectively do Steve Jobs and Michelle Obama use rhetorical techniques to inspire their particular audiences?		You analyse the rhetorical and emotive devices in Steve Jobs's 2005 Stanford commencement speech and Michelle Obama's 2015 Tuskegee commencement speech. Research into the settings and the people mentioned helps explain how each speaker motivates an audience, and you compare similar and different strategies. (p. 46)

How the essay is usually built (guide p. 45–46)

  • Introduction: explain your topic and the focus of your essay, and tell the reader which texts you will write about.
  • Body paragraphs: build an argument using textual support from your primary and secondary sources.
  • Conclusion: consider your answer to your research question in the light of your close readings of primary and secondary sources.
  • You may use sections and subsections with headings. Make sure the sections do not fragment your essay and the headings do not distract.
  • In a comparative essay, state the purpose of the comparison, your reason for selecting the texts, and the months or years the texts were published. You can briefly explain how your choice of texts was limited, but do not describe the other options you considered.

Common problems to avoid (guide p. 44–47)

  • Using your essay mainly as a vehicle for your personal thoughts and opinions. Analyse the ideas in the text first, then give your considered view, supported by quotations and examples.
  • Offering a general overview of the topic or repeating the views of established critics. The guide says such essays will not score well.
  • Summarising secondary sources, or presenting secondary sources without engaging with them critically.
  • Describing the plot instead of analysing. Identifying actions, relationships and concepts is not enough: you must explain their significance or effects.
  • In a comparative essay, only listing similarities and differences, or giving one primary text more attention than the other.

Rules for Language A EEs (guide p. 43–44)

  • Your essay must be written in the language for which your essay is registered. You may not submit a language A essay in the language of your language B subject.
  • At least one of your primary texts must have been originally written in the language of your essay. In a comparative essay, any other texts may be in translation.
  • Your essay cannot be based on a text you studied in your language and literature course. You may use a different text by an author you studied in the course.
  • If your text is a film, your focus must be on the screenplay (the written or spoken word), its delivery and its effect.
