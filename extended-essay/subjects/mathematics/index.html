What makes a Mathematics EE different (guide p. 68–71)

A mathematics EE usually takes one of two approaches. A theoretical essay explores a problem or area of mathematics outside the syllabus that caught your curiosity in class or in your wider reading. A practical essay uses mathematics to investigate an issue you care about, which could come from your career plans, a CAS project, a hobby or another subject. Many essays mix both approaches.

If you have a passion, such as a sport, that produces data, you can analyse that data, probably with statistics, to answer your research question. If your issue comes from another subject, the guide suggests you consider the interdisciplinary pathway. Whichever approach you take, a review of the literature (secondary research) is mandatory in a mathematics essay.

The guide calls knowledge and understanding "arguably the most important criterion in a mathematics essay". You are not expected to discover something new. You either explain mathematics that is new to you or apply mathematics you understand well to a new situation. Because algebra takes up space on the page, your final word count will probably fall short of 4,000 words.

Primary and secondary sources (guide p. 69–70)

Primary

Mathematics has no primary sources in the usual sense. In a practical essay, your starting point is data you collect yourself. The guide says to use raw data, because data that has already been processed (for example, batting averages in baseball) leaves you little to do.

Secondary

A review of the literature is mandatory in every mathematics essay. In a theoretical essay, your research mainly means reading around the subject: choose a few of the most relevant resources, because too many sources can be confusing.

How researchers in Mathematics answer questions (guide p. 69–71)

  • Theoretical essays: read around the area of mathematics, then explain the concepts clearly and prove your ideas, as in the guide's examples on RSA encryption and infinite sets.
  • Practical essays: collect appropriate data, graph the data first, and only then decide which techniques to use to analyse the data.
  • Modelling a real situation, for example building a population or habitat model and using the model to make a prediction.
  • Applying an algorithm to a real problem, for example using an algorithm such as Dijkstra's to choose a route.
  • Statistical analysis of data from an interest such as sport, for example testing whether home advantage is significant.

Choosing a topic (guide p. 68–71)

  • Start from an interest: a branch of mathematics that made you curious, or an issue from your career plans, a CAS project, a hobby or another subject that mathematics could investigate.
  • Narrow your interest to a topic. In a theoretical essay, the topic can show the area of mathematics you will explore. In a practical essay, the topic can show how mathematics will explore your area of interest.
  • Once your topic is narrow and focused, write a provisional research question. Expect the wording of your research question to change a little as your research develops.
  • Be careful with research questions that compare two approaches. The guide says a comparison of RSA with elliptic curves could not be explained fully within 4,000 words, so a question on RSA alone is better.
  • In a practical essay, do not name the mathematics or statistical methods in your research question, because your choice of method may change once you look at your data.
  • Do not be overly ambitious. Mathematics beyond the syllabus is not essential: you may be able to answer your research question with the mathematics you have already studied.

Examples from the guide

Topic and research questionWhy it works
Cryptography
What is RSA (Rivest–Shamir–Adleman) encryption and how does it help make the internet secure?		A mainly theoretical essay. You explain how RSA works, showing full understanding of any mathematics beyond the syllabus, and then explain how RSA keeps online transactions secure. The guide prefers this single-method question to a comparison of RSA with elliptic curves. (p. 69)
To infinity and beyond
How many infinities are there?		A mainly theoretical essay. You explore countably infinite sets, explain the concepts clearly and prove your ideas, then move on to the uncountable continuum and discuss how the idea of infinity could be extended further. (p. 69)
Fractal dimension
What are fractals and what is fractal dimension? (or: Length of a coastline)		A mainly theoretical essay. You explain how fractals develop and why fractals matter, perhaps exploring limits problems such as a finite area with an infinite perimeter. From there you could move on to the length of the coastline of a favourite island or to fractal dimension. (p. 69)
The last rhinoceros
How long do the rhinos have left?		A mainly practical essay. You explore both the population size and the habitat of the rhinoceros, try to build a realistic model for population or habitat area, and use the model to predict which reaches zero first. The guide says you would start by graphing the data. (p. 70)
Getting to school by public transport
What is the quickest and/or cheapest route from your home to school?		A mainly practical essay. You study the transport system in your area, compare different ways of getting to school, estimate travel and walking times, and use an algorithm such as Dijkstra's to decide on your preferred route. (p. 70)
Winning the English football Premier League
What is “home advantage”, and are Premier League football champions equally successful away from home?		A mainly practical essay. You use raw Premier League results to examine the distribution of goals scored at home and away, test whether home advantage is significant, and check whether the champions overcome home advantage in away games. The guide says you would start by graphing the data. (p. 70)

How the essay is usually built (guide p. 70–71)

  • Introduction: explain your research question and your plan clearly, in your own words, starting from mathematics you know from the syllabus.
  • Show and explain every essential step in your own words. Subheadings may help, depending on the mathematics you explore.
  • You may quote a complicated theorem without proving it if the proof is too difficult, but you must explain what the theorem is for and give an illustrative example.
  • In a practical essay, explain the background, then show your data before your analysis. Put a large amount of data in an appendix.
  • Write your mathematics with an equation editor: use a new line for each step of algebra, and never use * for multiplication or ^ for powers.

Common problems to avoid (guide p. 69–71)

  • Including mathematics you do not fully understand. If you cannot explain the mathematics, leave it out and change your plan.
  • Relying too heavily on your sources or copying information directly from your sources.
  • Trying to fit in too much, such as two methods, and losing a clear demonstration of your understanding of the mathematics.
  • Working with processed data instead of raw data.
  • Choosing your analysis techniques before you have looked at your data.
  • Extending your analysis beyond your research question, which the guide says is likely to weaken your essay.

Rules for Mathematics EEs (guide p. 69–70)

  • A review of the literature (secondary research) is mandatory in every mathematics essay.
  • In a data-based essay, use raw data rather than data that has already been processed.
