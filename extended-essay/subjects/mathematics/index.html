What makes a Mathematics EE different (guide p. 68–71)

A mathematics EE usually takes one of two approaches. A theoretical essay explores a problem or area of mathematics outside the syllabus that made you curious, in class or in your own reading. A practical essay uses mathematics to investigate an issue you care about. The issue could come from your career plans, a CAS project, a hobby or another subject. Many essays mix both approaches.

If you have an interest that produces data, such as a sport, you can analyse that data (probably with statistics) to answer your research question. If your issue comes from another subject, the guide suggests you think about the interdisciplinary pathway. Whichever approach you take, your mathematics essay must include a review of the literature (secondary research).

The guide calls knowledge and understanding "arguably the most important criterion in a mathematics essay". You don't have to discover something new. You either explain mathematics that is new to you, or you apply mathematics you understand well to a new situation. Algebra takes up space on the page, so your final word count will probably be under 4,000 words.

Primary and secondary sources (guide p. 69–70)

Primary

Mathematics has no primary sources in the usual sense. In a practical essay, you start from data you collect yourself. Use raw data: data that has already been processed (for example, batting averages in baseball) leaves you little to do.

Secondary

Every mathematics essay must include a review of the literature. In a theoretical essay, your research is mostly reading around the subject. Choose a few of the most relevant resources, because too many sources can be confusing.

How researchers in Mathematics answer questions (guide p. 69–71)

  • Theoretical essays: read around the area of mathematics, then explain the concepts clearly and prove your ideas. The guide's examples on RSA encryption and infinite sets work this way.
  • Practical essays: collect suitable data and graph the data first. Only then decide which techniques to use to analyse the data.
  • Modelling a real situation: for example, building a population or habitat model and using the model to make a prediction.
  • Applying an algorithm to a real problem: for example, using an algorithm such as Dijkstra's to choose a route.
  • Statistical analysis of data from an interest such as sport: for example, testing whether home advantage is significant.

Choosing a topic (guide p. 68–71)

  • Start from an interest: a branch of mathematics that made you curious, or an issue from your career plans, a CAS project, a hobby or another subject that mathematics could investigate.
  • Narrow your interest to a topic. In a theoretical essay, the topic can name the area of mathematics you'll explore. In a practical essay, the topic can show how mathematics will explore your area of interest.
  • Once your topic is narrow and focused, write a first version of your research question. Expect the wording of your research question to change a little as your research goes on.
  • Be careful with research questions that compare two approaches. The guide says a comparison of RSA with elliptic curves couldn't be explained fully in 4,000 words, so a question on RSA alone is better.
  • In a practical essay, don't name the mathematics or statistical methods in your research question. Your choice of method may change once you look at your data.
  • Don't be too ambitious. You don't need mathematics beyond the syllabus: you may be able to answer your research question with the mathematics you've already studied.

Examples from the guide

Topic and research questionWhy it works
Cryptography
What is RSA (Rivest–Shamir–Adleman) encryption and how does it help make the internet secure?		A mainly theoretical essay. You explain how RSA works, showing full understanding of any mathematics beyond the syllabus. Then you explain how RSA keeps online transactions secure. The guide prefers this one-method question to a comparison of RSA with elliptic curves. (p. 69)
To infinity and beyond
How many infinities are there?		A mainly theoretical essay. You explore countably infinite sets, explain the concepts clearly and prove your ideas. Then you move on to the uncountable continuum and discuss how the idea of infinity could be extended further. (p. 69)
Fractal dimension
What are fractals and what is fractal dimension? (or: Length of a coastline)		A mainly theoretical essay. You explain how fractals develop and why fractals matter. You might explore limits problems, such as a finite area with an infinite perimeter. From there, you could move on to the length of the coastline of a favourite island, or to fractal dimension. (p. 69)
The last rhinoceros
How long do the rhinos have left?		A mainly practical essay. You explore both the population size and the habitat of the rhinoceros. You try to build a realistic model for the population or the habitat area, and use the model to predict which one reaches zero first. The guide says you would start by graphing the data. (p. 70)
Getting to school by public transport
What is the quickest and/or cheapest route from your home to school?		A mainly practical essay. You study the transport system in your area and compare different ways of getting to school. You estimate travel and walking times, then use an algorithm such as Dijkstra's to choose your preferred route. (p. 70)
Winning the English football Premier League
What is “home advantage”, and are Premier League football champions equally successful away from home?		A mainly practical essay. You use raw Premier League results to look at how goals scored at home and away are distributed. You test whether home advantage is significant, and check whether the champions overcome home advantage in away games. The guide says you would start by graphing the data. (p. 70)

How the essay is usually built (guide p. 70–71)

  • Introduction: explain your research question and your plan clearly, in your own words. Start from mathematics you know from the syllabus.
  • Show and explain every essential step in your own words. Subheadings may help, depending on the mathematics you explore.
  • If the proof of a complicated theorem is too difficult, you may quote the theorem without proving it. You must still explain what the theorem is for and give an example of the theorem in use.
  • In a practical essay, explain the background, then show your data before your analysis. Put large amounts of data in an appendix.
  • Write your mathematics with an equation editor. Use a new line for each step of algebra, and never use * for multiplication or ^ for powers.

Common problems to avoid (guide p. 69–71)

  • Including mathematics you don't fully understand. If you can't explain the mathematics, leave it out and change your plan.
  • Relying too much on your sources, or copying information straight from your sources.
  • Trying to fit in too much (two methods, for example), so your understanding of the mathematics no longer shows clearly.
  • Working with processed data instead of raw data.
  • Choosing your analysis techniques before you've looked at your data.
  • Taking your analysis beyond your research question. The guide says extra analysis is likely to weaken your essay.

Rules for Mathematics EEs (guide p. 69–70)

  • Every mathematics essay must include a review of the literature (secondary research).
  • In a data-based essay, use raw data, not data that has already been processed.
