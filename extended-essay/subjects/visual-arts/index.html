What makes a Visual Arts EE different (guide p. 71–72)

An arts EE lets you explore a topic of particular interest to you in visual arts. Your essay must combine analysis of primary sources (in visual arts, your chosen artworks) with disciplined research informed by scholarly methods, and then critically evaluate the results. Secondary sources such as books, critiques, magazine articles, interviews, subject journals and websites must support your argument as evidence.

Your selection of artworks is your body of work, and your body of work needs enough substance to sustain an in-depth 4,000-word essay. The guide sets no precise number. Look first for complementary works, for example three or four by the same artist. As you analyse the works, you may find three give enough for comparison and contrast, or that a fourth deepens your analysis.

Arts essays more often use qualitative research methods that aim to understand the art form within a context. Approaches can include the study of aesthetic concepts, historical, social and cultural aspects, and gender-based or post-colonialist readings. The guide also presents your EE as a chance to study art in an international context, exploring both the familiar and the new.

Primary and secondary sources (guide p. 71–72, 76)

Primary

In visual arts, your primary sources are the artworks you analyse, and together your chosen artworks form your body of work. If you cannot see an artwork in person, you must rely on high-quality reproductions, videos and images instead.

Secondary

Secondary sources such as books, critiques, magazine articles, interviews, subject journals and websites must be used as evidential support for your argument. Secondary sources provide context, support the points in your analysis and explain the circumstances behind an artist's decisions; the views of artists, curators, historians and critics can add different perspectives.

How researchers in Visual Arts answer questions (guide p. 72, 77)

  • Artistic and contextual analysis of your chosen artworks, considering not only what the artworks say but also how the artworks say it.
  • Qualitative research that seeks to understand the art form within a context. Your essay must show clear knowledge and understanding of at least one appropriate context.
  • Readings through aesthetic concepts, historical, social and cultural aspects, or gender-based and post-colonialist lenses.
  • Comparison of two or more artworks. Your comparisons must be valid, come from sensitive and objective analysis, and have a clearly explained, significant purpose.
  • For an influence topic: rigorous formal analysis backed by supporting documents that evidence the influence.

Choosing a topic (guide p. 72, 76)

  • Keep your topic inside visual arts. The guide warns against topics that are peripheral to the arts subject, even though the arts link to many other disciplines.
  • Your research question must be answered through artistic and contextual analysis, and your essay must not drift into areas such as psychology, history or sociology.
  • You may discuss a social, political or gender issue arising from an artwork, but focus on the artistic meaning and significance of the artwork rather than using the artwork as documentary evidence of the issue.
  • Think carefully before writing about one artist's or style's influence on another. Finding similarities is relatively easy; demonstrating an actual influence is complex and needs formal analysis plus documentary evidence.
  • Review the existing literature on your topic to help you build your research question and judge whether your research question is relevant.
  • Select a limited number of artworks: enough substance to keep raising points without repeating yourself, but few enough for in-depth analysis within the word limit.

Examples from the guide

Topic and research questionWhy it works
The influence of Japanese prints on Claude Monet’s artistic practice
To what extent did aesthetic principles of Japanese prints from the Edo period influence Claude Monet’s material and conceptual artistic practice?		The guide names this as a good approach to an influence topic. You research the characteristics of Edo-period Japanese prints and of Impressionist paintings, then analyse Monet artworks from sequential periods of his life to trace the conceptual and material development of his work. You look for secondary sources that evidence the influence, give examples to support your claims, evaluate the strengths and limitations of your research, and conclude on the extent of the influence. (p. 75)
The embodiment of the cyclical nature of life and death in work by Joni Brenner
To what extent does the Skull Series of compositions by Joni Brenner embody the cyclical nature of life and death?		You first research the socio-historical context of Apartheid in South Africa and post-Apartheid art, so that you understand Brenner's ideas and subjects. You then analyse the artistic and symbolic aspects of the Skull Series, evaluate how far the artworks embody the cycle of life and death, illustrate your points, evaluate the strengths and limitations of your research, and conclude on the extent of that embodiment. (p. 76)

How the essay is usually built (guide p. 76–77)

  • Images are mandatory in a visual arts essay and must appear in the body of the essay. Use images selectively, to support the reader's understanding of your essay.
  • Identify and cite every image in the essay itself and in the separate list of sources at the end. Every image must be a reasonable size and high-quality resolution.
  • Build your argument from analysed and explained artworks, supported by secondary sources. The guide says a combination of both gives the most convincing argument.
  • Illustrate each point with examples from the artworks you examine, and use artistic terminology and concepts fluently and accurately.
  • In your discussion, direct the reader to evidence in the artworks and in your secondary research, in a balanced way. Identify the strengths and limitations of your research and how they shaped your interpretation and conclusions.

Common problems to avoid (guide p. 72, 76–77)

  • Choosing a topic that is peripheral to visual arts, so that your essay becomes psychology, history or sociology.
  • Treating an artwork merely as documentary evidence for a social, political or gender issue.
  • Claiming that one artist influenced another on the strength of visual similarities alone, without formal analysis and supporting documents.
  • Building your argument mainly by quoting or summarising secondary sources, which the guide says is likely to give limited argumentation.
  • Selecting too thin a body of work, which leads to overanalysing or repeating points, or too many artworks to analyse in depth within the word limit.
  • Making comparisons between artworks without explaining why the comparison matters, or comparisons that are not valid.

Rules for Visual Arts EEs (guide p. 72, 76–77)

  • Your essay must answer your research question using artistic and contextual analysis.
  • Secondary sources must be used as evidential support for your argument, and you must critically examine both primary and secondary sources.
  • Images are mandatory in a visual arts essay and must appear in the body of the essay.
  • All images must be properly identified and cited in the essay and in the list of sources, at a reasonable size and high-quality resolution.
