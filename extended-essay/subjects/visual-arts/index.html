What makes a Visual Arts EE different (guide p. 71–72)

In a visual arts EE, you explore a topic that interests you in visual arts. Your essay must combine analysis of primary sources (your chosen artworks) with careful research that uses scholarly methods. Then you critically evaluate the results. Secondary sources such as books, critiques, magazine articles, interviews, subject journals and websites must support your argument as evidence.

Your chosen artworks are your body of work. Your body of work needs enough substance for an in-depth 4,000-word essay. The guide doesn't set an exact number. Start by looking for works that go well together, for example three or four by the same artist. As you analyse the works, you may find that three are enough to compare and contrast, or that a fourth adds depth to your analysis.

Arts essays more often use qualitative research methods, which aim to understand the art form in its context. Approaches include studying aesthetic concepts, historical, social and cultural aspects, and gender-based or post-colonialist readings. The guide also sees your EE as a chance to study art in an international context, exploring both the familiar and the new.

Primary and secondary sources (guide p. 71–72, 76)

Primary

In visual arts, your primary sources are the artworks you analyse. Together, your chosen artworks form your body of work. If you can't see an artwork in person, use high-quality reproductions, videos and images instead.

Secondary

Secondary sources such as books, critiques, magazine articles, interviews, subject journals and websites must be used as evidence for your argument. Secondary sources give context, support the points in your analysis and explain the circumstances behind an artist's decisions. The views of artists, curators, historians and critics can add different perspectives.

How researchers in Visual Arts answer questions (guide p. 72, 77)

  • Artistic and contextual analysis of your chosen artworks: look at what the artworks say and also how the artworks say it.
  • Qualitative research that tries to understand the art form in its context. Your essay must show clear knowledge and understanding of at least one suitable context.
  • Readings through aesthetic concepts, through historical, social and cultural aspects, or through gender-based and post-colonialist lenses.
  • Comparing two or more artworks. Your comparisons must be valid and come from sensitive, objective analysis. Each comparison needs a clear, significant purpose that you explain.
  • For an influence topic: rigorous formal analysis (analysis of the visual elements of the works), backed by documents that give evidence of the influence.

Choosing a topic (guide p. 72, 76)

  • Keep your topic inside visual arts. The arts link to many other subjects, but the guide warns against topics that sit at the edge of the arts subject.
  • You must answer your research question through artistic and contextual analysis. Your essay must not drift into subjects such as psychology, history or sociology.
  • You may discuss a social, political or gender issue that comes from an artwork. Focus on the artistic meaning and significance of the artwork. Don't use the artwork only as documentary evidence of the issue.
  • Think carefully before writing about one artist's or style's influence on another. Finding similarities is fairly easy. Showing an actual influence is hard, and needs formal analysis plus documentary evidence.
  • Read the existing literature on your topic. The literature helps you build your research question and judge whether your research question is relevant.
  • Choose a limited number of artworks: enough substance to keep making new points without repeating yourself, and few enough to analyse in depth within the word limit.

Examples from the guide

Topic and research questionWhy it works
The influence of Japanese prints on Claude Monet’s artistic practice
To what extent did aesthetic principles of Japanese prints from the Edo period influence Claude Monet’s material and conceptual artistic practice?		The guide names this as a good approach to an influence topic. You research the features of Edo-period Japanese prints and of Impressionist paintings. Then you analyse Monet artworks from one period of his life after another, to trace how his ideas and his use of materials developed. You look for secondary sources that give evidence of the influence, and give examples to support your claims. Finally, you evaluate the strengths and limitations of your research and conclude on how far the influence went. (p. 75)
The embodiment of the cyclical nature of life and death in work by Joni Brenner
To what extent does the Skull Series of compositions by Joni Brenner embody the cyclical nature of life and death?		You first research the social and historical context of Apartheid in South Africa and of post-Apartheid art, so that you understand Brenner's ideas and subjects. Then you analyse the artistic and symbolic features of the Skull Series and judge how far the artworks embody the cycle of life and death. You illustrate your points, evaluate the strengths and limitations of your research, and conclude on how far the Skull Series embodies that cycle. (p. 76)

How the essay is usually built (guide p. 76–77)

  • Images are required in a visual arts essay and must appear in the body of the essay. Choose images carefully, to help the reader understand your essay.
  • Identify and cite every image, both in the essay itself and in the separate list of sources at the end. Every image must be a reasonable size and in high-quality resolution.
  • Build your argument from artworks you've analysed and explained, supported by secondary sources. The guide says combining both gives the most convincing argument.
  • Support each point with examples from the artworks you examine. Use artistic terms and concepts fluently and accurately.
  • In your discussion, point the reader to evidence in the artworks and in your secondary research, in a balanced way. Identify the strengths and limitations of your research, and how those strengths and limitations shaped your interpretation and conclusions.

Common problems to avoid (guide p. 72, 76–77)

  • Choosing a topic at the edge of visual arts, so that your essay turns into psychology, history or sociology.
  • Treating an artwork only as documentary evidence for a social, political or gender issue.
  • Claiming that one artist influenced another because their works look similar, without formal analysis and supporting documents.
  • Building your argument mainly by quoting or summarising secondary sources. The guide says an argument built that way is likely to be limited.
  • Choosing too few artworks (so you overanalyse or repeat points), or too many to analyse in depth within the word limit.
  • Comparing artworks without explaining why the comparison matters, or making comparisons that aren't valid.

Rules for Visual Arts EEs (guide p. 72, 76–77)

  • Your essay must answer your research question using artistic and contextual analysis.
  • Secondary sources must be used as evidence for your argument. You must critically examine both your primary and your secondary sources.
  • Images are required in a visual arts essay and must appear in the body of the essay.
  • Every image must be properly identified and cited in the essay and in the list of sources, at a reasonable size and in high-quality resolution.
