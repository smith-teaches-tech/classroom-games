What an interdisciplinary EE is (guide p. 20–21)

An interdisciplinary extended essay uses two DP subjects to research one topic. The guide defines interdisciplinary work as integrating knowledge, concepts, theories, perspectives or methods, or a combination of these, from your two chosen subjects so that the two subjects work together. The reason to combine two subjects is to reach fresh insights and deeper understandings that research within one DP subject alone could not produce.

Integration does not have to be in place on day one. The guide says integration develops and emerges as you research and write. You can start simply by applying some approaches from each of your two subjects to your research question. You then build a line of argument from the connections you find, and finish with a conclusion that shows the reader how you brought the two subjects together.

Your two subjects do not need equal weight. The balance depends on your topic and the emphasis of your research question. What your essay must show is that combining the two subjects is what allowed you to answer your research question well. Your understanding of both subjects also needs to be evident, which is why the guide strongly recommends that you study at least one of the two subjects.

Interdisciplinary or subject-focused? (guide p. 18, 20–22)

  • Choose the interdisciplinary pathway when your idea is better explored through the combined lenses of two DP subjects. Choose the subject-focused pathway when your idea suits one subject.
  • The interdisciplinary pathway fits when you want to answer a question of personal interest with more insight than one subject would allow.
  • The interdisciplinary pathway fits when you want to integrate concepts or methods from two subjects to understand an issue or problem more deeply.
  • The interdisciplinary pathway fits when you are investigating a complex issue of global concern that two DP subjects can address better than one.
  • The interdisciplinary pathway fits when you want to apply an insight, theory or method from one DP subject to the content of a second DP subject.
  • The interdisciplinary pathway fits when you want to explore questions at the boundaries of DP subjects.
  • If your topic combines theatre and literature, the guide tells you to choose literature and performance in the subject-focused pathway.
  • If your topic combines geography and biology, the guide suggests you consider environmental systems and societies (ESS) in the subject-focused pathway, depending on the nature of your topic.
  • Changing your thinking about the pathway before you settle on one is acceptable, according to the guide.

Rules about subjects (guide p. 20–21, 23)

  • An interdisciplinary extended essay combines two DP subjects.
  • Environmental systems and societies (ESS) and literature and performance are classed as cross-disciplinary subjects. You cannot choose either subject in the interdisciplinary pathway, only in the subject-focused pathway. The guide says the restriction aims to prevent over-complexity in your research and writing.
  • The guide strongly recommends that you study at least one of the two DP subjects you use for your extended essay.
  • When your interdisciplinary extended essay is registered with the IB, the registration must place the essay in one of the five frameworks. The connection with the framework is not directly assessed.

Developing an interdisciplinary research question (guide p. 23, 30–33)

  • Develop your research question once your topic is ready for the interdisciplinary pathway: that is, after you have selected a framework and chosen your two DP subjects.
  • Your research question should be clearly stated and focused.
  • Your research question should stay open to amendment or change if your research takes you in a different direction.
  • Your research question should invite a critical examination of your topic through integrating knowledge, concepts, theories, perspectives or methods, or a combination of these, from your two DP subjects.
  • Your research question should encourage analysis, evaluation and synthesis, not description, unsupported generalisations or value judgements.
  • Imagine answering your research question comfortably within the 4,000-word limit of the extended essay.
  • Your research question needs to reflect your interdisciplinary approach (criterion A).
  • The emphasis of your research question can place the same topic in a different framework. The guide's example on Samson and Delilah sits in "power, equality, justice" but could also work in "culture, identity, expression", depending on the focus of the research.
  • The guide's example topics are general enough to adapt to your own context. For example, the question on India could be addressed to Brazil or China, and the Singapore food question could be applied to another small country with similar restricted land.
  • For more detailed guidance, the guide points you to the "Researching and writing the extended essay" section of the guide.

Integrating the two subjects (guide p. 20–21, 23, 33)

  • Integration means combining knowledge, concepts, theories, perspectives or methods, or a combination of these, from your two subjects so that the two subjects work together on your topic.
  • Integration can lead to fresh insights and new or deeper understandings that research within one DP subject alone would not allow.
  • Integration develops as your research and writing progress. You do not need integration as your starting point.
  • The guide's sequence: apply approaches from both subjects to your research question, build a line of argument from connections between the two subjects, synthesise those elements, then write a conclusion that shows the reader how you integrated the two subjects. Your conclusion allows the success of your integrative approach to be evaluated.
  • The balance between your two subjects does not have to be even. Your essay must still show clearly that integrating the two subjects enabled an effective response to your research question.
  • Your understanding of both subjects needs to be evident. The guide calls the quality of the research the overall measure of rigour, and says a more rigorous research process is likely to lead to more reliable findings.
  • The connection between your essay and its framework is not directly assessed. The frameworks guide your inquiry and help the IB coordinate assessment.
  • As you write, read the assessment criteria sections of the subject-specific guidance for both of your subjects (or for the subject group that includes each subject), as well as the generic guidance for the extended essay.
  • Criterion A (framework for the essay): explain and justify your use of the interdisciplinary approach. The reader should see that you drew on the research methods of both subjects. Apply the structural conventions of essays in each subject where relevant.
  • Criterion B (knowledge and understanding): show your disciplinary grounding in both subjects through effective use of relevant terminology and concepts. The balance between the two subjects depends on your research question and need not be even.
  • Criterion C (analysis and line of argument): your findings should reflect the combination of your two subject lenses. Your line of argument should show the reader how the integration of the two subjects' perspectives develops and flows.
  • Criterion D (discussion and evaluation): discussion is the key tool for integrating the concepts, understandings and, where relevant, theories of your two subjects in relation to your research question. An appraisal of your interdisciplinary approach can strengthen your evaluation of the essay's effectiveness.
  • Criterion E (reflection): the guide notes that applying an interdisciplinary approach to research is one of the transferable skills you have learned.

Common problems to avoid (guide p. 20–21, 23)

  • Choosing environmental systems and societies (ESS) or literature and performance as one of your two subjects: neither subject is permitted in the interdisciplinary pathway.
  • Placing two subjects side by side without showing that combining the two subjects is what let you answer your research question.
  • Choosing two subjects you do not study: the guide strongly recommends that you study at least one of your two subjects.
  • Assuming you must have integration worked out before you begin. The guide says integration emerges as you research and write.
  • Forcing your topic to connect with every word in a framework's name. A topic can focus on one word, such as identity.
  • Going too long without checking the quality of your research with your supervisor. The guide says to check regularly and act to improve your research if needed.

Supervision (guide p. 20–21)

  • Make sure your supervisor shares the interdisciplinary guidance you need, or gives you access to it, from both the guide and the Extended essay support material.
  • Check regularly with your supervisor about the quality of your ongoing research and, if necessary, take action to improve your research. The guide presents regular checks as the way to safeguard the rigour of an interdisciplinary essay.
