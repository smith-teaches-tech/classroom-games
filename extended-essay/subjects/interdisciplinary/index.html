What an interdisciplinary EE is (guide p. 20–21)

An interdisciplinary extended essay uses two DP subjects to research one topic. You bring together knowledge, concepts, theories, perspectives or methods (or a mix of these) from your two subjects so that the subjects work together. The point of combining two subjects is to reach fresh insights and deeper understanding that research in one subject alone couldn't give you.

You don't need integration (your two subjects working together) on day one. Integration grows as you research and write. You can start by applying some approaches from each subject to your research question. Then you build a line of argument from the connections you find. Your conclusion shows the reader how you brought the two subjects together.

Your two subjects don't need equal weight. The balance depends on your topic and the focus of your research question. Your essay must show that combining the two subjects is what let you answer your research question well. You also need to show that you understand both subjects. For this reason, the guide strongly recommends that you study at least one of the two subjects.

Interdisciplinary or subject-focused? (guide p. 18, 20–22)

  • Choose the interdisciplinary pathway when your idea is better explored through two DP subjects together. Choose the subject-focused pathway when your idea suits one subject.
  • The interdisciplinary pathway fits when you want to answer a question that interests you with more insight than one subject would give.
  • The interdisciplinary pathway fits when you want to combine concepts or methods from two subjects to understand an issue or problem more deeply.
  • The interdisciplinary pathway fits when you're investigating a complex global issue that two DP subjects can address better than one.
  • The interdisciplinary pathway fits when you want to apply an insight, theory or method from one DP subject to the content of another DP subject.
  • The interdisciplinary pathway fits when you want to explore questions at the boundaries between DP subjects.
  • If your topic combines theatre and literature, the guide tells you to choose literature and performance in the subject-focused pathway.
  • If your topic combines geography and biology, the guide suggests you consider environmental systems and societies (ESS) in the subject-focused pathway, depending on your topic.
  • You can change your mind about the pathway before you settle on one.

Rules about subjects (guide p. 20–21, 23)

  • An interdisciplinary extended essay combines two DP subjects.
  • Environmental systems and societies (ESS) and literature and performance are cross-disciplinary subjects. You can choose either subject only in the subject-focused pathway, never in the interdisciplinary pathway. The guide says this rule stops your research and writing from becoming too complex.
  • The guide strongly recommends that you study at least one of the two DP subjects you use for your extended essay.
  • When your interdisciplinary extended essay is registered with the IB, the registration must place your essay in one of the five frameworks. The link with the framework isn't directly assessed.

Developing an interdisciplinary research question (guide p. 23, 30–33)

  • Write your research question once your topic is ready for the interdisciplinary pathway: after you've chosen a framework and your two DP subjects.
  • Your research question should be clear and focused.
  • Your research question should stay open to change if your research takes you in a different direction.
  • Your research question should invite a critical look at your topic by combining knowledge, concepts, theories, perspectives or methods (or a mix of these) from your two DP subjects.
  • Your research question should lead to analysis, evaluation and synthesis (combining ideas into a whole). Avoid a question that leads to description, unsupported generalisations or value judgements.
  • Check that you can imagine answering your research question comfortably within the 4,000-word limit of the extended essay.
  • Your research question needs to reflect your interdisciplinary approach (criterion A).
  • Where you put the emphasis in your research question can place the same topic in a different framework. The guide's example on Samson and Delilah sits in "power, equality, justice" but could also work in "culture, identity, expression", depending on the focus of the research.
  • The guide's example topics are general enough to adapt to your own context. For example, the question on India could be asked about Brazil or China, and the Singapore food question could be applied to another small country with similarly limited land.
  • For more detailed help, read the "Researching and writing the extended essay" section of the guide.

Integrating the two subjects (guide p. 20–21, 23, 33)

  • Integration means combining knowledge, concepts, theories, perspectives or methods (or a mix of these) from your two subjects so that the subjects work together on your topic.
  • Integration can lead to fresh insights and new or deeper understanding that research in one DP subject alone wouldn't allow.
  • Integration grows as your research and writing progress. You don't need to start with integration.
  • The guide's sequence: apply approaches from both subjects to your research question, build a line of argument from connections between the two subjects, bring those elements together (synthesise), then write a conclusion that shows the reader how you integrated the two subjects. Your conclusion is where the reader can judge how well your integrated approach worked.
  • The balance between your two subjects doesn't have to be even. Your essay must still show clearly that integrating the two subjects let you answer your research question well.
  • You need to show that you understand both subjects. The guide calls the quality of the research the overall measure of rigour (careful, thorough work), and says a more rigorous research process is likely to lead to more reliable findings.
  • The link between your essay and its framework isn't directly assessed. The frameworks guide your inquiry and help the IB coordinate assessment.
  • As you write, read the assessment criteria sections of the subject-specific guidance for both of your subjects (or for the subject group each subject belongs to), and the general guidance for the extended essay.
  • Criterion A (framework for the essay): explain and justify why you used an interdisciplinary approach. The reader should see that you drew on the research methods of both subjects. Where relevant, follow the essay structure each subject expects.
  • Criterion B (knowledge and understanding): show your grounding in both subjects by using the right terms and concepts well. The balance between the two subjects depends on your research question and doesn't have to be even.
  • Criterion C (analysis and line of argument): your findings should reflect your two subject lenses together. Your line of argument should show the reader how the two subjects' perspectives come together as your essay develops.
  • Criterion D (discussion and evaluation): discussion is your main tool for bringing together the concepts, understanding and, where relevant, theories of your two subjects to answer your research question. Judging how well your interdisciplinary approach worked can strengthen your evaluation of your essay.
  • Criterion E (reflection): the guide notes that applying an interdisciplinary approach to research is one of the transferable skills you've learned.

Common problems to avoid (guide p. 20–21, 23)

  • Choosing environmental systems and societies (ESS) or literature and performance as one of your two subjects. Neither subject is allowed in the interdisciplinary pathway.
  • Putting two subjects side by side without showing that combining the two subjects is what let you answer your research question.
  • Choosing two subjects you don't study. The guide strongly recommends that you study at least one of your two subjects.
  • Thinking you must have integration worked out before you begin. Integration grows as you research and write.
  • Forcing your topic to connect with every word in a framework's name. A topic can focus on one word, such as identity.
  • Going too long without checking the quality of your research with your supervisor. Check regularly, and act to improve your research if needed.

Supervision (guide p. 20–21)

  • Make sure your supervisor shares the interdisciplinary guidance you need from the guide and the Extended essay support material, or gives you access to that guidance.
  • Check regularly with your supervisor on the quality of your research, and take action to improve your research if needed. The guide presents these regular checks as the way to keep an interdisciplinary essay rigorous.
