What makes a Language B EE different (guide p. 48–49)

An extended essay in language B lets you develop your knowledge and understanding of the second language you are studying, and show that you understand the culture associated with that language. The guide names three areas you can explore: language use, literary analysis, or a study of the culture itself. You can combine these areas if your research question calls for it.

Your essay can be based on any meaningful example of the target language, spoken or written, as long as the example is linguistically or culturally complex enough to support academic analysis of a well-constructed research question. When you analyse language or literature, you will often be applying the cultural lens of the target language.

You do not need advanced fluency or highly developed writing in the target language to write a language B essay. The guide does say that clarity of meaning and your ability to address the assessment criteria matter. Your essay is still academic writing, so keep your tone and register appropriate: writing that is too informal can weaken your essay.

Primary and secondary sources (guide p. 48–50)

Primary

The texts at the centre of your essay, such as novels, short stories, speeches, advertisements, plays, screenplays, song lyrics or historical documents. Every text you analyse or use as a comparative example must have been originally written or spoken in the target language.

Secondary

A good range of relevant secondary sources, such as literary theory and literary articles, is essential to support your argument, and the guide expects most of your resource materials to be written in the target language. Published material is preferable and usually more reliable than unpublished material.

How researchers in Language B answer questions (guide p. 48–50)

  • Literary analysis: discuss an aspect of a literary work originally written in the target language, such as a literary technique in one text, a thematic comparison of two works from different periods, or a character study.
  • Language use: analyse an aspect of the development, structure or use of the target language, always in relation to its cultural context or a specific text. The guide's example is a study of how the language of American rap influences teenage communication in the USA, based on specific rap lyrics.
  • Cultural study: investigate a sociocultural issue specific to the target language, either through its influence on the form or use of the language, or through customs or artefacts specific to the country where the language is spoken.
  • Close analysis of a defined set of examples, such as three examples of a technique in each of two speeches, or six advertisements analysed as cultural artefacts.

Choosing a topic (guide p. 48–49)

  • Choose a topic in one of the three areas: literary analysis, language use and development, or a study of the culture. A combination of these areas is also acceptable.
  • A literary work does not need to be set where the target language is spoken. The guide notes that Romeo and Juliet is set in Italy but was originally written in English.
  • For a cultural topic, apply the guide's test of a cultural artefact: a concrete object, not a belief, a movement or a state of being, that reflects the target culture in a specific way. A film or television series qualifies only when you analyse it as a cultural artefact in the target language.
  • Choose an issue specific to the target language and its culture. Issues shared by many cultures, such as religion, racism or the rights of women, are intercultural and not specific enough, and neither are trends like social media acronyms or emoticons.
  • Texts can include "new textualities" such as literary blogs, micro fiction or graphic novels, but check first that a good range of relevant secondary sources exists for your topic.
  • If you choose literature, be aware that a recently published work may have little criticism written about it, and some "young adult" novels may lack the depth needed for detailed analysis.

Examples from the guide

Topic and research questionWhy it works
Two American presidents, two acceptance speeches—Comparing and contrasting rhetorical techniques
To what extent do the inaugural addresses of President Obama and President Trump reflect their different uses of Aristotle’s three rhetorical techniques?		A language use essay. Research covers ethos, pathos and logos and the purpose of each speech. The analysis compares three examples of each technique in extracts from both speeches, the intended effects, and how the contrasts reflect the speakers' different personalities and agendas. (p. 50)
The personification of Death as a narrator in The Book Thief by Marcus Zusak
How does the personification of Death as a narrator provide an empathetic or an objective perspective for the reader in The Book Thief by Marcus Zusak?		A literary analysis essay. You examine how Zusak develops the personification of Death through narrative analysis, discourse analysis and graphical layout, then judge how far those methods gain the reader's empathy. Literary theory and literary articles support the close reading of the novel. (p. 50)
The industry of American values—Advertising techniques in the USA
To what extent does Coca-Cola promote American values in its ads to appeal particularly to American society?		A cultural study. You define three American values (optimism, individuality and equality) and analyse six Super Bowl advertisements as cultural artefacts to show how Coca-Cola links its product to those values. Sources cover advertising techniques, the company's ideology and social attitudes in the USA, and images of the ads are included and referenced. (p. 50)
Political rap and racism in the USA
To what extent can the song “I’m Not Racist” by Joyner Lucas tell us about currently experienced racism in the USA?		A cultural study built on detailed analysis of the song's lyrics. After brief background on political rap and on the singer, you trace cultural topics such as stereotypes and police brutality in the lyrics, then compare the lyrics with news reports, polls and statistics to judge whether the song reflects reality. (p. 50)

How the essay is usually built (guide p. 50–51)

  • Your introduction must include a statement explaining how your topic fits the criteria. The guide suggests explaining how answering your research question develops your understanding of the target language and its culture, and why your topic is worth investigating.
  • Supporting images are generally not appropriate in the body of your essay unless you are analysing a specific advertisement or artwork. Any image you use must be properly referenced.
  • If poetry or song lyrics are your focus, place all significant extracts in context in the body of your essay. You can put the full text in an appendix, but the examiner is not required to read appendices.
  • Include a translation for any quotation or title that is not in the target language.

Common problems to avoid (guide p. 48–49)

  • Discussing the cinematographic qualities of a film. Those qualities are not language-based; the guide suggests they belong in an essay for film or literature and performance.
  • Choosing a language use topic that does not investigate the target language itself. The guide's example of an inappropriate topic is how second-language students learn English.
  • Relying on student-generated questionnaires for a cultural topic. The guide says questionnaires tend to be subjective and lack useful, reliable evidence.
  • Writing in a tone or register that is too informal for academic writing.

Rules for Language B EEs (guide p. 48–49)

  • You may not write a language B essay in a language you are studying as language A in the Diploma Programme.
  • Every text, whether the focus of your essay or a comparative example, must have been originally written or spoken in the target language. Texts translated into the target language are not acceptable as sources.
  • A film screenplay (script and directions) is acceptable for literary analysis only if you analyse the screenplay as literature.
  • A cultural topic must focus on a sociocultural issue specific to the target language, investigated either through its influence on the form or use of the language, or through customs or artefacts specific to the country where the language is spoken.
