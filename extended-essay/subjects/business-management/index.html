What makes a Business Management EE different (guide p. 55–57)

Business management is in the individuals and societies group, so your essay explores a question about human experience and behaviour. The guide's business management examples each focus on one real organisation. One essay looks at how McDonald's trains its managers. The other looks at McDonald's relationship with its charity, Ronald McDonald House Charities. The guide's sport example also points to a case study: a brand sponsoring a football club.

Your content alone won't make your essay a business management essay. Business management concepts, methods and theories give you insight and help you build a well-constructed argument. The same broad interest, such as sport, could become a business management essay or a global politics essay. Your approach decides which.

The aims for the whole individuals and societies group apply to you too. Your essay lets you challenge assumptions about the social world and build your knowledge by reading the subject literature. You'll also select theories or concepts and apply suitable methods to build an argument.

Primary and secondary sources (guide p. 56–57)

Primary

Primary research is data you collect yourself, such as interviews and surveys. In the guide's Hamburger University example, the student interviews trainers and surveys graduates. Not every business management essay needs primary research: the guide's second example uses secondary data only.

Secondary

Every extended essay must include secondary research (a literature review). In the guide's first example, secondary sources describe and evaluate McDonald's training courses. In the second example, recent academic articles about Ronald McDonald House Charities supply the evidence.

How researchers in Business Management answer questions (guide p. 55–57)

  • Case study of one organisation: the guide's sport example analyses a case study of a brand sponsoring a football club.
  • Describe and evaluate one practice of an organisation using secondary sources, supported by findings from your own primary research.
  • Primary research: interview people who carry out the practice and survey people who experienced the practice, as in the Hamburger University example.
  • Secondary data only: weigh up the advantages and disadvantages of a business relationship, as in the Ronald McDonald House Charities example.
  • Other research strategies for all individuals and societies subjects: comparisons, case studies, discourse analysis, critical analysis of a theory or concept, and in-depth analysis of a primary source.

Choosing a topic (guide p. 55–56)

  • Your topic can come from your own life, from a current debate, from a topic in one of your favourite subjects, or from a topic related to a favourite subject but not covered in it.
  • Big real-world issues, such as the future of global capitalism, can work. Turn the issue into a focused question and explore it from a business management point of view.
  • Your topic must be considered in terms of one individuals and societies subject. A broad interest such as sport could fit several subjects. The guide's business management version analyses a brand sponsoring a football club.
  • Your approach decides your subject. The concepts, methods and theories of business management are what ground your topic in business management.

How the essay is usually built (guide p. 56, 62–63)

  • Make sure your research question and research methods clearly fit business management. The guide suggests reading the business management subject guide to check that you understand the subject and its methods.
  • Keep your argument within the logic of business management: use the subject's terms and follow its usual style and structure.
  • In the Hamburger University example, secondary sources carry the description and evaluation, and primary research findings support the evaluation.
  • Label diagrams, images and graphs clearly, acknowledge any made by others, and refer to each one directly in the body of your essay.
  • Use headings to organise your essay where they help.

Common problems to avoid (guide p. 56, 62–63)

  • Relying on content alone. Without business management concepts, methods and theories, your essay won't be grounded in business management.
  • A research question or research methods that don't clearly fit business management.
  • Writing without business management terms. The guide says that using the subject's terms shows you understand the subject.
  • A one-sided discussion. A balanced discussion looks at different perspectives on your topic, and your evaluation should weigh up the strengths and limitations of your essay.
  • Using sources or methods without checking that they suit business management. Ask your supervisor and read the business management subject guide.
  • Diagrams or graphs that are unlabelled, unacknowledged, or never referred to in the body of your essay.

Rules for Business Management EEs (guide p. 55–56)

  • Your essay must include secondary research (a literature review).
  • Your topic must be considered in terms of business management, even if the same topic could be approached through another individuals and societies subject.
