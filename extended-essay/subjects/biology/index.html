What makes a Biology EE different (guide p. 63–64, 67)

A biology EE lets you investigate a focused research question by collecting primary data, secondary data, or both. You gather your evidence using scientific methodology, and your findings must lead to a valid conclusion based on that evidence. The guide names three features that separate a science essay from pseudoscience: developing hypotheses, producing replicable data and using peer-reviewed publications.

A good biology EE uses accepted biological methodology, shows that you understand the underlying concepts and uses correct terminology. If your essay is based on primary data, your experiment must use controlled, independent variables and a standardised methodology so that valid conclusions can be drawn. The guide gives one example of a good biology EE: an essay exploring the anti-proliferative effect of anthocyanin.

You do not need a university lab. The guide states that EEs based on simple experimental data are just as valid as research done in university labs or hospitals. Your conclusions must be supported by your data, but your hypothesis does not have to be confirmed: the guide treats "negative" results as just as valid as "positive" results.

Primary and secondary sources (guide p. 64)

Primary

Primary data in biology comes from your own experiment, which must use controlled, independent variables and a standardised methodology.

Secondary

Secondary data comes from published scientific articles or databases. The studies you use must follow similar standardised approaches and controls, and preferably similar methodologies, so that you can compare their data.

At ASD: science EEs are expected to be built on primary (experimental) data. The IB allows secondary-data science essays, but workshop guidance is that they rarely reach the top bands.

How researchers in Biology answer questions (guide p. 64, 67–68)

  • Experimental research: you design an experiment with a controlled, independent variable and a standardised methodology, then measure the dependent variable under standardised conditions.
  • A pilot experiment: the guide recommends one when your independent variable may need fine-tuning, for example nitrate concentrations that could kill the plants you are growing.
  • Research based on secondary data: you select studies or databases using predetermined criteria, state those criteria explicitly in your essay, and carry out the analysis yourself.
  • Analysis that follows standard processes: qualitative and quantitative approaches, statistical methods where appropriate, and possibly mathematical transformation of your data.

Choosing a topic (guide p. 64)

  • Be realistic about the facilities available to you at school.
  • Keep a strong focus on biology. Your topic must belong clearly to the subject you submit your EE in.
  • Avoid topics that are beyond the scope and timeline of an EE, or that need lengthy research with sophisticated equipment not usually found in school labs.
  • Choose a topic that interests you. Preferably, you already know some of the theory and understand the concepts. Conceptual understanding matters even more if your EE is based on secondary data.
  • Do not overcomplicate your topic. Keep your investigation manageable within the time you have.

KNO3 concentrations and growth rate
What is the effect of increasing KNO3 concentrations (0.1 mM, 0.2 mM, 0.3 mM, 0.4 mM, 0.5 mM) on the growth rate of onion roots (Allium cepa)?		An experimental essay. The guide warns that you may need to fine-tune the independent variable, because some KNO3 concentrations may kill the plants. Parity as a measurable variable in the development of breast cancer
To what extent is parity a measurable variable in the development of breast cancer?		A secondary-data essay. Studies from different countries on breast cancer rates and risk factors are found through PubMed, DOAJ, JSTOR, Google Scholar and Science Direct, then selected using predetermined criteria that the essay explains. You analyse the data so that valid comparisons can be made, and you make clear how you interpreted and manipulated the data. (p. 64)

How the essay is usually built (guide p. 67–68)

  • Begin with background research on the theories and concepts behind your topic, linked clearly to your research question.
  • Define and justify the variables in your research question, and use SI units throughout.
  • Describe your methodology in enough detail that someone else could replicate your investigation.
  • Present your results in a standardised format with clear headings, units and numbering. Science essays often contain more data than other subjects, so label every table, diagram and graph, process all your data, and include example calculations with uncertainties on your measurements.
  • Finish with a conclusion that synthesises your results rather than repeating them, and an evaluation of strengths and weaknesses that covers your sources and suggests improvements.

Common problems to avoid (guide p. 64, 67–68)

  • Including material that does not serve your research question. The guide's example: if your topic is the kinetics of photosynthesis, a discussion of the morphology of plant leaves is not relevant.
  • Reporting results without the theory behind them. The guide's example: an essay on the turnover rate of an enzyme must discuss enzyme kinetics.
  • Borrowing someone else's analysis. With secondary data you may copy graphs or tables from your sources, but you must perform the statistical analysis yourself.
  • Repeating your results in the conclusion. The guide asks for a supported, well-explained synthesis of your results instead.
  • Leaving gaps unexplained. If your research question is not fully answered, state the unresolved issues, account for them and suggest improvements.
  • Underestimating published literature. Scientific papers sometimes assume knowledge above the level expected of a DP student, so make sure you understand the concepts before you rely on a paper.

Rules for Biology EEs (guide p. 64, 67–68)

  • Do not choose a topic where collecting enough data to answer your research question would be unethical or impractical.
  • Use the International System of Units (SI).
  • If you carry out research at a university or another external organisation, the methods must be your own, not just methods the organisation has already established.
  • If your essay uses secondary data, the analysis of that data must be your own.
