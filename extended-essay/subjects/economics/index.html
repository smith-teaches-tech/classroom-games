What makes a Economics EE different (guide p. 55–56, 58, 63)

Economics sits in the guide's individuals and societies group, so your essay explores a question connected to human experience and behaviour. The guide's two economics examples share one pattern: an economic theory or idea is set against evidence. One essay tests the neoclassical argument about minimum wages against current research, and the other evaluates how far mobile banking has contributed to economic development in one town or village.

The guide says that content alone does not make an essay an economics essay. The concepts, methods and theories of economics are what give you insight and let you build a well-constructed argument. The guide's own example of economics terminology is “supply and demand”: using terms like “supply and demand” in a meaningful, explanatory way shows that you understand the subject.

The guide says individuals and societies subjects can tackle big questions about our shared present, past and future, and names the future of global capitalism as one such real-world issue. An economics essay turns a big issue into a well-focused question explored from the perspective of economics.

Primary and secondary sources (guide p. 56, 58)

Primary

Primary research means data you collect yourself, such as surveys, interviews or local prices. In the guide's mobile banking example, primary research in one town or village is used to judge how far economic development has happened there. Not every economics essay needs it: the guide's minimum wage example uses published research only.

Secondary

Every extended essay must include secondary research (a literature review). The guide's minimum wage example researches the neoclassical argument and the studies behind the counterarguments, and the mobile banking example includes data that other researchers obtained through randomised control trials.

How researchers in Economics answer questions (guide p. 56, 58)

  • Test an economic theory against current research: present the theory, then research the counterarguments and evaluate them by the nature of their evidence and the studies carried out.
  • Use recent evidence from news articles or data banks to assess a link between two variables, such as minimum wages and unemployment, in different places.
  • Combine primary research in one place with data that other researchers obtained through randomised control trials, then evaluate how far an outcome such as economic development has occurred.
  • The guide lists further research strategies for all individuals and societies subjects: comparisons, case studies, discourse analysis, critical analysis of a theory or concept, and in-depth analysis of a primary source.

Choosing a topic (guide p. 55–56, 58)

  • Your topic can come from your own life experience, from an issue of contemporary debate, from a topic in one of your favourite subjects, or from a topic related to but not covered by a favourite subject.
  • Big real-world issues, such as the future of global capitalism or the environmental and climatic crisis, can work if you pose the issue as a well-focused question explored from the perspective of economics.
  • Your topic must be considered in terms of one individuals and societies subject, even though many topics could be approached from more than one subject in the group.
  • Choose your angle for the approach, not the content. The concepts, methods and theories of economics are what ground your topic in economics.
  • The guide's economics examples narrow their focus with a named theory (the neoclassical argument about minimum wages) or a named place and start date (town or village “X” since 2010).

Examples from the guide

Topic and research questionWhy it works
Minimum wage theory
Does current economic research into minimum wage invalidate the neoclassical argument that increasing the minimum wage will lead to increased levels of unemployment?		Neoclassical economics argues that raising the minimum wage raises unemployment, while newer research argues that the neoclassical argument rests on flawed assumptions. The essay presents the neoclassical argument, then evaluates the counterarguments by the nature of their evidence and studies. Recent evidence from news articles or data banks can test the link between minimum wages and unemployment in different places, and a conclusion at each point of analysis builds towards a judgement on how valid the neoclassical model is. (p. 58)
The role of mobile banking in economic development
How has increased access to mobile banking contributed to economic development in town (or village) “X” since 2010?		The essay researches how mobile banking can contribute to economic development in theory, including data that other researchers obtained through randomised control trials. The essay focuses on aspects of development such as higher incomes, health and education outcomes, the empowerment of women and civic participation. Primary research then leads to an evaluation of how far economic development has occurred in the area investigated. (p. 58)

How the essay is usually built (guide p. 56, 58, 62–63)

  • Make your research question and your research methods align clearly with economics. The guide suggests checking the economics subject guide to confirm you understand the nature of the subject and its methodology.
  • Build your argument in stages. In the guide's minimum wage example, each independent point of analysis ends with a conclusion, and those conclusions build towards the overall judgement.
  • Keep your line of argument within the logic of economics: use terminology such as “supply and demand” and follow the subject's style and structural conventions.
  • Clearly label diagrams and graphs, acknowledge any that are the work of others, and refer to each one actively in the body of your essay.
  • Use headings to organise your essay where appropriate.

Common problems to avoid (guide p. 56, 58, 62–63)

  • Relying on content alone. Without economics concepts, methods and theories, your essay will not be grounded in economics.
  • A research question or research methods that do not clearly align with economics.
  • Reporting studies without evaluating them. In the guide's minimum wage example, each counterargument is judged by the nature of its evidence and the studies carried out.
  • A one-sided discussion. The guide says a balanced discussion considers different perspectives on your topic, and your evaluation should weigh up the strengths and limitations of your essay.
  • Using economics terms as labels only. The guide asks you to use key concepts in a meaningful and explanatory way.
  • Diagrams or graphs that are unlabelled, unacknowledged, or never referred to in the body of your essay.

Rules for Economics EEs (guide p. 55–56)

  • Your essay must include secondary research (a literature review).
  • Your topic must be considered in terms of economics, even if the same topic could be approached from another individuals and societies subject.
