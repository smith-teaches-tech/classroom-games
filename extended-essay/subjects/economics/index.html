What makes a Economics EE different (guide p. 55–56, 58, 63)

Economics is in the individuals and societies group, so your essay explores a question about human experience and behaviour. Both of the guide's economics examples set an economic theory or idea against evidence. One essay tests the neoclassical argument about minimum wages against current research. The other judges how far mobile banking has helped economic development in one town or village.

Your content alone won't make your essay an economics essay. Economics concepts, methods and theories give you insight and help you build a well-constructed argument. Use economics terms such as “supply and demand” to explain your analysis, and don't just drop them in as labels. Using terms that way shows you understand the subject.

Individuals and societies subjects can tackle big questions about our shared present, past and future. The guide names the future of global capitalism as one such issue. In an economics essay, you turn a big issue into a focused question and explore it from an economics point of view.

Primary and secondary sources (guide p. 56, 58)

Primary

Primary research is data you collect yourself, such as surveys, interviews or local prices. In the guide's mobile banking example, primary research in one town or village shows how far economic development has happened there. Not every economics essay needs primary research: the guide's minimum wage example uses published research only.

Secondary

Every extended essay must include secondary research (a literature review). The minimum wage example researches the neoclassical argument and the studies behind the counterarguments. The mobile banking example includes data that other researchers obtained through randomised control trials.

How researchers in Economics answer questions (guide p. 56, 58)

  • Test an economic theory against current research: present the theory, then research the counterarguments. Judge each counterargument by the nature of its evidence and the studies carried out.
  • Use recent evidence from news articles or data banks to test a link between two variables, such as minimum wages and unemployment, in different places.
  • Combine primary research in one place with data that other researchers obtained through randomised control trials. Then judge how far an outcome, such as economic development, has happened.
  • Other research strategies for all individuals and societies subjects: comparisons, case studies, discourse analysis, critical analysis of a theory or concept, and in-depth analysis of a primary source.

Choosing a topic (guide p. 55–56, 58)

  • Your topic can come from your own life, from a current debate, from a topic in one of your favourite subjects, or from a topic related to a favourite subject but not covered in it.
  • Big real-world issues, such as the future of global capitalism or the environmental and climate crisis, can work. Turn the issue into a focused question and explore it from an economics point of view.
  • Your topic must be considered in terms of one individuals and societies subject, even though many topics could fit more than one subject in the group.
  • Your approach decides your subject. The concepts, methods and theories of economics are what ground your topic in economics.
  • The guide's economics examples narrow their focus with a named theory (the neoclassical argument about minimum wages) or a named place and start date (town or village “X” since 2010).

Examples from the guide

Topic and research questionWhy it works
Minimum wage theory
Does current economic research into minimum wage invalidate the neoclassical argument that increasing the minimum wage will lead to increased levels of unemployment?		Neoclassical economics argues that raising the minimum wage raises unemployment. Newer research argues that the neoclassical argument rests on flawed assumptions. The essay presents the neoclassical argument, then judges each counterargument by the nature of its evidence and studies. Recent evidence from news articles or data banks can test the link between minimum wages and unemployment in different places. Each point of analysis ends with a conclusion, and those conclusions build towards a judgement on how valid the neoclassical model is. (p. 58)
The role of mobile banking in economic development
How has increased access to mobile banking contributed to economic development in town (or village) “X” since 2010?		The essay first researches how mobile banking can help economic development in theory, including data that other researchers obtained through randomised control trials. The essay looks at areas of development such as higher incomes, health and education outcomes, the empowerment of women and civic participation. Primary research then leads to a judgement of how far economic development has happened in the area studied. (p. 58)

How the essay is usually built (guide p. 56, 58, 62–63)

  • Make sure your research question and research methods clearly fit economics. The guide suggests reading the economics subject guide to check that you understand the subject and its methods.
  • Build your argument in stages. In the minimum wage example, each separate point of analysis ends with a conclusion, and those conclusions build towards the overall judgement.
  • Keep your argument within the logic of economics: use terms such as “supply and demand” and follow the subject's usual style and structure.
  • Label diagrams and graphs clearly, acknowledge any made by others, and refer to each one directly in the body of your essay.
  • Use headings to organise your essay where they help.

Common problems to avoid (guide p. 56, 58, 62–63)

  • Relying on content alone. Without economics concepts, methods and theories, your essay won't be grounded in economics.
  • A research question or research methods that don't clearly fit economics.
  • Reporting studies without evaluating them. In the minimum wage example, each counterargument is judged by the nature of its evidence and the studies carried out.
  • A one-sided discussion. A balanced discussion looks at different perspectives on your topic, and your evaluation should weigh up the strengths and limitations of your essay.
  • Using economics terms as labels only. The guide asks you to use key concepts in a meaningful way that explains something.
  • Diagrams or graphs that are unlabelled, unacknowledged, or never referred to in the body of your essay.

Rules for Economics EEs (guide p. 55–56)

  • Your essay must include secondary research (a literature review).
  • Your topic must be considered in terms of economics, even if the same topic could be approached through another individuals and societies subject.
