What makes a Physics EE different (guide p. 63–64, 66–67)

In a physics EE, you investigate a focused research question. You can use primary data (from your own experiment), secondary data (from published work), or both. You collect your evidence with scientific methods, and your conclusion must follow from that evidence. The guide lists three features that separate a science essay from pseudoscience: you develop hypotheses, you produce data that someone else could replicate, and you use peer-reviewed publications.

A good physics EE uses accepted physics methods. It shows that you understand the concepts behind your topic, and it uses the correct terms. If your essay uses primary data, your experiment must have controlled, independent variables and a standardised method, so that your conclusions are valid. The guide's physics examples show both routes: measuring how water flows out of an opening, and estimating the mass of Saturn from published data.

You don't need a university lab. The guide says an EE based on simple experiments is just as valid as research done in a university lab or a hospital. Your data must support your conclusions, but your hypothesis doesn't have to be proved right. The guide counts "negative" results as just as valid as "positive" results.

Primary and secondary sources (guide p. 64)

Primary

In physics, primary data comes from your own experiment. Your experiment must have controlled, independent variables and a standardised method.

Secondary

Secondary data comes from published scientific articles or databases. The sources you use must have similar standardised approaches and controls (and ideally similar methods), so that you can compare their data.

At ASD: your science EE should use primary (experimental) data. The IB allows science essays based on secondary data, but EE workshop guidance says secondary-data essays rarely reach the top mark bands.

How researchers in Physics answer questions (guide p. 64, 66–68)

  • Experimental research: you build a set-up that measures your dependent variable under standardised conditions while you control the other variables. In the guide's water-flow example, the set-up must measure two speeds the whole time the water is flowing.
  • Research with secondary data: you work out what information your research question needs and how to get that information. You state your selection criteria clearly in your essay, and you do the analysis yourself.
  • Applying fundamental principles to the problem: the guide's water-flow example looks at the whole event using the principles of fluid dynamics.
  • Standard ways of analysing data: qualitative and quantitative approaches, statistics where they fit, and possibly a mathematical transformation of your data.

Choosing a topic (guide p. 64)

  • Be realistic about the facilities you have at school.
  • Keep a strong focus on physics. Your topic must clearly belong to the subject you submit your EE in.
  • Avoid topics that are too big for the scope and timeline of an EE, or that need long research with advanced equipment most school labs don't have.
  • Choose a topic that interests you. Ideally, you already know some of the theory and understand the concepts. Understanding the concepts matters even more if your EE uses secondary data.
  • Don't make your topic too complicated. Keep your investigation small enough to finish in the time you have.

How the essay is usually built (guide p. 67–68)

  • Start with background research on the theories and concepts behind your topic. Link your background research clearly to your research question.
  • Define your variables and explain why you chose them. Use SI units throughout.
  • Describe your method in enough detail that someone else could repeat your investigation.
  • Present your results in a standard format with clear headings, units and numbering. Science essays often have more data than essays in other subjects. Label every table, diagram and graph, process all your data, and include example calculations with the uncertainties on your measurements.
  • End with a conclusion that brings your results together without repeating them. Then evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of your investigation, including your sources, and suggest improvements.

Common problems to avoid (guide p. 64, 67–68)

  • Including material that doesn't help answer your research question. The guide expects every part of a science essay to link to your line of argument.
  • Reporting results without the theory behind them. You must explore and discuss the processes and theories behind your results. The guide says the theory matters even more in essays based on secondary data.
  • Using someone else's analysis. With secondary data, you may copy graphs or tables from your sources, but you must do the statistical analysis yourself.
  • Repeating your results in the conclusion. The guide asks you to bring your results together in a well-explained conclusion that your evidence supports.
  • Leaving gaps unexplained. If your research question isn't fully answered, say which issues are still open, explain why, and suggest improvements.
  • Underestimating how hard published papers can be. Scientific papers sometimes expect more knowledge than a DP student has, so make sure you understand the concepts before you rely on a paper.

Rules for Physics EEs (guide p. 64, 67–68)

  • Don't choose a topic where collecting enough data to answer your research question would be unethical or impractical.
  • Use the International System of Units (SI).
  • If you do research at a university or another outside organisation, the methods must be your own. You can't simply use methods the organisation already has in place.
  • If your essay uses secondary data, you must do the analysis of that data yourself.
