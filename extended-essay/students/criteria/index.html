Understand the criteria

Examiners mark your EE against five criteria. If you know what each criterion asks for, you know what to aim for, from your first idea to your final draft.

How your EE is marked

  • An IB examiner marks your essay out of 30. Your supervisor doesn't mark it. Criteria A, B, C and D apply to the whole essay. Criterion E applies only to your reflective statement on the RPF (your reflection form) (guide p. 114).
  • Examiners mark positively: they look for what you've done and give you credit for it. They choose the level that fits your work best. The top level doesn't mean a perfect essay (guide p. 111).
  • Your total mark becomes a grade from A to E. You need a D or higher to be awarded the diploma (guide p. 14). Your supervisor sends the IB a predicted grade, not a mark.

What each criterion asks of you

AFramework for the essay6 marks

Strands: research question · research methods · structure

  • Research question: a real, specific question that comes from a topic in your subject. Your question should invite analysis rather than description. Avoid two questions joined together, and avoid questions with an obvious answer. Wording such as "to what extent", "how significant" or "how successful" helps.
  • Research methods: explain why your methods suit your question, and how your methods connect to the way your subject does research. If you ran an experiment or did fieldwork, describe the experiment or fieldwork in enough detail that someone could repeat it.
  • Structure: follow the usual structure for essays in your subject. Label every figure and table, and refer to each one in your text.

Guide pp. 37–39 · Work on it in step 6 and step 10, and check your subject guide for structure.

BKnowledge and understanding6 marks

Strands: knowledge · understanding of terminology · understanding of concepts

  • Knowledge: show that you've used your research findings (from the literature, secondary sources and your own data) to explore the topic in an organised way. Show your knowledge in your own writing. Avoid long quotations.
  • Terminology: use the vocabulary of your subject accurately and consistently.
  • Concepts: use the main ideas of your subject correctly to frame your analysis.

Guide p. 39 · You build your knowledge through your reading in steps 3 to 9.

CAnalysis and line of argument6 marks

Strands: analysis · line of argument

  • Analysis: break your topic into its parts, using your research question as a guide. Support every point with evidence from your research. An essay that mostly describes or tells a story won't show enough analysis.
  • Line of argument: a reasoned thread that links your research question, your findings and your conclusion. The guide says fitting the parts together is the writer's job. Signpost where each part starts and ends.

Guide pp. 39–40.

DDiscussion and evaluation8 marks

Strands: discussion · evaluation

  • Discussion: a balanced review of what your findings mean. Include a range of factors or perspectives, and support your conclusions with evidence.
  • Evaluation: weigh the strengths and limitations of your sources and your research, and notice the positions the writers take. The guide says evaluation belongs throughout the essay, not in a separate section at the end.

Guide p. 40 · The evaluation part of your annotations in step 9 is practice for this criterion.

EReflection4 marks

Strands: evaluative · growth · marked on your RPF reflective statement only (500 words maximum)

  • Evaluative: judge what the EE experience did for you as a learner. Give specific examples of skills you've used in other contexts.
  • Growth: show how your thinking and decisions changed, and how you'll carry what you learned into future study, work or life.

Guide pp. 40–41 · Keep your RRS (researcher's reflection space: your own EE notebook) up to date from step 0. Your RRS is your evidence for this criterion.

See the criteria in real essays

Every IB sample essay in the sample library shows the examiner's mark for each criterion. Pick two essays in your subject with different grades. Compare their marks for criterion A and criterion C: those two criteria usually explain most of the difference.
