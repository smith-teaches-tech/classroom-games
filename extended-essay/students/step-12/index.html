Outline your essay

Plan your essay before you write it. An outline lists your sections, the main point of each one, and the evidence each one uses.

Why this step matters

Your outline is your line of argument on one page. If a section doesn't help answer your research question, you'll see it now, not after 4,000 words. (guide pp. 40–41)

What your outline needs

  • Introduction: your topic, the background, your focus and your research question.
  • Body: your sections. Each paragraph makes one main point, backed by evidence.
  • Conclusion: your answer to the research question. No new points.

Every essay also needs a title page, a contents page and a bibliography. Your subject's conventions are in step 10. (guide pp. 39, 87)

In your subject

Biology, Chemistry, Physics: Plan sections for background, method, results, analysis and conclusion. The method must be detailed enough for someone to repeat it. (guide p. 67)
Business Management: Plan your body sections around the business tools and theories you'll apply to your organisation. Label any diagrams and refer to each one in your text.
Global Politics: Start with a review of what has been written on your main political concepts. Then plan a section for each case you analyse.
Psychology: Plan sections that weigh studies supporting your argument against studies that challenge it.
Language A: Plan body sections that each make one claim about your text, with the quotations you'll use. There is no separate literature review section: plan where critics' views support or challenge your reading. (guide p. 44)
Language B: Plan where your introduction explains how your topic fits Language B and the culture of the language. (guide p. 50) Analysing literature? Plan it like Language A: no separate literature review section.
Visual Arts: Plan a section for each artwork or theme. Note which images you need: every image must be labelled and referenced.
Mathematics: Start from the mathematics you know from the syllabus. Subheadings help. Plan where each essential step will be shown and explained. (guide p. 70)
Interdisciplinary: Plan where each subject comes in, and where you show how the two subjects work together to answer your question. (guide p. 33)
