Annotated bibliography: 20 sources

Choose the 20 sources that matter most for your broad question, annotate each one, and find what is missing — the gap your essay can fill.

Why this step matters

An annotated bibliography forces you to understand each source well enough to explain it in your own words and judge it. Laying 20 sources side by side also shows you where the research runs out — and that is where your own question belongs.

The IB's support material is clear about this: your research question "should be sufficiently original that you are making a genuine contribution to knowledge in that field", and gaps in what secondary research can tell you are "often a cue that primary research is needed".

From 40 sources to 20

Go back to PowerNotes and keep the 20 sources that best help with your broad question from step 8. Choose for:

  • Relevance — the source speaks directly to your broad question, not just your broad topic.
  • Quality — mostly scholarly sources: peer-reviewed articles, academic books, reputable data.
  • Different perspectives — sources that disagree; sources from different places, time periods, schools of thought or methods.
  • Method — at least a few sources that show a method you might use (from step 6).

Move your 20 into a new PowerNotes category called Annotated 20. Keep the other 20 — you may need them later.

What an annotation contains

Each entry starts with the full citation in your chosen style (APA or MLA), followed by a paragraph of roughly 100 to 150 words, in your own words, that covers:

  1. Summary — what the source argues or finds, and how (its method or evidence).
  2. Evaluation — how reliable it is: who wrote it, for whom, with what evidence, and any limitations.
  3. Connection — how it relates to your broad question and to your other sources: does it agree, disagree, or add something new?

The Purdue OWL has examples of annotated bibliographies in both styles: annotated bibliography samples.

Find the gap

Put your 20 sources into a matrix: one row per source, with columns for its position, its method and its main finding. Then read down each column and ask what is missing. A gap might be:

  • a place nobody has studied — including your own city or region. "Local" simply means a specific place; local data can still say something that matters internationally;
  • a group of people, a time period, or a text or artwork that has not been examined in this way;
  • a method that has not been applied to this question;
  • a disagreement between sources that nobody has tested directly;
  • a combination of two things that have only been studied separately.

Your gap does not need to be dramatic. A focused, well-chosen gap that you can actually investigate in 4,000 words is exactly right.
