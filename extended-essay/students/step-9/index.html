Annotated bibliography

Choose the sources that matter most for your broad question, annotate each one, and find what's missing: the gap your essay can fill.

Why this step matters

An annotated bibliography is a list of sources with a short paragraph about each one. Writing one makes you understand each source well enough to explain and judge it in your own words. Putting your best sources side by side also shows you where the research runs out. That's where your own question belongs.

The IB's support material says your research question "should be sufficiently original that you are making a genuine contribution to knowledge in that field". It also says gaps in what secondary research can tell you are "often a cue that primary research is needed".

Your subject from step 1 is highlighted.

Language A, Language B and Visual Arts students: annotate secondary sources: criticism, context, theory, interviews. Your primary texts or artworks are what you analyse in the essay itself.

Go back to PowerNotes. Choose the sources that help most with your broad question from step 8. Look for:

  • Relevance: the source speaks directly to your broad question, not just your broad topic.
  • Quality: mostly scholarly sources: peer-reviewed articles, academic books, reputable data.
  • Different perspectives: sources that disagree, and sources from different places, time periods, schools of thought or methods.
  • Method: at least a few sources that show a method you might use (from step 6).

Move the sources you choose into a new PowerNotes category called Annotated. Keep the rest. You may need them later.

What an annotation contains

Each entry starts with the full citation in your chosen style (APA or MLA). Then write a paragraph of about 100 to 150 words, in your own words, that covers:

  1. Summary: what the source argues or finds, and how (its method or evidence).
  2. Evaluation: how reliable the source is. Who wrote it, for whom, with what evidence, and what are its limitations?
  3. Connection: how the source relates to your broad question and to your other sources. Does it agree, disagree, or add something new?

The Purdue OWL has examples of annotated bibliographies in both styles: annotated bibliography samples.

Find the gap

Put your annotated sources into a matrix (a table): one row per source, with columns for its position, its method and its main finding. Then read down each column and ask what's missing. A gap might be:

  • a place nobody has studied, including your own city or region. "Local" simply means a specific place. Local data can still say something that matters internationally;
  • a group of people who haven't been studied in this way;
  • a time period that hasn't been examined in this way;
  • a text or artwork that hasn't been examined in this way;
  • a method that hasn't been applied to this question;
  • a disagreement between sources that nobody has tested directly;
  • a combination of two things that have only been studied separately.

Your gap doesn't need to be dramatic. A focused, well-chosen gap that you can actually investigate in 4,000 words is exactly right.
