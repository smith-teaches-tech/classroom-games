What the extended essay is, how this site works, and the one document you need before you begin.

What the extended essay is

The extended essay (EE) is an independent research project on a question you choose. You investigate your question, then write up what you find as a formal academic essay.

4,000 words maximum

Examiners stop reading at 4,000 words. Anything after that point isn't marked.

One subject or two

A subject-focused essay uses one DP (Diploma Programme) subject. An interdisciplinary essay combines two DP subjects through one of five frameworks.

Three reflection sessions

You meet your supervisor three times: a first session, an interim (middle) session, and a final session called the viva voce.

A 500-word reflective statement

You write it after the viva voce, on the Reflection and Progress Form (RPF). It's marked under Criterion E.

IB examiners mark the essay out of 30 across five criteria (A Framework for the essay, B Knowledge and understanding, C Analysis and line of argument, D Discussion and evaluation, E Reflection). The mark becomes a grade from A to E. Your EE grade and your TOK grade together can earn up to three points towards your diploma. A grade E in the EE is a failing condition: you won't be awarded the diploma.

How the core fits together

CAS

Shows you what you care about. What you care about is often the best place to find an EE topic.

TOK

Teaches you to ask questions: what counts as evidence in a subject, and which methods the subject trusts.

Your subjects

Give you the knowledge, concepts and terms.

The EE

Is where you put all three together, on your own question.

How this site works

There are 19 steps in four stages: Explore, Narrow, Research and write, and After submission. Do them in order. Each step page has:

  • A short guide: why the step matters and what to do.
  • A thinking tool: questions that help you brainstorm. Nothing is submitted or graded. You keep your answers in your RRS (see below).
  • Before you move on: a short checklist at the end of the thinking tool.

Your extended essay is your own independent work. The thinking tools only ask questions. They never write anything for you.

Set up your RRS before anything else

The IB strongly recommends that every EE student keeps a researcher's reflection space (RRS). Your RRS is your own EE notebook. In it you record what you read, what you think, the questions that come up and the decisions you make. You'll use your RRS to prepare for each reflection session. It's also the best source for your reflective statement at the end.

Your teacher will tell you which kind of RRS to keep:

A paper notebook

Write your answers to each thinking tool by hand in your EE notebook. Then take a photo of the pages and add the photo to your RRS in Folio.

A Google Doc

Make a Google Doc called EE RRS: your name. Use the Copy for Google Docs button to copy each thinking tool into your doc.

  1. Start each entry with today's date and the name of the step.
  2. Answer the thinking tool below in your RRS. You can type your answers on this page first, or write straight into your notebook.
  3. Do the same at every step. Add your own notes in between: reactions to sources, new questions, and things you changed your mind about.

Your dated entries show how your ideas grew over time. They're also evidence that your essay is your own work.

Source: IB Extended essay guide (first assessment 2027), "Reflection and the extended essay" and "Assessment".
