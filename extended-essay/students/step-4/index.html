Current sources: what is being said now

Find out what researchers, journalists and organisations are saying about your topic right now — and where they disagree.

Why this step matters

Your essay joins a conversation that is already happening. Before you can add anything, you need to know who is speaking now, what they agree on, and where they disagree.

"Current" usually means roughly the last five years — but this depends on the subject. A fast-moving science topic may need sources from the last two years; a literary topic may still be shaped by criticism from twenty years ago.

Where to look

  • Google Scholar: search your key terms from step 3, then use Since in the left-hand menu to limit results to recent years. Click Cited by under a useful article to find newer work that builds on it.
  • School library databases: ask the librarian which databases ASD students can use, and how to log in from home.
  • Reports and data: recent reports from organisations such as the World Bank, OECD, United Nations agencies and government departments, and their data portals.
  • Quality journalism: recent long-form reporting and analysis, which often names the researchers involved — then look up their work.

Reading a research article quickly

  1. Read the abstract. Does it actually relate to your topic?
  2. Read the introduction's last paragraph — it usually states the aim — and then the conclusion.
  3. Only then read the method and results, if the article is worth it.
  4. Look at the reference list for sources you have not yet found.

What to do

  1. Find about 10 current sources. Save each in PowerNotes with its citation, in a category called Current.
  2. For each, note the position it takes, or the finding it reports.
  3. Map the debate: who agrees with whom, and where do they disagree?

Source: IB Extended essay guide (first assessment 2027), "Researching the extended essay" (preliminary investigation: "What has already been written about this topic? Is there a range of views or perspectives?").
