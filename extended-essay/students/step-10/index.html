Your research question and the parts of a paper

Turn your broad question into a research question. Learn how essays in your subject are built, and get ready for your first reflection session.

Why this step matters

Your research question is the centre of Criterion A, and every other criterion depends on your research question too. The IB guide says your research question should keep you "within the scope of the 4,000 words" and help you "avoid producing an unfocused, mostly descriptive piece of writing".

What the IB asks of a research question

Your research question must be written as a question. A hypothesis (a prediction) or a statement of what you plan to do won't count. Your research question should:

  • be clearly stated and focused;
  • be open to change if your research takes you in a different direction, or if there aren't enough resources;
  • lead you to examine the topic critically, using the knowledge, concepts, theories, perspectives or methods of your subject(s);
  • encourage analysis, evaluation and synthesis (bringing ideas together), rather than description, unsupported generalizations and value judgements.

Subject reminders: in the sciences, the variables in your research question must be defined, justified and measurable. In an interdisciplinary essay, your research question must reflect both subjects and the reason for combining them.

What examiners said about real questions

These research questions come from the sample essays, word for word, with the examiner's comment on each.

Design Technology
Sample A (criterion A: 2/6)Can compostable materials be a sustainable solution to how the fields of mass manufactured EPS food packaging contributed to the world’s environmental causes?
Examiner: “clear but only partially focused in relation to the scope of the essay”
Sample C (criterion A: 5/6)With reference to structural integrity and environmental consideration, what would be the most suitable cement alternatives for the construction of stabilized rammed earth structures?
Examiner: “fully relevant to the topic, easy to understand and thoroughly focused within a clearly defined scope”
Physics and ESS
Physics Sample D (criterion A: 2/6)How do different types of Stirling engines contribute to energy generated and efficiency?
Examiner: “relevant to the topic but only partially focused”; overall, “A descriptive work without a clear focus”
ESS Sample C (criterion A: 6/6)How far is the enhanced infiltration of UVA radiation (315-400nm) due to ozone depletion less detrimental than UVB (280-315nm) for the germination and growth of Gerbera (Gerbera L.) and Chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla) flowering plants?
Examiner: “relevant and focused”; the method “includes justifications for the selection of variables”

Two more examiner comments worth knowing:

  • Chemistry Sample A (criterion A: 5/6): the question was "clear and focused but stated as a yes/no answer type question". A yes/no question limits your argument.
  • IB Economics example 1 (criterion A: 3/6): a "to what extent" question didn't work because "it is not possible to measure the extent" of what it asked about. Only use "to what extent" when the extent can actually be judged.

Once you have your research question, use exactly the same wording on your title page, in your introduction and in your conclusion.

The parts of a paper

Every extended essay must include these parts:

  1. Title page
  2. Contents page
  3. Introduction
  4. Body of the essay
  5. Conclusion
  6. Reference list or bibliography

Each subject also has its own structural conventions (the usual way essays in that subject are organised):

SubjectStructural conventions
Language AAn introduction that explains the topic and focus, and names the texts. Body paragraphs that build an argument, supported by evidence from primary and secondary sources. A conclusion that answers the question using your close readings. Sections and headings are allowed, but they must not break the essay into disconnected pieces.
Language BImages usually aren't useful in the body unless you're analysing an advertisement or artwork. If you analyse poetry or song lyrics, quote the important extracts in the body. The full text can go in an appendix, but examiners don't have to read appendices.
Visual ArtsChoose a small number of works so you can analyse them in depth within the word limit. Review the existing literature on your topic to shape your research question. Label and reference any images you include.
Biology, Chemistry, PhysicsA method detailed enough for someone else to repeat it. SI units. Results in a standardized format with labelled headings, units and numbering. Processed data with uncertainties. Conclusions supported by the data. An evaluation linked to the question and method.
Business Management, Economics, Global PoliticsClearly labelled diagrams, maps, images and graphs. Acknowledge any that are someone else's work, and refer to each one in your text. Use headings to organise the essay where they help.
MathematicsState the question clearly and stay focused on it. Write mathematics with an equation editor. Use a new line for each step of algebra, and don't use * for multiplication or ^ for powers.

Anything important to your argument must be in the body of the essay. Examiners don't have to read appendices, footnotes or endnotes.

Your first reflection session

After this step you'll have your first reflection session with your supervisor. It's the first of the three sessions recorded on your RPF (Reflection and Progress Form). The session focuses on your first ideas and how you plan to do your research. Bring your RRS (your EE notebook).
