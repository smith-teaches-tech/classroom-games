Historical sources: how we got here

Trace how thinking on your topic developed: the foundational (early and important) work, the old debates, and what changed people's minds.

Why this step matters

Current research builds on older work. If you know the history of your topic, you can see why today's researchers ask the questions they ask. You can also spot assumptions that nobody has tested for a while.

What "historical" means in your subject

  • Sciences and Mathematics: the classic experiments, theorems and models your topic rests on, and who first proposed them.
  • Economics, Business Management and Global Politics: the theories you will apply (where they came from, and what they were responding to), and earlier events or cases like yours.
  • Language A and Language B: how the text was received when it was published, how critics' readings have changed since, and the context the text was written in.
  • Visual Arts: the movements, influences and earlier artists behind the work you are studying, and how the work has been interpreted over time.

How to find foundational work

  • Notice which older sources are cited again and again in the current sources from step 4. Those are usually the foundational works.
  • On Google Scholar, a very high Cited by number on an older article often means the article shaped the field.
  • Look for review articles or textbook chapters that summarise how thinking on your topic has developed.

What to do

  1. Find about 10 historical sources. Save each in PowerNotes with its citation, in a category called Historical.
  2. Build a short timeline of how ideas about your topic changed.
  3. Note one older idea that current sources now challenge.

Source: IB Extended essay guide (first assessment 2027), "Writing your extended essay" (citation and referencing: "They demonstrate whether you know who the most reputable authors are for the topic").
