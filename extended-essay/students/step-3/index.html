Open sources: get the background

Before you read research papers, learn the basics of your topic: the main terms, people, events and debates.

Why this step matters

Scholarly (academic) articles assume you already know the basics. Open sources are free, general and easy to read. They give you the basics quickly, so the harder reading in steps 4 to 6 makes sense.

Open sources are for orientation: finding your way around your topic. You'll rarely cite open sources much in your final essay. They point you towards the sources you will cite.

Where to look

  • Encyclopedias: Encyclopaedia Britannica, subject encyclopedias, the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy for ideas and theories.
  • Wikipedia: good for an overview. The reference list at the bottom of each Wikipedia article leads you to better sources. Follow those references, but don't cite Wikipedia itself.
  • Reputable news: BBC, Reuters, The Guardian, The Economist, Al Jazeera English, and newspapers from the country your topic is about.
  • Organisations: United Nations agencies, the World Health Organization, the World Bank, government statistics offices, museums, professional bodies.
  • Open-access material: free articles found through Google Scholar, and the free ERIC database for education topics.

Judge every source

The IB guide points out that you'd judge "a post on Reddit, a sensationalist newspaper article, a blog post by an apparent expert and an article in a reputable academic journal" very differently. For each open source, ask:

  • Who wrote it, and what makes them qualified?
  • When was it published? Is it still current?
  • Why was it written: to inform, persuade or sell?
  • What evidence does it give? Can you trace that evidence to its source?

What to do

  1. Read 8 to 12 open sources on your broad topic. Save each one in PowerNotes with its full citation in your chosen style. Put it in a category called Open / background.
  2. Make a glossary (a list of terms and their meanings) of the key terms in your topic, in your own words.
  3. Note the names that keep appearing: people, organisations, places and events.
  4. Write down the questions that start to form as you read.

Source: IB Extended essay guide (first assessment 2027), "Researching the extended essay" (preliminary investigation) and "Writing your extended essay" (citation and referencing).
