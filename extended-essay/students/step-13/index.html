Introduction and literature review

Write the first part of your essay: the introduction, then a review of what has already been written about your topic.

Why this step matters

The introduction tells the examiner what to expect. The literature review shows you know what others have found. In most subjects, it also leads to the gap your essay fills (from step 9). (guide p. 37)

What goes in your introduction

  • Your topic, and the background the reader needs.
  • Your focus: what you will and won't cover.
  • Your research question, worded exactly as on your title page.
  • The sources and methods you'll use, and a hint of your argument. (In a literature essay, your method is literary analysis, so it can be implied.)

Tip from the guide: come back and edit your introduction once the body is finished. (guide pp. 39, 89)

Your literature review

Most subjects: build it from your step 9 annotations. Group your sources by idea or debate, not one paragraph per source. End with the gap: the question your sources haven't answered, which your essay takes on.

Literature essays (Language A, and Language B essays on literature): there is no separate literature review. Bring critics' views in throughout your analysis, where they support or challenge your reading. (guide p. 44)

In your subject

Biology, Chemistry, Physics: Explain the theory and concepts behind your topic. Define your variables and say why you chose them. Use SI units. (guide p. 67)
Business Management: Introduce the organisation or issue, and the business tools and theories you'll apply.
Global Politics: Your literature review covers what has been written on your main political concepts. It comes before your case analysis.
Psychology: Your whole essay is a review of research. In your introduction, define your main idea in a way you can measure (operationalise it).
Language A: Name your texts in the introduction. Critics' views come in throughout your analysis, not in a separate section, and you don't need to show a gap. Your method (literary analysis) can be implied. (guide p. 44)
Language B: Your introduction must explain how your topic fits Language B, and how it builds your understanding of the language and culture. (guide p. 50) Analysing literature? Follow the Language A approach: critics throughout, no gap needed.
Visual Arts: Name your artworks and artists. Use what art historians and critics have written to set up your research question.
Mathematics: Explain your research question and your plan in your own words. A literature review is required. (guide pp. 69–70)
Interdisciplinary: Name your two subjects and your framework. Explain why your question needs both subjects. (guide pp. 33, 88)

What to do

  1. Draft your introduction. Tick each part off in the thinking tool.
  2. Write your literature review from your step 9 annotations.
  3. Hand both in by the date in the EE calendar. Your supervisor talks them through with you at the interim reflection session.
