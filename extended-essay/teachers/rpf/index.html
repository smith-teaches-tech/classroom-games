What the RPF is

The Reflection and Progress Form (RPF) is the two-page record that travels with every extended essay. Page 1 logs three formal reflection sessions between student and supervisor. Page 2 holds the student's reflective statement, which is the only thing marked under Criterion E.

Reflective statement · page 2

Written by the student after the viva voce. No more than 500 words, in the same language as the essay. Assessed under Criterion E.

02

Criterion E: Reflection

4 marksreflective statement only

Does the student evaluate the effect of the extended essay learning experience on them as a learner?

Strand1 mark2 marks3 marks4 marks
Evaluative Reflection on the EE learning experience is descriptive. Reflection is descriptive and includes some specific examples. Reflection is partially evaluative and includes specific examples. Reflection is consistently evaluative and includes specific examples.
Growth Reflection on growth is limited to a description of the process; no evidence of transfer of learning. Reflection sometimes shows evidence of growth and transfer of learning. Reflection often shows evidence of growth and transfer of learning. Reflection consistently shows evidence of growth and transfer of learning.

Wording condensed from the IB Extended essay guide (first assessment 2027). If the work does not reach the standard of the 1-mark descriptors, 0 is awarded.

Evidence the reflection may draw on

  • experiences and insights that could shape future thinking
  • explicit examples of how skills learned could be used in other current contexts and in the future
  • how changes in perspective impacted decision-making

The guide says evidence may be drawn from, but is not limited to, these three.

The ladder in one line each

1RetellsWhat happened, in order.
2Retells with examplesSpecific moments, little judgement.
3Judges in placesSome "why", growth shown often.
4Judges throughoutEvery example evaluated; transfer shown.

04

What separates the scores

Patterns across the six samples, for supervisors preparing students for the viva voce and the reflective statement.

"Why", not just "what"

The Biology statement (4) explains why the scope had to narrow: the in vitro models were infeasible in time and resources. The Business statement (2) reports that the question changed, but rarely says what the change taught the student.

Length is not quality

The Mathematics statement (2) uses 495 of its 500 words; the Interdisciplinary statement (3) uses about 300. Generic sentences ("never give up", "the reward was sweet") fill space without earning marks.

Specific moments carry the marks

An anomaly in the results (Biology), feedback on a first draft (Language A), a single-subject draft that failed (Interdisciplinary). Named moments give the student something to evaluate.

Transfer must be shown

Language A (3) claims the skills transferred to "university classes" and a "future job" but gives no example. The Interdisciplinary statement (3) names a result: improved English grades. Neither sustains it, which is part of why both stop at 3.

Motivation is not reflection

The Psychology statement (1) spends its opening on why the topic mattered personally and names a challenge (narrowing the topic) without saying how it was solved. Its one forward-looking idea comes in the final paragraph.

Use the viva to set it up

The statement is written after the viva voce. Questions such as "What would you do differently, and why?" and "Where have you used this skill since?" rehearse the evaluative moves the rubric rewards.
